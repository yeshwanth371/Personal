Africa (continued)

1. Introduction, Resources and Underdevelopment

  • Africa is the second-largest continent and lies roughly symmetrically about the Equator, so most of it has a tropical climate.
  • Fossil evidence shows that the earliest humans evolved and lived in Africa (see notes in the 10-07-2026 file).
  • Despite this long history of human interaction, Africa's natural resources are still under-harnessed / under-utilised.
  • Main reasons for the socio-economic underdevelopment of most African countries:
    1. Difficult terrain - plateau edges, escarpments and rapids.
    2. Non-navigable rivers - waterfalls and rapids near the coast block inland navigation.
    3. Vast patches of climatically hostile land (desert, dense rainforest, disease-prone areas) that hampered human mobility.
    4. Political boundaries drawn by Europeans that totally neglected geographical and cultural reality.
  • The lack of congruence between political identity and cultural identity led to political disturbance in most African countries. Example: Libya, where the north, east and south have different identities.

[Correction] (1) "2 largest continent" = second-largest (after Asia). Africa extends from about 37°N to 35°S, so it is approximately symmetrical about the Equator, not exactly. (2) Libya's three historic regions are Tripolitania (north-west, around Tripoli), Cyrenaica (east, around Benghazi) and Fezzan (south-west, desert) - so the "north" identity is the western one. They were Ottoman provinces joined by Italy and then as independent Libya (1951); since 2011 the country has been split between rival administrations in the west and east [Verification Required] - check the current political situation before quoting.

[Additional] The partition of Africa among European powers was formalised at the Berlin Conference (1884-85); many borders follow straight lines or colonial rivers rather than ethnic, linguistic or geographic units, splitting some peoples (e.g. the Somali, Tuareg and Maasai) across several states and joining rival groups inside one state. Landlocked states (Mali, Niger, Chad, Uganda, Zambia, Ethiopia, etc.) also depend on neighbours' ports.

3. Region 1 - Northern Dry Region / Sahara Region

3.1 Location and the Sahara

  • The northern dry region is spread across the Tropic of Cancer, which passes through Western Sahara, Mauritania, Mali, Algeria, Niger, Libya and Egypt (Map 1).
  • The region has the world's largest desert - the Sahara.
  • The Sahara is flanked by the Mediterranean coast in the north and the Sahel region in the south.

[Correction] (1) The Sahara is the world's largest hot desert; the largest desert of all is Antarctica (a cold desert). (2) Niger is also crossed by the Tropic of Cancer and was missing from the rough list. (3) Western Sahara is a disputed territory (mostly administered by Morocco; claimed by the Polisario Front) - see the 10-07-2026 notes.

3.2 The Mediterranean Coast of Africa

  • The Mediterranean coast of Africa is spread across 5 countries: Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt.
  • Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia have the Atlas Mountains (young fold mountains) - the region is called the "mat" region in the rough notes.
  • Morocco is rich in phosphate - it has the largest reserves in the world.
  • The Mediterranean coast is characterised by the production of wheat and citrus fruits.

[Correction] "Mat region" is not a standard geographical term; it is probably "Maghreb" (Arabic for "the west"), the north-western African region of Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia (sometimes with Libya and Mauritania) [Verification Required] - the rough-note word is unclear. The Atlas system has several ranges (High, Middle and Anti-Atlas in Morocco; Tell and Saharan Atlas in Algeria; Mt Toubkal, about 4,167 m, is the highest peak). Libya and Egypt have no Atlas - Egypt's coast is the Nile delta and a desert plateau. The Atlas formed in the Alpine orogeny (collision of Africa and Eurasia) - the same system as the Alps (see Europe section 2.2).

[Additional] Phosphate (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026, 2025 estimates): Morocco (with Western Sahara) holds about 50 billion tonnes of the world's 73 billion tonnes of phosphate-rock reserves (about 68%) and produced about 36 million tonnes - the world's second-largest producer after China (110 Mt). The Mediterranean climate (hot dry summers, mild wet winters) suits wheat, olives, citrus, grapes. India link: India imports large amounts of phosphate-based fertilisers (DAP) and raw materials, and Morocco is a major supplier [Verification Required] - check the current import data (Department of Fertilizers / MoPNG-type official statistics) before quoting shares.

3.3 The Sahel

  • The first vegetation found after the Sahara is the Sahel region.
  • The Sahel is a semi-arid region (grasses and shrubs).
  • It is spread across Senegal, Mali, northern Nigeria, Niger, Chad, northern Cameroon, Sudan and Eritrea (Map 1, hatched belt).
  • The region is facing political turmoil and terrorist activity.

[Correction] Spellings: "sherbs" = shrubs; "Semegal" = Senegal; "iretria" = Eritrea; "cameron" = Cameroon. The Sahel is the transition belt between the Sahara (north) and the wetter savanna (south) and stretches from the Atlantic Ocean to the Red Sea. Definitions vary: the UN Integrated Strategy for the Sahel (UNISS) lists ten countries - Senegal, Gambia, Mauritania, Guinea, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Chad, Cameroon and Nigeria, while Mauritania, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad formed the former G5 Sahel core; Sudan and Eritrea are included in geographical definitions (PMF IAS, source). Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger have been run by military governments after coups (Mali 2020 and 2021, Burkina Faso 2022, Niger 2023; Guinea 2021 and Chad 2021 also saw military takeovers) - see PYQ 3. Check the latest security situation before quoting [Verification Required].

[Additional] Causes of Sahel instability: climate stress and desertification (shrinking Lake Chad, erratic rain), weak governance, arms flows from Libya after 2011, competition over pasture and water between herders and farmers, and armed extremist groups. The Great Green Wall initiative (African Union, launched 2007) aims to restore degraded land in a belt across the Sahel [Verification Required] - check the current progress figures from UNCCD before quoting.

3.4 People of the Sahel and Nile Valley

The region is inhabited by two broad types of groups:

  1. Arab groups - they live near the Nile basin in Egypt and Sudan, have a better standard of living and control political institutions.
  2. Non-Arab groups such as the "Massatiz" and the Tuareg - nomadic herders who rear animals and keep moving in search of pasture and water.

[Correction] This is a broad generalisation, not a rule. (1) The dominant population of the Nile valley and North Africa speaks Arabic, but Berbers (Amazigh) are the indigenous people of the Maghreb and the Sahara fringe, and Nubians, Beja and many Darfuri groups in Sudan are non-Arab. Control of political institutions has historically rested with riverine Arab elites in Sudan, but conflicts (Darfur; the war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023) cut across ethnic lines [Verification Required]. (2) The "Massatiz" is unclear - possibly the Masalit of Darfur (Sudan-Chad), who are non-Arab farmers, [Verification Required]; the Maasai are pastoralists of Kenya and Tanzania (East Africa, see section 5.3), not the Sahel. Typical Sahel nomads are the Tuareg (Sahara fringe: Mali, Niger, Algeria), the Fulani (Fula/Peul) cattle herders from Senegal to Chad and the Toubou.

3.5 Lake Chad

  • Lake Chad is spread across Nigeria, Niger, Chad and Cameroon (Map 2).

[Additional] Lake Chad is a shallow, endorheic (closed-basin) freshwater lake in the Sahel fed mainly by the Chari-Logone river system. It has shrunk drastically since the 1960s because of lower rainfall, irrigation withdrawals and climate variability [Verification Required] - quote exact area figures only from NASA Earth Observatory or the Lake Chad Basin Commission. Its shrinkage and the insurgency of Boko Haram / ISWAP around the basin are a standard example of the climate-security link.

3.6 The Nile River System

  • The Nile (the "longest river of the planet" in the rough notes) flows through the eastern part of the Sahara and the Sahel region.
  • It originates as the White Nile from Lake Victoria, and is joined by the Blue Nile in Sudan. The combined stream flows northward as the Nile and forms a delta on the Mediterranean coast of Egypt.
  • Ethiopia has built the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) on the Blue Nile, which contributes "85%" of the water in the Nile.
  • Aswan Dam in Egypt forms Lake Nasser (man-made).
  • Lake Victoria is spread across "4 countries".

Source: the White Nile is not a stream "from Lake Victoria" alone: the Victoria Nile leaves the lake at Jinja (Uganda), crosses Lakes Kyoga and Albert and the Sudd marshes of South Sudan to become the White Nile; the farthest headwaters lie in Rwanda and Burundi (the Kagera basin). The Blue Nile rises from Lake Tana in Ethiopia, and the two meet at Khartoum. The Nile flows through the Sahara (a perennial "exotic" river) - the only major river that crosses the entire desert.

Egypt (which depends on the Nile for about 90% of its water) and Sudan have raised concerns about downstream flows and the absence of a binding filling and operation agreement. The spelling "Etiopia" = Ethiopia; the name is the Aswan High Dam (completed 1970; the "Nasir/Nasser" lake extends into Sudan as Lake Nubia).

[Additional] India link: The Nile dispute is a standard example of transboundary water conflict (compare the Indus Waters Treaty and Brahmaputra dams). GERD also supports Ethiopia's electricity exports (Kenya signed a purchase agreement of up to 400 MW per press reports [Verification Required]).

4. Region 2 - Congo Basin

4.1 Extent, River and Rainforest

  • The Congo basin is spread across the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Republic of the Congo, Central African Republic, Cameroon, Gabon, Equatorial Guinea and parts of Angola (Map 1).
  • It is drained by the Congo River, which "mostly flows through the Rift Valley", with a very high hydroelectric capacity.
  • The Great Inga project (DRC) is on the Congo River.
  • The basin has the second-largest patch of rainforest / evergreen forest - the "green lung of the earth".
  • The region has the largest peatlands in the world. Peatlands are wetlands which hold a huge amount of carbon in the form of undecomposed organic matter; they also support fisheries.

[Correction] (1) The Congo River flows mainly through the Congo (Cuvette Centrale) basin, a broad shallow depression, not along the Rift Valley. Its headwaters (the Lualaba) rise on the highlands of Katanga near the western branch of the Rift, and Lake Tanganyika (a rift lake) drains into it through the Lukuga, which is the link the note may refer to. The river crosses the Equator twice, has the second-largest discharge in the world (after the Amazon) and is the world's deepest river. It reaches the Atlantic through a series of rapids and falls (Livingstone Falls, Inga) that make the lower river non-navigable - one reason for under-development noted in section 1. (2) Great Inga is a planned mega-project (tens of gigawatts proposed); the existing dams at Inga (Inga I and II) are much smaller [Verification Required] - confirm the current status and capacity from the DRC / World Bank before quoting. (3) Peat is partially decomposed plant matter (waterlogged, so decay is slow); the Congo basin's Cuvette Centrale is the largest tropical peatland complex, not the world's largest peatland - the world's largest peatlands are in the boreal zone (e.g. the West Siberian Lowland) [Verification Required] - the tropical peatland area and carbon stock (a figure of about 30 billion tonnes is widely quoted from a 2022 Nature study) should be checked in the source before quoting.

4.2 Minerals

  • The Congo basin is rich in petroleum and critical minerals: cobalt, coltan, gold, diamonds, lithium, copper, tungsten, etc.
  • The "Catanga plateau" in the DRC is known for cobalt and tantalum production. Gold mining also takes place in the DRC.

4.4 Agriculture and Threats

  • Two types of agricultural systems in the Congo basin: (1) slash-and-burn (shifting) agriculture; (2) plantation agriculture - rubber, coffee, palm oil.
  • The expansion of oil-palm cultivation is a threat.
  • Climate change, infrastructure projects, agriculture and mining are all posing threats to the forest.

[Additional] In slash-and-burn cultivation, the forest is cut and burnt, crops (cassava, plantain, maize) are grown for a few years and the plot is then left fallow; it is sustainable at low population density but causes degradation when fallow periods shorten (compare milpa and jhuming). Other pressures: logging, charcoal and fuelwood demand, and roads that open the forest. India link: India is a major importer of palm oil (mainly from Indonesia and Malaysia) and supports the National Mission on Edible Oils - Oil Palm (NMEO-OP), 2021 [Verification Required] for current targets.

5. Region 3 - East African Highlands

5.1 Extent and Relief

  • Spread across Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Malawi, Mozambique and Zambia.
  • It is the highest region of Africa; Mt Kilimanjaro - a volcanic peak - is in Tanzania.
  • The East African region has been uplifted due to faulting. Faulting created a crack / fissure in eastern Africa called the East African Rift Valley, with multiple lakes and rivers.
  • The Red Sea and the Jordan river valley (West Asia) are the northern extension of this rift.

[Correction] (1) The highlands are not uplifted by faulting alone: the crust was domed upward over a hot mantle upwelling (the Afar/East African plume) and then stretched (crustal extension), which produced the normal faults and the down-dropped graben that form the rift valley (Figure 1). The rift lies on the boundary where the Nubian (African) Plate is splitting from the Somali Plate; it is a divergent plate-boundary setting that may eventually open a new ocean. (2) "Fisher" = fissure. (3) The Rift has two branches: the Eastern (Gregory) Rift (Ethiopia-Kenya-Tanzania; volcanic, with Kilimanjaro and Mt Kenya nearby) and the Western (Albertine) Rift (Lakes Albert, Edward, Kivu, Tanganyika and Malawi; deep lakes). Lake Victoria lies in the shallow depression between the two branches. (4) The Red Sea is a younger arm of the same rift system; the Gulf of Aqaba-Dead Sea-Jordan valley follows the related Dead Sea Transform fault (a transform boundary), so it is usually described as the northern continuation rather than an identical structure. Ethiopia's highlands (Abyssinian Plateau) are the highest large block; Kilimanjaro (Uhuru Peak, 5,895 m) is Africa's highest point and the world's highest free-standing mountain.

5.2 Minerals

  • The East African Highlands are rich in graphite ("2 largest"), lithium, tungsten and copper.

5.4 Challenges of Agriculture and Farm-Size Trends

  • Challenges: small farmers, weak institutional capacity, rain-fed farming, poor technology, inadequate infrastructure, climate change and demographic change.
  • According to a recent survey, the number of medium-sized land holdings has increased; land consolidation is happening due to purchase of agricultural land by urban people and influential people.

[Correction] The trend described matches research on "medium-scale farms" (roughly 5-100 hectares) in Ghana, Kenya, Tanzania and Zambia, where land is bought by urban professionals, civil servants and investors [Verification Required] - the survey is not named in the rough notes; confirm from the source (e.g. Jayne et al., Agricultural Economics, 2016) before quoting. The process is better called land concentration / rise of medium-scale farms than "consolidation" (which usually means merging fragmented plots), and it can displace smallholders.

[Additional] Most farms in the region are under 2 hectares and rain-fed; yields of maize and other staples are low because of limited fertiliser, seed and irrigation, and the region is exposed to droughts and floods (for example the 2011 and 2020-23 Horn of Africa droughts). India link: India imports pulses (pigeon pea / tur from Tanzania, Malawi and Mozambique) under bilateral arrangements [Verification Required] for current import data.

5.5 The Zambezi, Dams and Victoria Falls

  • Zambezi River hydropower projects: the Kariba Dam and the Cahora Bassa Dam (Map 2).
  • Victoria Falls - "the world's most voluminous falls".

[Correction] Victoria Falls (Mosi-oa-Tunya, "the smoke that thunders") on the Zambia-Zimbabwe border is described as the world's largest sheet of falling water, about 1.7 km wide and about 108 m high, a UNESCO World Heritage Site - it is not the most voluminous waterfall (that title is given to falls such as Inga on the Congo, depending on the measure). Kariba (1959) is on the Zambia-Zimbabwe border and holds one of the largest man-made reservoirs by volume, Lake Kariba; Cahora Bassa (1970s) lies in Mozambique downstream. The Zambezi flows east to the Indian Ocean and is a southern African river rather than a highland-only river; its basin is shared by Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique and others [Verification Required] for exact figures.

6. Region 4 - West Africa (Guinea Coast)

  • A climatically diverse region along the Guinea coast, spread across Senegal, Gambia, Guinea-Bissau, Guinea, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Ivory Coast (Cote d'Ivoire), Ghana, Togo, Benin and Nigeria; Burkina Faso is an inland country.
  • The coastal part is warm and humid; the northern part is semi-arid.
  • The region is drained by two river systems: the Niger and the Volta; both flow into the Gulf of Guinea.
  • Agriculture is mostly the same as in East Africa: plantations (cocoa, rubber, coffee); small-scale mixed farming - food crops (maize and rice) and cash crops (cotton, tobacco, groundnut); animal rearing; small-scale landholdings.
  • The region is rich in petroleum, gold and bauxite.
  • Nigeria has the 2nd largest petroleum reserves after Libya - onshore as well as offshore.

[Correction] (1) Mali and Niger are also landlocked countries in the Sahel-West African zone (Burkina Faso is not the only one). (2) Nigeria's reserves: the OPEC Annual Statistical Bulletin 2025 (via the US EIA) puts Nigeria's proven crude-oil reserves at 37.5 billion barrels (2024), the largest in Africa after Libya (the Libyan figure was not re-checked here [Verification Required]) - the statement should read "in Africa", not "in the world"; Nigeria also holds Africa's largest proved natural-gas reserves (211.1 trillion cubic feet). Nigeria produced about 1.5 million barrels a day of crude and condensate in 2024 (EIA); the Dangote refinery (650,000 b/d, Lekki near Lagos, commercial operations from January 2024) is Africa's largest. The oil is mostly light, sweet crude from the Niger Delta (onshore and shallow water) and deep-offshore fields. (3) Bauxite: Guinea (Boke) is the world's largest producer (about 150 Mt in 2025e) and holds the largest reserves (7.4 billion tonnes) - USGS. Gold: Ghana is Africa's largest producer (about 150 t in 2025e, the world's sixth). (4) Cocoa - Cote d'Ivoire and Ghana are the leading producers in the world [Verification Required] for the current shares (ICCO).

[Additional] India link: Nigeria exported about 123,000 barrels a day of crude to India in 2024 - the largest Asian buyer of Nigerian crude (EIA); Nigerian LNG also reaches India. The Niger flows in a great arc (rising near the Atlantic in the Guinea highlands, flowing north-east to Timbuktu and then south-east into the Gulf of Guinea through its delta); the Volta is dammed at Akosombo (Lake Volta, Ghana) (Map 2).

7. Region 5 - Southern Africa

7.1 Countries and Relief

  • Countries: South Africa, Lesotho, Swaziland (Eswatini), Zimbabwe, Namibia, Botswana and southern Angola.
  • The eastern margin is the Drakensberg mountains - also called the Great Escarpment.
  • The plateau ("Weld") west of the Drakensberg has temperate grasslands, spread across South Africa and Zimbabwe.
  • The western side has a semi-arid region - the Karoo; the Namib is a sand-dune desert on the Atlantic coast.

7.2 Rivers and the Okavango

  • Rivers of the region flow east to west, e.g. the Orange River.
  • The Limpopo River flows eastward "due to the rift valley".
  • The Okavango wetland is a playa in the Kalahari region of Botswana, called the "jewel of the Kalahari", and is inhabited by the Bushman tribe.

[Correction] (1) "Swaziland" was renamed Eswatini in 2018; "Weld" = veld (Afrikaans for field) - the high plateau grassland is the Highveld (South Africa, with a similar highveld in Zimbabwe). The Drakensberg is the highest part of the Great Escarpment, which rims the southern African plateau, and includes Lesotho's Thabana Ntlenyana (about 3,480 m, the highest point in southern Africa) [Verification Required]. (2) The Orange River rises in the Lesotho highlands (Drakensberg) and flows west across the plateau to the Atlantic because the interior plateau tilts west; most of the other major rivers (Limpopo, Save, Zambezi) flow east. (3) The Limpopo flows east to the Indian Ocean (Mozambique) down the eastern side of the plateau; there is no source linking this to the Rift Valley [Verification Required]. (4) The Okavango is an inland delta (alluvial fan), not a playa: the Okavango River (from Angola) spreads over the Kalahari sands and ends in swamps and evaporation without reaching the sea (endorheic). A playa is a dry salt-lake bed - the neighbouring Makgadikgadi Pans are the playa example. The delta is a UNESCO World Heritage Site; Bushmen (San) are the indigenous hunter-gatherers of the Kalahari.

7.3 Agriculture

  • The Veld / eastern highland region has advanced commercial agriculture (maize and wheat).
  • The Cape region is known for Mediterranean agriculture (wheat and citrus).
  • Sheep rearing is practised in the western dry region ("subsistence") - Karoo.
  • Dairy and horticulture have developed around Pretoria and Johannesburg.

[Correction] Karoo sheep farming (Merino wool, mutton) is mostly commercial ranching, not subsistence. The Cape region has a Mediterranean climate (winter rain) and is also famous for grapes and wine, deciduous fruit and citrus. The cities named lie in Gauteng.

7.4 Minerals

  • Rich in manganese, chromium and platinum - South Africa is No. 1 producer.
  • Diamond and gold are also found; Botswana is No. 1 producer of diamonds in the world by value.
  • Namibia is the 3rd largest producer of uranium after Kazakhstan and Canada.
  • Zimbabwe has lithium reserves.

8. Industry in Africa

  • The level of industrialisation in Africa is relatively lower than on other continents.
  • Industry is present in four regions:
    1. South Africa - the most industrialised country of Africa; major cities Cape Town and Johannesburg; industry is technologically advanced and highly diversified.
    2. Morocco - known for the automobile industry; Casablanca and Tangier are the major industrial centres; attracting investment in electric vehicles and lithium-battery manufacturing (gigafactory).
    3. Kenya and Egypt - other industrial countries with basic industries such as cement, chemicals and food processing.

[Correction] (1) The rough notes announce four regions but list three; a fourth industrial pole is usually Nigeria / West Africa (Lagos - cement, refining, food) [Verification Required] - the class's fourth region is not stated. (2) South Africa's industrial core is Gauteng (Johannesburg-Pretoria, with the Witwatersrand), with Durban, Cape Town, Gqeberha and East London as coastal centres; Cape Town is a secondary industrial and port centre, not the chief one. (3) Morocco has become Africa's major vehicle-exporting country with plants near Tangier (Tanger Med free zone) and Kenitra (and Casablanca); investment in battery cells is recent [Verification Required] - confirm current output and projects from official Moroccan or industry sources. (4) Egypt has a wider base than the note suggests (textiles, fertiliser, steel, petroleum products, automobile assembly) [Verification Required]. The low overall level is explained by small markets, weak transport and power, and the colonial pattern of exporting raw materials.

9. Region 6 - Islands and India's Interests

9.1 Madagascar and the Surrounding Islands

  • The major island is Madagascar, a continental island situated near Mozambique; it is rich in graphite and chromium.
  • Known for vanilla production; has a "monsoon climate".
  • Surrounded by multiple volcanic islands such as "seashells" (Seychelles), Comoros, Mauritius and Reunion.

[Correction] (1) "Seashells" = Seychelles. (2) Madagascar is a continental island - a fragment of Gondwana that separated from Africa and later from India - and is the world's fourth-largest island and a biodiversity hotspot. It is the world's leading vanilla producer [Verification Required]; for graphite it ranks No. 2 in the world (80,000 t, 2025e, USGS). (3) Its climate is not monsoonal: the east coast has a hot, wet, trade-wind rainforest climate (rain from the south-east trades), the west is drier with a distinct dry season, and the south-west is semi-arid. (4) Not all the surrounding islands are volcanic: Comoros, Mauritius and Reunion are volcanic (Piton de la Fournaise on Reunion is active), but the inner Seychelles are granitic islands - ancient continental fragments - while the outer Seychelles (Aldabra) are coral atolls [Verification Required]. (5) Reunion and Mayotte are French departments.

9.2 India's Strategic Interests in the Western Indian Ocean

  • Agalega Islands (Mauritius) - India is engaged in infrastructure projects there.
  • Chagos Islands - in the "southern equatorial Indian Ocean"; recently Britain agreed to return these islands, except Diego Garcia, to Mauritius.
  • Assumption Island (Seychelles) - there was a diplomatic effort by India for projects there.

[Correction] (1) Chagos: the UK and Mauritius signed a treaty on 22 May 2025 under which the UK would transfer sovereignty over the archipelago to Mauritius while keeping Diego Garcia on a long lease (99 years) for the US-UK base; but the treaty is signed, not in force and reports in October 2026 describe the deal as stalled (Al Jazeera, 2 October 2026) [Verification Required] - check the current status before quoting. The Chagos lie in the central Indian Ocean, about 6-7°S, south of the Maldives, on the Chagos-Laccadive ridge; they are not part of Africa geographically but are administered with Mauritius in politics. The International Court of Justice's 2019 advisory opinion said the UK should end its administration. (2) Agalega (two small islands about 1,100 km north of Mauritius): India has built an airstrip and jetty there, inaugurated in February 2024 [Verification Required] - confirm from the Ministry of External Affairs. (3) Assumption Island (part of the Aldabra group of outer Seychelles): a 2015 agreement to develop a naval facility ran into domestic political opposition in Seychelles and was stalled / not implemented [Verification Required] - verify from official sources.

[Additional] Why these islands matter: They sit along the sea lanes between the Gulf and East Asia and around the Cape route, near the Mozambique Channel; India's SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region, 2015) policy and the maritime security grouping with Mauritius, Seychelles, Sri Lanka and the Maldives reflect this. See the 10-07-2026 notes for the political status of Mayotte, Reunion and Western Sahara.

2. Physiographic Divisions of Europe

Geographically, Europe may be divided into 4 regions (the rough notes list three):

  1. Highlands of North-West Europe (Scandinavian Highlands)
  2. Alpine mountains
  3. Great European Plain
  4. Central Uplands [Additional] - not in the rough notes

[Correction] The notes announce four regions but list only three (the heading "Great European Plain" is empty). The standard fourth division is the Central Uplands (old, low block mountains and plateaus - Meseta, Massif Central, Ardennes, Vosges, Black Forest, Harz, Bohemian Massif) [Verification Required] - confirm the class's fourth division from the next class or from the teacher's notes.

2.1 Highlands of North-West Europe (Scandinavian Highlands)

  • Also called the Scandinavian Highlands.
  • They are related to the Appalachian Mountains of the USA; old fold mountains rich in minerals.
  • Sweden is known for iron-ore production (Kiruna region); rare-earth metals in Norway.
  • The region has imprints of the Ice Age, for example fjords - glacial valleys submerged under sea water. Norway is famous for fjords (tourism, fishing, natural harbours for ports) and for petroleum.
  • The North Sea is the major producer of petroleum in western Europe; Norway has a large share in North Sea petroleum (Brent oil, high-quality sweet oil).

[Correction] Relation to the Appalachians: the Scandinavian Highlands (the Scandes), the Scottish Highlands and the northern Appalachians are fragments of the same Caledonian-Appalachian mountain belt that formed when ancient continents collided and was later split by the opening of the Atlantic (the "related" idea). They are very old, eroded and low (compared with the Alps), and are built of ancient crystalline rocks of the Fennoscandian (Baltic) Shield, which is why they hold iron ore, copper and other metals. "Nowagian" = Norwegian; "Kiruna" = Kiruna in northern Sweden (Lapland), the site of a very large underground iron-ore mine run by the state-owned LKAB [Verification Required] - confirm "Europe's largest" before quoting; the town is being relocated because of mining subsidence.

[Correction] Rare earths: both Norway (Fen carbonatite complex, Telemark) and Sweden (Per Geijer deposit near Kiruna) have announced large rare-earth discoveries in 2023-24 - Europe's biggest known resources - but they are not yet producing, so "rich in rare earths" should be read as resources, not production [Verification Required] - confirm tonnages and status from the Geological Survey of Norway / Sweden or the companies before quoting. Fjords are U-shaped glacial troughs flooded by the sea after the ice melted (Figure 2); they also occur in Chile, New Zealand (Fiordland), Alaska, British Columbia, Greenland and Scotland (sea lochs). Because the water is deep right to the shore and sheltered, they are natural harbours; Norway's fjord coast has few large ports because steep terrain limits the hinterland, so the main uses are tourism, fishing, fish farming (salmon) and hydropower.

[Correction] Brent oil is not a Norwegian field and is not "Norway's share": Brent was a field in the UK sector of the North Sea (east of Shetland) that gave its name to the benchmark; today "Brent" is a benchmark grade priced from a basket of North Sea streams (Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk, Troll, and since 2023 WTI Midland) [Verification Required] - confirm the current basket from the price-reporting agency. Brent is light and sweet (low sulphur). Norway produces from its own fields (Ekofisk, Statfjord, Johan Sverdrup, Troll) and is western Europe's largest oil and gas exporter [Verification Required] for exact production shares. India link: Brent is the benchmark for India's imported crude (the "Indian basket" is a mix of Oman, Dubai and Brent), so Brent prices directly affect India's import bill.

2.2 Alpine Mountains

  • The young fold mountains of Europe, uplifted due to the collision between the African and Eurasian plates.
  • They have multiple ranges: Pyrenees (France, Spain and Andorra), Alps, Carpathians, Caucasus.
  • The Caucasus is the highest range. Mt Elbrus is the highest peak of Europe and part of the Caucasus range in Russia.
  • The main Caucasus range is in Russia; the lower Caucasus / Transcaucasus spread across Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan.
  • Abkhazia, on the Black Sea coast, is a breakaway region of Georgia where Russia is planning to develop its 2nd naval base.

(2) The Alpine orogeny resulted from the northward movement of the African (and smaller Adriatic/Arabian) plates towards Eurasia - the same collision that built the Atlas in North Africa (section 3.2) and, further east, the Himalaya. Mont Blanc is the highest peak of the Alps, so "the Alps" are not the highest range - the Caucasus are. 2.3 Great European Plain

  • Extent: from the French Atlantic coast (Aquitaine and the Paris basin) through the North German Plain, Poland and the Baltic states into the East European (Russian) Plain up to the Urals (Map 5).

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical themes synthesised from the main notes:

  • Why is resource-rich Africa under-developed? Physical barriers (escarpments, rapids, deserts, rainforest), colonial borders that ignore ethnic geography, landlocked states, weak institutions, raw-material export dependence (the "resource curse") and conflict minerals; link to Libya, Sahel and eastern DRC.
  • Sahel crisis: climate stress + weak states + arms flows + extremism = coups and displacement; consequences for migration and for India's trade and Africa policy.
  • Transboundary rivers: Nile (GERD, Aswan, colonial-era treaties), Zambezi, Niger and Congo - hydropower potential vs downstream rights; compare Indus and Brahmaputra.
  • Critical minerals: DRC cobalt, Zimbabwe lithium, graphite (Madagascar, Mozambique, Tanzania), South African PGMs and manganese, Guinean bauxite - corridors (Lobito vs TAZARA), export quotas and value addition; India's critical-minerals strategy.
  • Congo basin as a climate asset: rainforest and tropical peatland carbon stock vs oil palm, mining and infrastructure pressure.
  • East African Rift: a divergent plate boundary that explains highlands, deep lakes, volcanoes and the northward link to the Red Sea - Jordan valley.
  • Agriculture: smallholder, rain-fed, low technology; rise of medium-scale farms; plantation export crops (coffee, tea, cocoa).
  • Indian Ocean islands: Agalega, Chagos and Assumption in India's SAGAR / MAHASAGAR-type maritime outlook - sea-lane security and Africa partnership.
  • Europe's physiography: old Caledonian highlands in the north-west, young Alpine fold mountains in the south, and a broad plain across the continent; the plain explains dense agriculture, rivers and population, and the invasion-prone geography of Eastern Europe.
  • Black Sea geopolitics: Turkish straits and the Montreux regime; Crimea, Abkhazia and Novorossiysk-Sevastopol as naval nodes.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Sahara = largest hot desert, not the largest desert (Antarctica). Niger is on the Tropic of Cancer too.
  • Lake Victoria - 3 countries (Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania), not 4; Lake Chad - 4.
  • The Nile is not uncontested as the "longest river"; the Blue Nile gives most of the flood flow but not 85% all year; the correct name is the Aswan High Dam.
  • Congo River does not flow in the Rift Valley; Cuvette Centrale is the largest tropical peatland, not the world's largest peatland.
  • Great Inga is planned, not built. Katanga = copper and cobalt; coltan is in the east.
  • Lobito Corridor is Western-backed, TAZARA and the Kenya SGR are Chinese-built.
  • Victoria Falls is the largest sheet of water, not the most voluminous.

30-Second Revision

Africa: 6 regions - Sahara/Sahel + Maghreb (Atlas, phosphate, Nile, GERD, Aswan); Congo basin (rainforest, peat, cobalt-copper, Inga, Lobito); East African Highlands (Rift, Kilimanjaro, lakes, graphite, coffee-tea, Zambezi dams); West Africa (Niger/Volta, Nigeria oil, Guinea bauxite, Ghana gold, cocoa); Southern Africa (Drakensberg, Highveld, Karoo, Namib, Okavango, PGM-chrome-manganese-uranium-lithium); islands (Madagascar, Mauritius, Seychelles; Agalega-Chagos-Assumption). Industry: South Africa (Gauteng), Morocco (cars), Egypt, Kenya, Nigeria. Europe: seas - Adriatic, Aegean, Black, Caspian, Baltic, North; Dover, Channel, Turkish Straits; Malta, Cyprus; Urals. Four regions: Scandinavian (old, fjords, Kiruna, North Sea oil), Alpine (young - Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, Caucasus), Great European Plain, Central Uplands.
