Europe (continued)

3. Great European Plain

  • A vast, fertile plain extending from the Atlantic coast in the west up to the Ural Mountains in the east (Map 1).
  • It is drained by many rivers: Thames, Loire, Seine, Rhine, Elbe, Vistula, Danube, Dniester, Dnieper, Don and Volga.
  • The Volga is the longest river of Europe.

3.1 Climate, soils and farming

  • Western part - cool maritime (British-type) climate, favourable for wheat, horticulture and dairy farming.
  • Farmers here are connected with agri-businesses through legal institutions such as contract farming and cooperatives.
  • Eastern part - steppe / temperate-grassland climate. The fertile chernozem (black) soil supports commercial grain farming; major crops include wheat, sunflower, potato, barley and others.
  • In the drier margins of the steppes, animal ranching is practised, known for meat and hides.

[Correction] Crops and soils. "Steppy type" = steppe-type. (1) Chernozem forms under tall-grass steppe where cold winters slow decay, leaving thick humus; it stretches from Ukraine and Moldova across southern Russia to Kazakhstan - it is not the same as India's black (regur) soil, which forms on Deccan basalt. (2) Potato is grown mainly in the cooler north and centre of the plain (Poland, Belarus, Germany, central Russia) while wheat, sunflower and sugar beet are the black-earth belt crops; Ukraine and Russia are the world's two largest sunflower-seed producers [Verification Required] for the current year's rank (FAOSTAT / USDA). (3) The "drier margins" are the lower Volga and northern Caspian lowlands, where sheep and cattle ranching gives meat, hides and wool.

  • These highlands have reserves of "critical" minerals - copper, uranium, silver, gallium, indium, zinc and others.
  • Prominent highlands: Meseta in the Iberian peninsula, Vosges and Lorraine in France, Black Forest in Germany (the list in the notes ends with a comma - see the table for the usual continuation).

4. Mediterranean Region

  • The Mediterranean coast of Europe covers Spain, France, Monaco, Italy, the Balkan countries (Slovenia, Croatia, Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro), Greece, Turkey, Malta and Cyprus (Map 2).
  • Major rivers flowing into the Mediterranean Sea: the Rhone (France) and the Po (Italy; Lombardy plain).
  • The region is known for horticulture, dairy and sericulture.

4.1 Mediterranean agriculture

  • Horticulture - fruits, vegetables and tree crops: citrus, olives, grapes and figs.
  • Dairy - concentrated in the Po (Lombardy) plain, famous for cheese.
  • Sericulture - silk-rearing (mulberry-fed silkworms), historically important in Lombardy and southern France.

[Additional] Why these crops? The Mediterranean (Csa/Csb) climate has hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, so deep-rooted or drought-tolerant tree crops thrive and irrigation fills the summer gap; the rain falls in winter when most crops are dormant. Wheat and barley are grown as winter crops, and vineyards and olive groves dominate the dry slopes.

[Correction] Mediterranean farming. The best-known group is tree crops and viticulture, not only "horticulture". Dairy is a speciality of the Po plain (and parts of France) rather than the dry coastal belt. Sericulture was historically important in Lombardy (Como) and southern France (Cevennes, Lyon) but is now very small in Europe; today China and India are the leading silk producers - India is the world's second-largest producer [Verification Required] (check the Central Silk Board).

5. Steel Industry in Europe

  • Annual steel production in the EU stands at around 125 million tonnes.
  • European countries are described as "highly dependent on imported steel" to meet their demand.
  • Mostly about 50% of steel is produced through blast-furnace technology. These integrated steel plants are located either near coalfields - the Ruhr basin (Germany), Upper Silesia (Poland) - or at coastal locations - Dunkirk, Port Talbot, Taranto (Italy), Marseille, the Rhone valley (Map 3).
  • Around 45% of European steel is produced by electric arc furnace (EAF) technology in mini steel plants, located near cities (scrap supply and power).
  • Challenges: high labour costs, stagnant domestic demand, carbon tax and depletion of local raw materials.
  • To protect domestic steel from the carbon-price burden, the EU has adopted the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), under which it imposes a high import duty on carbon-intensive steel.

"Mostly 50%" is better written "a little over half" through BF-BOF, with the EAF share about 44-45% (the notes' 45% is consistent) [Verification Required] - the exact year-wise split should be taken from EUROFER's European Steel in Figures. The transitional phase (reporting only) ran from October 2023 to December 2025. India link: the EU is an important market for Indian steel and aluminium; India has argued that CBAM is trade-distorting and has sought flexibility in trade talks [Verification Required] - check the latest from the Ministry of Commerce / PIB. Asia

1. Boundary between Europe and Asia

  • The Urals, the Caspian Sea and the Caucasus form the border between Asia and Europe.
  • [Additional] The conventional line runs south along the Ural Mountains and Ural River to the Caspian Sea, then along the Caucasus to the Black Sea and the Turkish Straits (Map 4).

[Correction] The Europe-Asia boundary is a convention, not a natural continental edge (Europe and Asia form one landmass, Eurasia), and authorities draw it differently in the Caucasus - along the main watershed or along the Kuma-Manych depression - which decides whether Elbrus is in Europe (see c29 and c24 in the 10-08-2026 file). [Additional] What is a syntaxial bend? The Himalaya ends at its two ends in sharp turns - the western syntaxis at Nanga Parbat (Pakistan) and the eastern syntaxis at Namcha Barwa (Tibet) - beyond which the ranges swing to a north-south direction: the Sulaiman-Kirthar ranges in the west and the Purvanchal / Arakan Yoma ranges in the east. The bends mark where the Indian plate pushes hardest into Eurasia. The Indus and Brahmaputra cut gorges through them.

[Additional] Taklamakan and Tarim. The Taklamakan is a sandy desert filling the Tarim basin between the Tian Shan (north) and the Kunlun (south). The Tarim River runs along the northern margin and ends in the Lop Nur basin - an inland drainage with no outlet to the sea; Lop Nur's lake has largely dried up [Verification Required] for the present extent.

[Correction] Heading and terms. The Taklamakan is a desert in the Tarim basin, not a mountain range. The Pamir Knot is a mountain knot (a high plateau-like mountain mass, mostly in Tajikistan) rather than a plateau only. "Sytaxial" = syntaxial; "Elburz" = Alborz/Elburz; "Hindukudh" = Hindu Kush; "Tarum" = Tarim; "Torkiye / Turkiye" is the Turkish spelling of Turkey (now also the UN-recognised name, 2022).

3.1 West and Central Asia

  • Two rivers from the highlands of Turkey - the Tigris and the Euphrates - "merge in the Gulf of Persia".
  • In Central Asia, the Syr Darya and Amu Darya flow into the Aral Sea.
  • The Helmand river from the Hindu Kush ends in a lake (Hamun-e-Helmand).
  • The Jordan, Helmand and Syr Darya "merge into a sea" - i.e. they end in inland drainage basins, not in an ocean.

[Correction] Tigris and Euphrates. The two rivers meet at al-Qurna in southern Iraq to form the Shatt al-Arab, which then flows into the Persian Gulf; they do not merge "in the Gulf". Both rise in the highlands of eastern Turkey (Euphrates from the Karasu and Murat; Tigris near Lake Hazar), flow through Syria and Iraq, and give Mesopotamia ("between the rivers") its name. See section 4.4.

[Correction] Rivers that end inland. The Jordan ends in the Dead Sea, the Syr Darya in the Aral Sea and the Helmand in the Hamun lakes - all inland, salt-lake basins with no outlet to the ocean (endorheic), so these are not rivers that "merge into the sea" in the usual sense; the Dead Sea and Aral are lakes called seas. The Amu Darya (the Oxus) rarely reaches the Aral Sea now because its water is diverted for irrigation. "Amur Daria" in the Central Asia notes should read Amu Darya - the Amur is a different river, on the China-Russia border.

3.2 Siberia

  • Siberia is drained by four rivers: Ob, Yenisey, Lena and Amur.
  • The Angara originates in Lake Baikal (the most voluminous freshwater lake) and is a tributary of the Yenisey.
  • The Amur forms the border between China and Russia.

[Correction] Siberian rivers. The Ob, Yenisey and Lena flow north into the Arctic Ocean (Kara Sea and Laptev Sea) - they freeze for months and flood when the upper courses thaw before the mouths do. The Amur is different: it flows east into the Sea of Okhotsk (Pacific) and runs along the Russia-China border for much of its course. Lake Baikal is the world's deepest lake (about 1,640 m) and holds about one-fifth of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water (UNESCO World Heritage, [Verification Required] for exact figures); the Angara is its only outflow and joins the Yenisey, while the Lena rises in the mountains just west of the lake.

3.3 East Asia

  • East Asia has three main rivers that originate in Tibet: Huang Ho (Yellow River), Yangtze and Si Kiang.
  • Huang Ho and Yangtze flow into the East China Sea; Si Kiang into the South China Sea.
  • Taiwan is a junction between the East China Sea and the South China Sea.

[Correction] Sources and mouths. The Huang He and the Yangtze rise on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (in Qinghai province rather than the Tibet Autonomous Region) - the Yangtze in the Tanggula range, the Huang He in the Bayan Har range. The Xi Jiang (Si Kiang, West River) rises on the Yunnan Plateau, not in Tibet, and forms the Pearl River system with the Bei and Dong rivers. The Huang He enters the Bohai Sea (the northern arm of the Yellow Sea), not the East China Sea; the Yangtze enters the East China Sea near Shanghai. The Huang He carries loess silt, which gives it its colour and its reputation as "China's sorrow" for floods; the Yangtze (Asia's longest river) has the Three Gorges Dam.

[Correction] Taiwan. The strait that links the East China Sea and the South China Sea is the Taiwan Strait (about 180 km wide at its narrowest) - Taiwan itself is an island, not a "junction". Taiwan is governed separately from the People's Republic of China, which claims it; most states, including India, have no formal diplomatic relations with Taipei but maintain trade and cultural offices [Verification Required] - check the Ministry of External Affairs. The strait is a major shipping route [Additional].

3.4 South-East Asia

  • South-East Asia has the Indo-China mainland and the islands (Indonesian and Philippine islands).
  • The mainland has five rivers: Irrawaddy and Salween (Myanmar) flow into the Andaman Sea; the rest flow into the South China Sea - Chao Phraya, Mekong (the longest) and the Red River (Vietnam).
  • "All these originate in the Tibet region."
  • Lake Tonle Sap controls floods on the Mekong.

[Correction] Origins and mouths. Only the Salween and the Mekong rise on the Tibetan Plateau. The Irrawaddy forms in Kachin State, Myanmar, where the Mali and N'mai rivers meet; the Red River rises in Yunnan, China; the Chao Phraya forms entirely in Thailand where the Ping, Wang, Yom and Nan rivers meet and enters the Gulf of Thailand. The Salween flows through Myanmar to the Gulf of Martaban in the Andaman Sea. The Mekong is the longest and passes through six countries (China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam). Tonle Sap acts as a flood buffer: when the Mekong floods in the monsoon, water reverses up the Tonle Sap river into the lake, which expands several-fold, and drains back in the dry season; upstream dams can change this "flood pulse" [Verification Required] for current assessments (Mekong River Commission).

[Additional] India link: India's Mekong-Ganga Cooperation (launched 2000) links India with the five Mekong-basin states; the Indus, Sutlej and Brahmaputra also rise on the Tibetan Plateau, which makes the plateau a "water tower" for South and South-East Asia.

West Asia

4.1 Extent and Boundaries

  • West Asia is a dry region surrounded by the Mediterranean Sea and Black Sea in the west, the Red Sea in the south-west, the Arabian Sea in the south, the Sulaiman and Kirthar ranges in the east and the Hindu Kush and Elburz in the north.
  • The Mediterranean coast of West Asia is called the Levant (section 4.2).

[Correction] Limits. "Mediterranean and Black Sea in the west" is fine (the Black Sea borders Turkey's north), and the Caspian Sea, Caucasus and Gulf of Aden are also part of the frame. The Sulaiman and Kirthar ranges are in Pakistan and mark the eastern edge of the Iranian Plateau; some authorities stop West Asia at the Iran-Afghanistan-Pakistan border, and Afghanistan is variously treated as West, Central or South Asia. The Arabian Peninsula, Iran, Iraq, Turkey, the Levant and often the South Caucasus (Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan) are the core. [Verification Required] - confirm which definition the course follows.

  • The Levant region covers Syria, Israel, Lebanon, Jordan, Sinai (Egypt), the territories of the Palestinian Authority (Gaza and the West Bank) and parts of Turkey.
  • The Golan Heights - a plateau on the Israel-Syria border - is a disputed region between these two countries.
  • The Jordan River originates in the highlands of the Syria-Israel border and ends in the Dead Sea (between the West Bank and Jordan).
  • The Golan Heights has a freshwater lake - the Sea of Galilee.
  • The region is strategic because of its height.

[Correction] Golan Heights. Progress has been slowed by regional conflict [Verification Required] - check the Ministry of External Affairs and the Ministry of Ports.

4.3 Turkiye (Asia Minor)

  • Asia Minor / Turkiye is part of West Asia, strategically located at the junction of the Black Sea and the Mediterranean.
  • The Turkish Straits - the Bosporus and the Dardanelles - lie in Turkiye. Most of the trade of Russia and eastern Europe (through the Black Sea) passes through these straits.
  • Turkiye has two mountain ranges - Pontic and Taurus - and the Anatolian Plateau lies between them.
  • Anatolia is a dry region with several hypersaline lakes; "Lake Van is the most saline lake in the world".

[Correction] Straits and trade. "Torkiye / spontik / tourus" = Turkiye / Pontic / Taurus; "Dardaneli" = Dardanelles. The Turkish Straits - Dardanelles, the Sea of Marmara and the Bosporus - link the Aegean (Mediterranean) with the Black Sea and are the only sea route out for the Black Sea states (Ukraine, Russia, Romania, Bulgaria, Georgia and Turkey). It is more precise to say the straits carry the Black Sea trade of Russia, Ukraine and Kazakhstan's Caspian Pipeline Consortium oil exports from Novorossiysk, rather than "most of the trade of Russia and eastern Europe", because much trade moves by rail, the Baltic and other routes [Verification Required] for any percentage. The Montreux Convention (1936) gives Turkey control of the straits and limits warships of non-Black Sea states (see the 10-08-2026 table).

[Correction] Anatolian lakes. Lake Van is Turkey's largest lake, a high-altitude (about 1,640 m) soda (alkaline) lake in eastern Anatolia with moderately saline water; it is not the most saline lake in the world. The hypersaline lake of the Anatolian Plateau is Tuz Golu (Salt Lake), which is largely shallow and dries to a salt crust in summer. Far saltier waters exist elsewhere - the Dead Sea, Lake Assal (Djibouti) and some Antarctic ponds [Verification Required]. Anatolia is also the setting for a 2022 UPSC question (PYQ 3).

4.4 Tigris-Euphrates and Mesopotamia

  • The Tigris and Euphrates originate in the highlands of Turkiye and flow into the Gulf of Persia.
  • They create a fertile basin in Iraq, the birthplace of Mesopotamian civilisation.
  • Cotton and food grains are cultivated with irrigated farming.

[Correction] Mesopotamia. "Mesopotamia civilization" = Mesopotamian civilisation (Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, Assyria). Wheat, barley, dates and (historically) cotton are the main irrigated crops; irrigation and poor drainage cause salinisation, an ancient problem. Iraq's water supply is now stressed by upstream dams in Turkey (the Southeastern Anatolia Project, GAP, including the Ataturk Dam) and in Iran, and by drought [Verification Required] - check recent reports. The southern marshes are a UNESCO World Heritage site [Additional]. Rivers: see c14.

4.5 Persian Gulf, Petroleum and the Strait of Hormuz

  • The Gulf of Persia is surrounded by eight countries: Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, UAE and Oman.
  • It is a major supplier of petroleum to the world market, with both onshore and offshore reserves; more than 50% of proven petroleum reserves of the world are in this region.
  • Saudi Arabia has the 2nd-largest reserves of the world.
  • Petroleum is exported to Asian and European markets through the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint between the Gulf of Persia and the Gulf of Oman. Around 20% of petroleum consumed worldwide passes through it.
  • The strait is also important for fertiliser supply: "around 50% of nitrogenous fertilisers are manufactured in this region".

The Gulf states hold about half of world proven crude oil reserves (roughly 48-50%, depending on whose data and which year is used) rather than "more than 50%" [Verification Required] - check the OPEC Annual Statistical Bulletin and the Energy Institute Statistical Review. Saudi Arabia ranks second in proven crude reserves - after Venezuela, ahead of Iran, Canada and Iraq on OPEC and Energy Institute counts [Verification Required]. Reserves are time-sensitive - state the reference year. Onshore and offshore: Saudi Arabia's Ghawar is onshore, Safaniya is offshore, and the giant North Field / South Pars gas field is shared by Qatar and Iran [Additional].

[Correction] Hormuz and fertilisers. The "20%" is confirmed: the EIA reports about 20 million barrels a day in 2024, equal to about 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption, plus about one-fifth of global LNG (mostly from Qatar). The claim that "around 50% of nitrogenous fertilisers are manufactured in this region" is not supported: the Gulf states (Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Oman, UAE, Bahrain, Kuwait and Iran) are major exporters of urea and ammonia because cheap gas is the feedstock, and a large share of seaborne urea trade passes through Hormuz (a third has been reported) [Verification Required] - confirm with FAO or industry data. India link: India imports crude oil, LNG, LPG and fertilisers (urea, DAP, ammonia) from the Gulf, so Hormuz disruptions raise India's energy and fertiliser costs; the current security situation is time-sensitive and should be checked [Verification Required].

4.6 Yemen, Bab-el-Mandeb and the Horn of Africa

  • Yemen is a country of the Arabian Peninsula, situated near the Bab-el-Mandeb, the chokepoint between the Arabian Sea (Gulf of Aden) and the Red Sea.
  • The strait is "taken by" the militant group called the Houthis (pro-Iran).
  • Political turmoil in Yemen and the Horn of Africa (Somalia, Ethiopia, Djibouti, Eritrea) threatens trade between the Asia-Pacific and Atlantic economies.

[Correction] Houthis and the strait. The Houthis (Ansar Allah), an Iran-backed movement, control north-western Yemen (including the capital Sanaa and the Red Sea port of Hodeidah) and from late 2023 attacked commercial shipping; they do not hold the whole strait - it is shared by Yemen (with Perim Island), Djibouti and Eritrea. The "Atlantic economies" reach Asia through the Suez Canal - Red Sea - Bab-el-Mandeb route, which is why attacks forced many ships to take the longer Cape of Good Hope route. Ethiopia has been landlocked since Eritrea's independence (1993) and depends on Djibouti's port. The security situation is time-sensitive [Verification Required]. India link: the Indian Navy has deployed warships in the Gulf of Aden for anti-piracy duty since 2008 and in the Red Sea against attacks on shipping from 2024 [Verification Required] - check the Ministry of Defence.

Central Asia

  • A bowl-shaped region surrounded by highlands and mountains, spread across Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and northern Afghanistan (Map 9).
  • The region is drained by the Syr Darya and Amu Darya, which flow into the Aral Sea - the border between Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan.
  • It is a dry region where most people practise nomadic herding.
  • In the flood regions of the Syr Darya and Amu Darya, wheat, cotton and millets are cultivated with irrigation.
  • The intensification of irrigated farming together with climate change has magnified the water crisis. According to reports, the Aral Sea has shrunk by more than 50% because of reduced water supply in both rivers.
  • Central Asia is rich in petroleum and critical minerals. Petroleum reserves are concentrated in the Caspian region (Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan).
  • Kazakhstan accounts for more than 40% of global uranium production.
  • Also rich in copper, manganese, lithium, iron, phosphate, chromium, rare-earth metals and others.

[Correction] Geography. "Bowl-shaped" is acceptable for the Aral-Caspian (Turan) lowland, which is a basin of inland drainage ringed by the Kopet Dag, Pamir, Tian Shan and Hindu Kush, though it is open to the north towards the Kazakh steppe and West Siberian plain. Spellings: "Kazkhstan" = Kazakhstan; "Uzbekhstan" = Uzbekistan; "Kyrgykhstan" = Kyrgyzstan; "Tazkhstan" = Tajikistan; "amur daria" = Amu Darya. The usual list of Central Asia is the five "-stan" republics; northern Afghanistan is only sometimes included. Nomadic herding (sheep, goats, horses, camels) suits the dry steppes; oases and river flood-plains (e.g. the Fergana valley and Khorezm) grow cotton, wheat and also rice. [Additional] The Karakum Canal (Turkmenistan) is one of the largest irrigation canals in the world and diverts the Amu Darya. Upstream-downstream tensions (hydropower vs irrigation) shape regional politics.

[Correction] The Aral Sea. "More than 50%" greatly understates the loss. NASA Earth Observatory records that the Aral, once the fourth-largest lake in the world, is a fraction of its 1960 extent, split into a North Aral and a South Aral; the eastern lobe of the South Aral disappeared completely in 2014. The cause was the Soviet diversion of the Syr Darya and Amu Darya from the 1960s to irrigate cotton and other crops. Kazakhstan's Kok-Aral dike and dam (completed 2005) has revived the North Aral (fisheries rebounded) but reduces flow to the South Aral. The widely quoted loss is about 90% of its area [Verification Required] - confirm with UNEP or NASA before quoting. Effects: collapse of fisheries, toxic dust storms, salinisation and a harsher local climate.

[Correction] Uranium. Kazakhstan was the largest producer in 2024 with about 39% of world mine supply (World Nuclear Association: 23,270 tonnes of uranium out of 60,213 t), followed by Canada (24%) and Namibia (12%). "More than 40%" was true in several earlier years (2019-2021) but the share was about 39% in 2024 - a time-sensitive figure that varies with year and production decisions. Uzbekistan also produces (about 4,000 t estimated, 2024). About 55% of world uranium now comes from in-situ leach (ISL) mining, the method used in Kazakhstan. India's own uranium output is small (about 500 t estimated), so Kazakhstan is an important supplier to India's nuclear programme [Verification Required] for current contracts.

[Correction] Petroleum and other minerals. Oil is concentrated in Kazakhstan (Tengiz, Kashagan, Karachaganak); Turkmenistan's wealth is mainly natural gas, not oil; Azerbaijan is the other big Caspian producer. Kazakhstan was the third-largest producer of chromite in 2025 (about 7 Mt of 51 Mt gross ore, behind South Africa and Turkey - USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026), and about 95% of world chromium resources lie in Kazakhstan and southern Africa. Copper, manganese, iron ore and phosphate (Karatau) are well-known Kazakh minerals. Lithium and rare-earth elements are reported as resources or discoveries, not as established production [Verification Required] - confirm with a geological survey before quoting. India link: India's "Connect Central Asia" policy, the proposed TAPI gas pipeline (Turkmenistan-Afghanistan-Pakistan-India) and the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC) via Iran's Chabahar aim at energy and market access [Verification Required] for present status.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Great European Plain: Atlantic coast to the Urals. Volga = longest river of Europe (about 3,530 km, ends in the Caspian); Danube = second longest, ends in the Black Sea. Western plain: cool maritime (Cfb); eastern plain: steppe with chernozem; dry margins - ranching.
  • Old highlands of the plain (Meseta, Massif Central, Vosges, Lorraine, Black Forest, Ardennes, Harz, Bohemian Massif) are mainly Hercynian (Palaeozoic), not Pre-Cambrian; the Pre-Cambrian shields are the Baltic and Ukrainian.
  • Mediterranean coast of Europe: Spain, France, Monaco, Italy, Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, Albania, Greece, Turkey, Malta, Cyprus. Rhone - Gulf of Lion; Po - Adriatic (Lombardy plain). Mediterranean climate: hot dry summer, mild wet winter.
  • EU steel: 129.5 Mt (2024, worldsteel); about 125.8 Mt (2025, EUROFER). Import share a record 29% of consumption (Q3 2025). BF-BOF a little over half, EAF about 44-45%. Integrated sites: Ruhr, Upper Silesia (coalfields); Dunkirk, Taranto, Fos-sur-Mer (coast). Port Talbot is in the UK (Wales).
  • CBAM: definitive regime from 1 January 2026; covers iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, electricity, hydrogen; certificates priced on EU ETS - not an import duty.
  • Asia relief: Pontic and Taurus (Turkey); Zagros and Elburz (Iran); Hindu Kush; Sulaiman and Kirthar (Pakistan) and Arakan Yoma (Myanmar) at the syntaxial bends; Kunlun; Tian Shan; Pamir Knot (Tajikistan). Taklamakan is a desert in the Tarim basin.
  • Rivers: Tigris + Euphrates meet at Qurna - Shatt al-Arab - Persian Gulf. Syr Darya and Amu Darya - Aral basin; Helmand - Hamun; Jordan - Dead Sea (all inland). Ob, Yenisey, Lena - Arctic; Amur - Sea of Okhotsk. Huang He - Bohai Sea; Yangtze - East China Sea; Xi Jiang - South China Sea (rises in Yunnan). Salween and Mekong rise on the Tibetan Plateau; Irrawaddy and Salween - Andaman Sea; Mekong - longest of the mainland South-East Asian rivers; Tonle Sap - flood buffer.
  • Levant: Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine, Jordan (plus sometimes Sinai, Cyprus, Hatay). Golan Heights - disputed (captured 1967, annexed 1981). Jordan - Sea of Galilee (freshwater) - Dead Sea (about 430 m below sea level). Ports: Latakia, Haifa.
  • Turkiye: Bosporus + Dardanelles + Marmara = Turkish Straits; Montreux Convention (1936). Anatolian Plateau between Pontic and Taurus; Lake Van = soda lake; Tuz Golu = salt lake.
  • Persian Gulf: 8 littoral states; about half of world proven oil reserves (about 48-50%); Saudi Arabia 2nd after Venezuela. Hormuz: about 20 mb/d in 2024 = about 20% of world liquids consumption and about 1/5 of LNG (EIA).
  • Bab-el-Mandeb: Red Sea - Gulf of Aden; Houthis control north-west Yemen and attacked ships from late 2023. Horn of Africa: Somalia, Ethiopia (landlocked), Djibouti, Eritrea.
  • Central Asia: five "-stan" republics. Aral Sea - border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan; eastern South Aral lobe gone in 2014; Kok-Aral dam 2005. Kazakhstan about 39% of world uranium (2024; WNA); third-largest chromite producer (2025e; USGS). Oil: Kazakhstan; gas: Turkmenistan.

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical themes synthesised from the main notes:

  • Why is the Great European Plain Europe's core? Level land, fertile soils (loess, chernozem), navigable rivers and canals, coal on the plain's margin, and high population - the basis of agriculture, industry and a dense transport network; also an invasion corridor in the east.
  • West-east contrast: maritime, cooperative, dairy and horticulture agriculture in the west vs large-scale steppe grain and ranching in the east - compare India's agro-climatic regions and cooperative movement.
  • Mediterranean agriculture: climate-adapted tree crops and viticulture, irrigation, tourism pressure and water stress - compare with California, central Chile, south-western Australia and the Cape.
  • Decarbonising European steel: BF-BOF to EAF and hydrogen-based direct reduction; high energy and labour costs, record imports, CBAM and the EU ETS; implications for Indian steel and aluminium exporters and for India's own steel-decarbonisation roadmap.
  • Tibetan Plateau as Asia's "water tower": Huang He, Yangtze, Mekong, Salween, Brahmaputra, Indus and Sutlej rise there - transboundary water, dams and flow data are a strategic issue; compare Mekong-Ganga Cooperation and the Mekong River Commission.
  • Inland-drainage rivers and the Aral lesson: irrigation diversion destroys terminal lakes (Aral, Hamun, Lop Nur, Dead Sea) - environmental, health and livelihood consequences; the upstream-downstream hydropower vs irrigation conflict in Central Asia.
  • Chokepoints: Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb, the Turkish Straits and Suez - energy security, supply-chain risk and India's response (diversified energy sources, strategic reserves, naval presence, IMEC, Chabahar and INSTC).
  • Levant geopolitics: water (Jordan basin, Sea of Galilee), strategic heights (Golan, Hermon) and ports; India's links through Haifa and IMEC.
  • Central Asia: resource-rich but landlocked; competition among Russia, China, the West and India; critical minerals (uranium, chromite) and gas pipelines; India's "Connect Central Asia" policy and TAPI / INSTC.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Volga flows to the Caspian, the Danube to the Black Sea; the Danube is second, not first, in length. The Thames is not a continental-plain river.
  • Chernozem is not regur. Chernozem is black steppe soil; regur (black cotton soil) forms on Deccan basalt.
  • The old highlands of the plain are Hercynian (Palaeozoic), not Pre-Cambrian; Gallium and indium are by-products, not primary mine products.
  • Turkey straddles two continents; Serbia, North Macedonia and Kosovo are landlocked Balkan states; Bosnia has only a tiny coast.
  • Port Talbot is in the UK, not the EU; the EU's problem is imported raw materials and rising steel imports (29%), not "highly dependent on imported steel".
  • CBAM is not an import duty and the EU ETS is not a tax; CBAM's definitive phase began on 1 January 2026.
  • The Taklamakan is a desert, not a range; the Pamir Knot is mountains, not just a plateau; the Sulaiman and Kirthar are in Pakistan.
  • Tigris and Euphrates meet at al-Qurna to form the Shatt al-Arab; they do not meet "in the Gulf".
  • Amu Darya (Central Asia, to the Aral) is not the Amur (Russia-China border, to the Pacific).
  • Huang He enters the Bohai Sea, not the East China Sea; the Xi Jiang rises in Yunnan, not Tibet; the Red and Chao Phraya rivers do not rise in Tibet.
  • The Taiwan Strait, not Taiwan, links the East China and South China seas.
  • Lake Van is a soda lake, not the world's most saline lake; Tuz Golu is Anatolia's salt lake; the Dead Sea is far saltier.
  • The Golan Heights are not recognised by most states as Israeli (US 2019 exception); the Dead Sea and the Aral are lakes.
  • The Gulf holds about half, not more than half, of reserves; Hormuz carries about 20% of liquids; "50% of fertilisers" is not supported.
  • Houthis do not hold the whole Bab-el-Mandeb; they control north-west Yemen.
  • Aral shrinkage is about 90%, not 50%; Kazakhstan's uranium share was about 39% in 2024, not "more than 40%".
