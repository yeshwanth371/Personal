4. The Great Plains and the Mississippi Basin

  • Lies on the eastern side of the Rockies.
  • Drained by the Mississippi river, which flows into the Gulf of Mexico.
  • The western part of the Mississippi basin is the Great Plains.

The Mississippi basin is an extensive, agriculturally fertile region drained by the Mississippi river and its tributaries such as the Ohio (from the east) and the Missouri (from the west). The western part of this basin is an elevated plain called the Great Plains.

[Additional] The Mississippi-Missouri is the longest river system of North America (about 6,000 km) and its basin is the largest in North America, covering about 40% of the conterminous USA (31 US States and 2 Canadian provinces). The Mississippi rises at Lake Itasca (Minnesota), meets the Missouri near St. Louis and the Ohio at Cairo (Illinois), and forms a bird's-foot delta below New Orleans. The Great Plains rise gently westward from about 500 m to about 1,500-1,800 m at the foot of the Rockies, and roughly follow the dry side of the 100th meridian - the traditional line between the humid east and the semi-arid west. Since an executive order of January 2025 the US federal government uses the name "Gulf of America"; the international name remains the Gulf of Mexico.

4.1 Great Plains - Prairies, Wheat and Ranching

  • The Great Plains, spread across Canada and the USA, have temperate grasslands called the Prairies.
  • The region is known for commercial wheat farming and animal (cattle) ranching.
  • Wheat is grown on fertile black soil called chernozem / prairie soil - hence the region is called the "Bread Basket" (of the world).

[Additional] Chernozems are rich in humus from the decay of dense grass roots and low leaching in the sub-humid climate. Two wheat belts: spring wheat (sown in spring, harvested in late summer) in the colder north - Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Alberta, North and South Dakota, Montana; and winter wheat (sown in autumn, harvested in early summer) in the milder south - Kansas (the leading winter-wheat State), Oklahoma, Nebraska. Features: large mechanised farms, low population density, extensive cultivation, high output per worker but low yield per hectare. Similar temperate grasslands: Steppes (Eurasia), Pampas (Argentina), Veld (South Africa), Downs (Australia), Canterbury (New Zealand). The 1930s Dust Bowl on the southern plains followed ploughing of grassland and drought.

4.2 Corn Belt and the South

  • The eastern part of the Mississippi basin is relatively wetter and is famous for corn (maize) and soybean cultivation - the Corn Belt.
  • Corn is used for animal feed, ethanol and bioplastics.
  • Soybean is used for producing edible oil, food, animal feed (soymeal) and industrial raw material.
  • The region south of the Corn Belt is used for cotton, sugarcane, tobacco and rice production.
  • The region is also rich in petroleum reserves.

[Correction] The oil region of the interior USA is called the Mid-Continent oil field (Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas), not "Mid-West petroleum belt" - the Midwest is the Corn Belt / Great Lakes region. Today the largest US oil-producing basin is the Permian Basin (west Texas and south-east New Mexico), and the Bakken shale (Williston Basin, North Dakota) lies under the northern Great Plains. See section 7.4. Spelling: "Ohaio" = Ohio; "praires" = prairies; "soyabean" is acceptable (Indian usage), "soybean" in US usage.

[Additional] The USA is the world's largest producer and exporter of maize; Iowa and Illinois lead. Corn and soybean are grown in rotation (soybean, a legume, fixes nitrogen). Corn-based ethanol is blended into petrol under the US Renewable Fuel Standard - relevant to India's ethanol blending programme (E20), which uses sugarcane and grains. Corn-belt fertiliser run-off carried by the Mississippi causes a seasonal "dead zone" (hypoxia) in the Gulf of Mexico. In the South: tobacco - Kentucky, North Carolina; rice - Arkansas (largest US producer), Louisiana; sugarcane - Louisiana, Florida.

5. Islands of North America

5.1 Hawaiian Islands

  • Part of the USA; situated in the North Pacific Ocean; a product of hotspot volcanism.
  • The south-eastern islands are bigger than the north-western ones. Hawai'i (the "Big Island") is the biggest and south-eastern-most island, from which the group gets its name.
  • There are about 8 main (larger) islands.
  • The Hawai'i region has some of the biggest active volcanoes: Mauna Loa, Kilauea (and Mauna Kea).

[Correction] Hawai'i is not merely "administered" by the USA - it is the 50th State of the USA (since 1959). The reason the south-eastern islands are bigger: the Pacific Plate moves north-west over a stationary hotspot, so the youngest islands are in the south-east (still volcanically active and large), while older islands to the north-west have been eroded and have subsided. Mauna Kea is dormant (last erupted about 4,500 years ago), not active; the active ones are Mauna Loa (largest active volcano on Earth by volume; last erupted 2022) and Kilauea (one of the most active). Spelling: "Hawaian" = Hawaiian; "Mouna" = Mauna.

[Additional] The 8 main islands: Hawai'i, Maui, O'ahu (capital Honolulu, Pearl Harbor), Kaua'i, Moloka'i, Lana'i, Ni'ihau, Kaho'olawe. Volcanoes here are shield volcanoes (basaltic, gentle slopes). Mauna Kea is the tallest mountain from base to peak (about 10,200 m from the sea floor; 4,207 m above sea level) and hosts major observatories. The chain continues north-west as the Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain, whose bend records a change in plate motion about 47 million years ago. A new volcano, the Kama'ehuakanaloa (Lo'ihi) seamount, is growing under the sea south-east of the Big Island.

5.2 Aleutian Islands

  • A chain of volcanic islands, part of the Pacific Ring of Fire.
  • Lie between Alaska (USA) and the Kamchatka peninsula (Russia).
  • Strategically located near the Bering Strait, which connects the Pacific Ocean and the Arctic Ocean.
  • Due to global warming, the Arctic Ocean is becoming navigable for normal ships for longer periods; this may shift some maritime traffic from the Red Sea (Suez) route to Arctic routes.
  • The Red Sea route is not only longer than the Northern Sea Route but also unsafe due to political turmoil and extremism in surrounding countries.
  • In this scenario the Bering Sea and the Aleutian Islands are becoming strategically and geopolitically more important.

[Correction] The Aleutians form a volcanic island arc created by subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate along the Aleutian Trench. They belong to the USA (Alaska), except the westernmost Commander Islands, which are Russian. They separate the Bering Sea (north) from the North Pacific (south); the Bering Strait itself lies about 1,000 km further north, between the Chukchi Peninsula (Russia) and the Seward Peninsula (Alaska), linking the Bering Sea to the Chukchi Sea (Arctic). The Arctic route is the Northern Sea Route (NSR, along Russia's Arctic coast) - not the "North Sea route" (the North Sea is in Europe). Spelling: "Aleutsian" = Aleutian; "Baring" = Bering; "Alsaka" = Alaska.

[Additional] Two Arctic shipping routes both exit to the Pacific through the Bering Strait: the Northern Sea Route (Russia) and the Northwest Passage (through the Canadian Arctic Archipelago). For northern Europe - north-east Asia voyages, the NSR can be up to about 40% shorter than the Suez route. The Red Sea route has been disrupted by Houthi attacks on shipping near Bab-el-Mandeb since late 2023 and earlier by Somali piracy. Limits of Arctic routes: navigable only in summer-autumn, need ice-class ships or icebreaker escort, high insurance costs, sparse ports and search-and-rescue, Russian regulation of the NSR, and environmental risks (oil spills, black carbon). The Bering Strait is about 82 km wide; the Diomede Islands (Big Diomede - Russia, Little Diomede - USA) lie in it, with the International Date Line between them.

5.3 Arctic Archipelago and Greenland

  • A group of islands situated at the junction between the North Atlantic and the Arctic Ocean.
  • Greenland, the largest island in the world, belongs to Denmark.
  • The US and Denmark are reported to be negotiating a deal that may transfer some administrative / security powers regarding Greenland to the USA.
  • Other islands in this group belong to Canada, e.g. Baffin Island, Victoria Island.
  • Davis Strait lies between Greenland and Baffin Island; it connects Baffin Bay and the Atlantic.

[Correction] The term "Arctic Archipelago" normally means the Canadian Arctic Archipelago (about 36,000 islands - Baffin, Victoria, Ellesmere, etc.) north of mainland Canada; Greenland is a separate island to its east, not a member of that group. Greenland is an autonomous territory within the Kingdom of Denmark (Home Rule 1979; Self-Government Act 2009) - it is geographically part of North America but politically linked to Europe, and it left the European Community in 1985. Davis Strait connects Baffin Bay (north) with the Labrador Sea (south), an arm of the Atlantic. The status of any current US-Denmark negotiation on Greenland is [Verification Required] - check the latest news before quoting it. Spelling: "Archiplago" = Archipelago; "startegic" = strategic; "prescence" = presence.

Why does the USA want control over / presence in Greenland?

  1. Strategic location - situated at the transition between the Arctic and the Atlantic.
  2. With increasing trade traffic in the Arctic region, it is gaining strategic and economic value.
  3. Because of its location between the US and Russia, a US base here can track any missile launched by Russia over the polar route.
  4. Greenland has reserves of critical minerals (rare earths, graphite, etc.) and petroleum.
  5. Increasing presence of China: in recent years China has invested in Greenland's mining sector, which the USA perceives as a threat.

[Additional] The US already operates the Pituffik Space Base (formerly Thule Air Base) in north-west Greenland under the 1951 US-Denmark defence agreement; it hosts ballistic-missile early-warning radar. Greenland also guards the GIUK gap (Greenland-Iceland-UK), a naval chokepoint between the Arctic and the Atlantic. Mineral examples: rare earths at Kvanefjeld (Kuannersuit) and Tanbreez, plus graphite, zinc, nickel and copper; significant lithium resources are [Verification Required]. China's Shenghe Resources is a major shareholder in the Kvanefjeld project, and in 2018 a Chinese state firm's bid to build Greenland airports was dropped after Denmark stepped in. Note: Greenland's government stopped new oil and gas licensing in 2021 and passed a law banning uranium mining, which stalled Kvanefjeld - so petroleum remains largely unexploited. Rising Arctic interest has also been driven by melting ice opening sea routes and resources. Island sizes: Greenland about 2.16 million km2 (largest); Baffin Island 5th largest; Victoria Island 8th; Ellesmere Island 10th.

[Additional] Bay vs Gulf - meaning, difference and similarity

Bay: a body of sea water partly enclosed by land, with a wide mouth, forming a broad curve or indentation of the coastline (e.g. Bay of Bengal, Bay of Biscay, Hudson Bay, Baffin Bay, Chesapeake Bay).

Gulf: a large body of sea water that penetrates deep into the land and is more enclosed, usually with a relatively narrow mouth - often a strait (e.g. Gulf of Mexico, Persian Gulf, Gulf of St. Lawrence, Gulf of Aden; in India - Gulf of Kutch, Gulf of Khambhat, Gulf of Mannar).

Similarities: both are coastal indentations of a sea or ocean partly enclosed by land; both offer sheltered water for ports, harbours and fishing; both can be formed by tectonic subsidence, erosion, glaciation or post-glacial sea-level rise; and both names are historical conventions, not strict scientific categories - which is why there are many exceptions in size.

Straits

Straits are narrow channels / natural waterways that connect two large water bodies or seas, e.g. the Bering Strait, which connects the Pacific and the Arctic.

  • Straits are chokepoints on trade routes, which can be captured or blocked by enemy countries, hostile forces or extremist groups. Thus, straits are vulnerable zones on global trade routes.
  • Straits also suffer from congestion, which may increase travel time and the cost of shipping.

[Additional] Key chokepoints: Strait of Hormuz (Persian Gulf - Gulf of Oman; about one-fifth of world petroleum liquids flow), Strait of Malacca (Indian Ocean - South China Sea; China's "Malacca dilemma"), Bab-el-Mandeb (Red Sea - Gulf of Aden), Strait of Gibraltar, Bosphorus and Dardanelles (Black Sea - Mediterranean), Dover Strait (busiest shipping lane). Man-made equivalents: Suez and Panama canals. Contrast: an isthmus is a narrow strip of land joining two land masses (e.g. Isthmus of Panama); a strait is a narrow strip of water. Other risks at straits: accidents and groundings (e.g. Ever Given in the Suez Canal, 2021), piracy, oil spills.

5.4 Newfoundland Island

  • A Canadian island situated near the mouth of the St. Lawrence river.
  • The Newfoundland region is rich in fisheries because of:
    1. Convergence of a cold current and a warm current.
    2. A broad continental shelf.
    3. Foggy conditions.

[Correction] Newfoundland lies at the mouth of the Gulf of St. Lawrence (the river first widens into an estuary and then the gulf) and is part of the province of Newfoundland and Labrador. The currents are the cold Labrador Current and the warm Gulf Stream (North Atlantic Drift). Fog is a result, not a cause, of the fishing richness: warm moist air over the Gulf Stream is cooled over the cold Labrador Current, producing some of the foggiest conditions in the world - a hazard for navigation. The real causes are (1) mixing of the two currents, which brings up nutrients; (2) the shallow continental shelf / banks (the Grand Banks), where sunlight reaches the nutrient-rich water and plankton flourishes; and (3) cool water favouring cod, haddock, herring, etc.

[Additional] The Grand Banks were among the richest fishing grounds in the world until over-fishing caused the collapse of Atlantic cod, leading Canada to declare a moratorium in 1992 - a classic case of the "tragedy of the commons". Similar current-convergence fishing grounds: Dogger Bank (North Sea), off Japan (Kuroshio - Oyashio). The Newfoundland offshore also produces oil (Hibernia) - see section 7.4.

5.5 Caribbean Islands

  • A chain of islands around the Caribbean Sea, located at the junction between the American plates and the Caribbean plate.
  • The northern, bigger islands are the Greater Antilles: Cuba, Hispaniola (Haiti and the Dominican Republic), Jamaica and Puerto Rico.
  • To the south-east of the Greater Antilles are hundreds of smaller islands - the Lesser Antilles.
  • The area has a large concentration of coral reefs.
  • Tobacco and sugarcane plantation crops are grown, along with tropical fruits.

[Correction] Not all are volcanic, and the plates involved are oceanic parts of the North American and South American plates meeting the Caribbean plate. The Lesser Antilles form an active volcanic island arc, where Atlantic oceanic crust subducts beneath the Caribbean plate (volcanoes: Mount Pelée - Martinique, 1902; Soufrière Hills - Montserrat; La Soufrière - St. Vincent, 2021). The Greater Antilles are older, largely non-volcanic today (old arc rocks and limestone) and lie along a transform (strike-slip) boundary - hence earthquakes such as Haiti 2010. Puerto Rico is a US territory; Hispaniola is shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic - not to be confused with Dominica, a separate country in the Lesser Antilles. "2nd largest concentration of coral reefs": the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef (off Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras) is the second-largest barrier reef system after Australia's Great Barrier Reef; the largest overall concentration of reefs is in the Coral Triangle (South-East Asia). "Tropical millets" are not a characteristic crop here; typical crops are sugarcane, tobacco, bananas, coffee, cocoa and spices (e.g. nutmeg in Grenada). Spellings: "Carrabean" = Caribbean; "anteles" = Antilles.

[Additional] The Bahamas lie in the Atlantic, not the Caribbean Sea, and are low coral-limestone islands. Barbados is not volcanic either (coral cap on an uplifted accretionary prism). The region's economy depends heavily on tourism; it is also exposed to hurricanes and to coral bleaching from ocean warming.

6. Mining and Minerals

Two themes: (1) critical minerals and (2) petroleum (section 7).

6.1 Critical Minerals - Concept

The concept of criticality revolves around two things: (a) importance and (b) being irreplaceable in emerging technologies - combined with supply risk.

Critical minerals are minerals that are necessary ingredients of newly emerging technologies such as:

  • Clean-energy technologies - wind turbines, solar panels, EV batteries;
  • Space technologies;
  • Precision weapons / defence technology;
  • Information and communication technologies, AI and robotics.

They are important for the socio-economic development as well as the security / defence preparedness of a country. Their supply is vulnerable to disruption in the wake of war, political turmoil or concentration of production in a few countries.

[Additional] India's Ministry of Mines identified 30 critical minerals (June 2023). The MMDR Amendment Act, 2023 empowered the Centre to auction blocks of critical minerals, and the National Critical Mineral Mission (approved January 2025; outlay Rs 16,300 crore plus about Rs 18,000 crore expected from PSUs) aims at exploration, processing, recycling and overseas acquisition. KABIL (Khanij Bidesh India Ltd) acquires assets abroad (e.g. lithium blocks in Argentina). India is a member of the US-led Minerals Security Partnership (2023). GSI reported an inferred lithium resource of 5.9 million tonnes in Reasi, Jammu and Kashmir (2023).

6.2 Types of Critical Minerals

1. Rare Earth Elements (REEs)

  • 17 metals: the 15 lanthanides plus 2 non-lanthanides - scandium and yttrium - with excellent chemical, electrical and magnetic properties.
  • Used in making permanent magnets and catalytic agents.
  • The word "rare" is a misnomer - they are quite abundant in the Earth's crust.
  • They are called rare because they occur in complex minerals, so purification requires intense processing of the ore. Rare-earth processing is capital-intensive and water-intensive.
  • During processing a lot of waste containing radioactive elements like uranium and thorium is generated; its safe disposal is another challenge.
  • Around two-thirds of global rare-earth mining is concentrated in China; processing is about 90% in China.

[Correction] Mining shares in the rough notes ("15% US, 10% Australia") are outdated. USGS (2024 estimates): world mine production about 390,000 t (REO equivalent) - China about 270,000 t (~69%), USA about 45,000 t (~12%), Myanmar about 31,000 t (~8%), Australia, Nigeria and Thailand about 13,000 t each (~3% each), India about 2,900 t (under 1%). China also refines about 90% and makes about 90%+ of rare-earth permanent magnets (IEA). Spelling: "Yetrium" = yttrium; "lantenides" = lanthanides; "misnomar" = misnomer.

2. Battery Minerals

With rising environmental consciousness and increasing thrust on renewable energy, the importance of energy-storage devices (e.g. lithium-ion batteries) is increasing. Battery minerals: lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, manganese, phosphate (for LFP batteries).

[Additional] Lithium: Australia (hard rock) and the "Lithium Triangle" of Chile-Argentina-Bolivia (brines) dominate; cobalt: DR Congo (about 70%+); nickel: Indonesia (about half); graphite: China. Spelling: "consincise" = consciousness.

3. Refractory and Corrosion-Resistant Metals

Can withstand very high temperatures and resist chemical damage / rusting: tungsten, molybdenum, niobium, tantalum, nickel (superalloys, stainless steel), chromium.

[Correction] Beryllium is not a refractory metal (melting point about 1,287 °C); it is a very light, stiff metal used in aerospace, defence, satellites and X-ray windows - better grouped with aircraft / defence metals. Spellings: "Molybdinu" = molybdenum; "Nickle" = nickel.

4. Platinum Group Metals (PGMs)

Used as catalytic agents (catalytic converters, hydrogen fuel cells, electrolysers), electronics, jewellery.

[Correction] There are six PGMs: platinum, palladium, rhodium, ruthenium, iridium and osmium. "Rinium" is probably rhodium; rhenium is a separate (refractory) metal, not a PGM. "Plladium" = palladium. [Additional] South Africa (Bushveld Complex) leads platinum output; Russia (Norilsk) leads palladium.

5-7. Other Groups

  • Aircraft manufacturing metals: aluminium (light, strong alloys), also titanium and beryllium.
  • Electrical metals: copper (best affordable conductor - grids, motors, EVs).
  • Nuclear minerals: uranium and thorium.

[Additional] Aluminium is on the US critical-minerals list but not on India's list of 30. In India, uranium and thorium are atomic minerals (Part B of the First Schedule, MMDR Act) handled by the Department of Atomic Energy, so they are not counted in the 30. India has large thorium reserves in monazite - the basis of the third stage of its three-stage nuclear programme.

7. Petroleum

Petroleum consists of naturally occurring hydrocarbons trapped in sedimentary basins of the crust.

7.1 Formation

Three processes of formation:

  1. Decomposition of marine plankton (small marine micro-organisms) found in shallow seas;
  2. Sedimentation (burial under layers of sediment);
  3. Structural traps (folding / faulting).

Petroleum is produced by biochemical and geophysical processes such as decomposition of marine micro-organisms, sedimentation, and folding and faulting. Folding and faulting create structural traps in sedimentary rocks, e.g. an anticline, where petroleum is stored.

[Correction] A complete petroleum system needs: source rock (organic-rich shale), maturation (the "oil window" of about 60-150 °C; higher temperatures yield gas), migration, a porous and permeable reservoir rock (sandstone, limestone), an impermeable cap rock (shale, salt) and a trap - structural (anticline, fault, salt dome) or stratigraphic. Organic matter is preserved only in oxygen-poor (anoxic) conditions. "Viscus" = viscous.

7.2 Crude Oil and Natural Gas

  • Crude oil - a viscous liquid made of long-chain hydrocarbons; its heaviest, most viscous form is bitumen.
  • Natural gas - short-chain hydrocarbons (mainly methane).
  • May be found in sandstone or in shale rocks.

7.3 Conventional Gas, Shale Gas and Hydraulic Fracturing

Normally natural gas is found in permeable sedimentary strata like sandstone; this gas can be extracted by conventional drilling. Natural gas found in non-permeable rocks like shale is called shale gas. It is extracted by hydraulic fracturing (fracking): the rock is broken / fractured by pumping fluid under very high pressure; once the rock is fractured, the gas escapes through the fractures into the well.

[Correction] The fracking fluid is mainly water (~90%) with sand (~9%) as a proppant that keeps the cracks open, and a small share (under 1%) of chemicals - it is not necessarily a viscous liquid. Fracking is combined with horizontal (directional) drilling along the shale layer; the gas flows through the fractures into the same well bore, not "another tunnel". The same technique produces shale (tight) oil.

The USA developed this technology and became the world's top producer of natural gas. Currently the USA is the top producer of both natural gas and crude oil.

[Additional] The US has been the world's largest crude oil producer since 2018 (EIA) and the largest natural gas producer for over a decade - the "shale revolution" also made it a major LNG exporter. Major shale plays: Marcellus (Appalachian - gas), Permian and Eagle Ford (Texas - oil), Bakken (North Dakota - oil), Haynesville (Louisiana - gas). The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas has identified six potentially shale-bearing basins in India - Cambay, Assam-Arakan, Gondwana, Krishna-Godavari, Cauvery and Indo-Gangetic - but water scarcity and dense population limit fracking. EIA data for 2023: the USA produced about 21.9 million barrels per day of total oil (crude + other liquids), about 22% of world output, followed by Saudi Arabia, Russia and Canada (4th).

Challenges of fracking:

  1. Risk of earthquakes (induced seismicity);
  2. Groundwater pollution;
  3. Disposal of waste (contaminated "flowback" and produced water);
  4. Huge demand for water.

[Additional] Most induced earthquakes (e.g. Oklahoma's surge after 2009) are linked mainly to deep injection of wastewater, not the fracturing itself (USGS). Add: methane leakage (a potent greenhouse gas), air pollution, land disturbance and truck traffic. Several countries and States (e.g. France) have banned fracking.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Mississippi: source Lake Itasca; Missouri joins near St. Louis, Ohio at Cairo; bird's-foot delta into the Gulf of Mexico.
  • Great Plains = Prairies, chernozem, wheat + ranching ("Bread Basket"); spring wheat (north), winter wheat (Kansas). Corn Belt: Iowa-Illinois-Indiana-Ohio; corn + soybean.
  • Temperate grasslands: Prairies, Steppes, Pampas, Veld, Downs, Canterbury.
  • Hawai'i: 50th US State; hotspot; youngest in SE; active - Mauna Loa, Kilauea; Mauna Kea dormant.
  • Aleutians: island arc (Pacific Plate under North American Plate); separate Bering Sea from the Pacific; Commander Islands - Russia.
  • Bering Strait: Pacific (Bering Sea) - Arctic (Chukchi Sea); about 82 km; Diomede Islands; International Date Line.
  • Greenland: largest island; autonomous territory of Denmark; Pituffik Space Base (US). Baffin Island: 5th largest island (Canada).
  • Davis Strait: Baffin Bay - Labrador Sea (Atlantic), between Greenland and Baffin Island.
  • Bay: wide mouth, open; Gulf: deep inlet, narrow mouth. Bay of Bengal is the largest bay.
  • Grand Banks (Newfoundland): Labrador Current (cold) + Gulf Stream (warm); cod moratorium 1992.
  • Greater Antilles: Cuba, Hispaniola (Haiti + Dominican Republic), Jamaica, Puerto Rico. Lesser Antilles: active volcanic arc. Mesoamerican Barrier Reef: 2nd largest barrier reef.
  • REEs: 17 = 15 lanthanides + Sc + Y; China ~69% of mining, ~90% of refining; India has the 3rd largest REE reserves (USGS).
  • PGMs (6): Pt, Pd, Rh, Ru, Ir, Os. India's critical minerals list: 30 (2023); National Critical Mineral Mission (2025).
  • USA: No. 1 beryllium; Mexico: No. 1 silver; Canada: No. 1 potash, No. 2 uranium (after Kazakhstan), No. 2 niobium (after Brazil).
  • Fracking fluid: ~90% water, ~9% sand (proppant), <1% chemicals + horizontal drilling. USA: top producer of crude oil and natural gas.
  • First commercial oil well: Drake well, Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859. Prudhoe Bay - largest North American oilfield; Athabasca - oil sands (bitumen).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Northern Sea Route is not the North Sea; the Northwest Passage (Canada) is a different route from the NSR (Russia).
  • Aleutians are not in the Bering Strait; they form the southern rim of the Bering Sea.
  • Greenland is not part of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago; it belongs to the Kingdom of Denmark but is geographically North American.
  • Mauna Kea is dormant; Mauna Loa and Kilauea are active.
  • Dominica ≠ Dominican Republic. Greater Antilles are not the active volcanic arc; the Lesser Antilles are.
  • Fog off Newfoundland is an effect of current mixing, not a cause of fish richness.
  • Bay vs gulf is not decided by size - the Bay of Bengal is bigger than the Gulf of Mexico.
  • Mid-Continent oil field ≠ Midwest (Corn Belt).
  • Rare earths are not rare; scandium and yttrium are rare earths but not lanthanides.
  • Rhenium is not a platinum-group metal; beryllium is not a refractory metal.
  • Potash in Canada is sedimentary (Saskatchewan prairies), not from the Shield.
  • Shale is usually the source rock; conventional reservoirs are sandstone / limestone. Most induced quakes are linked to wastewater injection, not fracturing itself.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2012 - General Studies Paper I

Recently, there has been a concern over the short supply of a group of elements called 'rare earth metals'. Why?

  1. China, which is the largest producer of these elements, has imposed some restrictions on their export.
  2. Other than China, Australia, Canada and Chile, these elements are not found in any country.
  3. Rare earth metals are essential for the manufacture of various kinds of electronic items and there is a growing demand for these elements.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3
Correct answer: (c) 1 and 3 only. REEs are widely distributed (the "rare" misnomer - India, USA, Brazil, Myanmar etc. have them), so statement 2 is wrong; Chinese dominance and export curbs plus rising electronics demand cause supply concern. Verified question and answer: PMF IAS.

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014 - General Studies Paper I

With reference to two non-conventional energy sources called 'coalbed methane' and 'shale gas', consider the following statements:

  1. Coalbed methane is the pure methane gas extracted from coal seams, while shale gas is a mixture of propane and butane only that can be extracted from fine-grained sedimentary rocks.
  2. In India, abundant coalbed methane sources exist, but so far no shale gas sources have been found.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Correct answer: (d) Neither 1 nor 2. Shale gas is mainly methane trapped in shale (not just propane and butane), and India has identified six potentially shale-bearing basins. Links to section 7.3. Verified question and answer: Testbook.

3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2016 - General Studies Paper I

In which of the following regions of India are shale gas resources found?

  1. Cambay Basin
  2. Cauvery Basin
  3. Krishna-Godavari Basin

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 3 only
  3. 2 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3
Correct answer: (d) 1, 2 and 3. All three are among the six basins identified by MoPNG (with Assam-Arakan, Gondwana and Indo-Gangetic). Verified question and answer: Testbook.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - General Studies Paper I

"Petroleum refineries are not necessarily located nearer to crude oil producing areas, particularly in many of the developing countries. Explain its implications."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: Contrast raw-material-oriented location (e.g. Gulf Coast refineries near US oilfields) with market- and port-oriented refineries in importing countries (Jamnagar, coastal Indian refineries). "Discuss the geophysical characteristics of Circum-Pacific Zone."

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2022 - General Studies Paper I

"What are the forces that influence ocean currents? Describe their role in fishing industry of the world."

Fishing role - mixing of cold and warm currents and upwelling bring nutrients; examples: Grand Banks (Labrador Current + Gulf Stream), Dogger Bank, Japan (Kuroshio-Oyashio), Peru (upwelling). Mention over-fishing (1992 cod moratorium). Use section 5.4. Verified question/year: WRAP Exams.
