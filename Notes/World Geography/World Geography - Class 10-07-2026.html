10. Iron and Steel Industry (North America)

10.1 Importance of the Iron and Steel Industry

  • The iron and steel industry is a fundamental (basic) and strategically important industry. It supplies steel, which is used as a raw material in infrastructure projects and in many industries - defence, automobiles, medical diagnostics, surgical implants, electronics, machinery manufacturing, etc.
  • Because so many other industries depend on it, it is often called a "mother industry" or key industry [Additional].
  • The rate of growth of a country's economy is closely linked to the rate of growth of its steel industry.
  • Currently China produces about 55% of the world's steel; in the 1950s, the USA contributed more than 45%.

10.2 Technologies for Producing Steel

Steel is iron with a small, controlled amount of carbon (generally below 2%) and other alloying elements. The rough notes list three technologies:

  1. Blast furnace: iron ore + coke (coking coal / charcoal) + limestone are subjected to temperatures of more than 1,200°C in the presence of oxygen; produces iron (Fe + CO2) and slag. Slag is used in fertiliser and cement industries. Highly polluting process.
  2. Electric arc furnace (EAF): scrap metal + sponge iron, melted using electricity → steel.
  3. DRI (Direct Reduced Iron).

[Correction] A blast furnace produces molten pig iron ("hot metal", about 4% carbon), not steel. Coke burns with the hot air blast (often oxygen-enriched, not pure oxygen) to form carbon monoxide, which reduces the iron oxide (Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2); the hottest zone near the tuyeres reaches about 2,000°C. Limestone is a flux: it combines with silica and other impurities to form slag. The hot metal is then converted to steel in a basic oxygen furnace (BOF), where pure oxygen is blown in to burn off excess carbon - hence the BF-BOF route. Charcoal replaces coke only in some places (notably Brazil - see correction c31). Slag uses are correct: cement (GGBS, slag cement), road construction and improvement of agricultural soil (see PYQ 1).

[Correction] DRI is an iron-making step, not a separate steelmaking furnace. Iron ore is reduced to metallic iron without melting, using natural gas (shaft furnaces) or non-coking coal (rotary kilns); the porous product is called sponge iron. It must still be melted in an EAF or induction furnace to make steel - so the real routes are BF-BOF, DRI-EAF and scrap-EAF. The EAF in the notes ("scrap + sponge iron, using electricity") is correct. India is the world's largest DRI producer: 58.9 Mt in 2025, about 38% of world DRI (153.0 Mt); Iran is second (37.2 Mt) (worldsteel, World Steel in Figures 2026).

10.3 Steel and Carbon Emissions

  • The steel industry is one of the major emitters of greenhouse gases / CO2.
  • Blast-furnace-based steel mills use huge amounts of coal in the metallurgical process, so they have a high carbon footprint.
  • To cut carbon emissions, the steel industry is shifting from traditional blast-furnace technology to DRI and electric arc furnace technologies, which are less carbon-intensive.

[Additional] worldsteel estimates the industry's emissions at about 4.1 Gt CO2e in 2024, or 7-8% of global anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions, an average of 2.18 t CO2e per tonne of steel (worldsteel climate policy paper, source). Decarbonisation levers: more scrap recycling in EAFs; gas-based DRI, moving towards green-hydrogen DRI (H2 replaces carbon as the reducing agent, giving water vapour instead of CO2 - see PYQ 2); renewable electricity for EAFs; carbon capture, utilisation and storage (CCUS) for blast furnaces; energy efficiency and biomass (charcoal) substitution.

[Correction] DRI is not automatically low-carbon. Gas-based DRI emits much less than a blast furnace, and hydrogen-based DRI can approach near-zero, but coal-based DRI - which dominates India's sponge-iron sector - has a high carbon footprint. Likewise, an EAF is only as clean as its electricity: an EAF on a coal-heavy grid still has significant indirect emissions.

[Additional] India link: the Ministry of Steel released India's Green Steel Taxonomy on 12 December 2024: steel is "green" if emissions are below 2.2 t CO2e per tonne of finished steel, with star ratings - 5-star below 1.6, 4-star 1.6-2.0, 3-star 2.0-2.2; thresholds are to be reviewed every three years, and the National Institute of Secondary Steel Technology (NISST) is the nodal agency for measurement and certification (PIB). The National Green Hydrogen Mission (2023) supports hydrogen use in steel. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) covers iron and steel imports, which matters for Indian steel exporters to Europe. 10.4 Integrated Steel Plants vs Mini Steel Plants

  • Every country producing steel has integrated steel plants (blast furnace) and mini steel plants (arc furnace).
  • Integrated plants are located near coal mines / iron ore mines.
  • Mini steel plants use electricity and scrap iron → a decentralised industry, located near cities; they produce speciality / area-specific steel.
  • In the USA, 71% of steel comes from mini steel plants and 29% from integrated plants.

10.5 Steel Industry in North America

The steel industry of North America has two segments: (1) large integrated plants and (2) mini steel plants. Integrated plants are concentrated mainly in two regions (see Map 1):

  1. Appalachian region - (a) Pittsburgh (Pennsylvania); (b) the Alabama region (Birmingham).
  2. Great Lakes region - Chicago (with Gary), Cleveland.

[Correction] Spellings: "Pitts berg (pensivenia)" = Pittsburgh (Pennsylvania); "Applachean" = Appalachian; "cleaveland" = Cleveland; "Detrait" = Detroit. The Alabama centre is Birmingham, unusual because iron ore, coal and limestone were all found nearby. Other Great Lakes centres: Gary and Burns Harbor (Indiana, on Lake Michigan), Detroit, Buffalo (historic) and Hamilton (Ontario, Canada's main steel city). The Lake Superior ore comes mainly from the Mesabi Range (Minnesota): its rich hematite ore was largely exhausted by the mid-20th century, and the industry now mines low-grade taconite, concentrated into pellets. Canada also draws ore from the Labrador Trough (Quebec-Labrador). Several historic integrated works (Pittsburgh's riverside mills, Buffalo, Baltimore's Sparrows Point) have closed.

Advantages enjoyed by the steel plants of North America:

  1. Raw material availability - coal from the Appalachians, iron ore from the Lake Superior region.
  2. Easy access to markets (domestic and international):
    • Detroit - automobiles; Chicago - machinery;
    • Atlantic coast - New York - electronics, aviation, etc.; steel can also be exported to European markets;
    • Boston, Seattle - shipbuilding.
  3. Transport - water transport (Great Lakes - St. Lawrence) and rail connecting markets and raw materials.
  4. Technology, capital, skilled workers, world-class infrastructure and multiple sources of power (coal, hydroelectricity).

[Additional] The Great Lakes give a cheap bulk-water route: ore boats carry pellets from Duluth / Two Harbors through the Soo Locks (St. Marys River) to Lake Michigan and Lake Erie ports, and coal moves by rail the other way - so plants grew at the meeting point of ore and coal (a break-of-bulk location). The St. Lawrence Seaway (1959) links the lakes to the Atlantic.

[Correction] Boston and Seattle are historic shipbuilding centres (Boston Navy Yard and Quincy's Fore River yard have closed; the Puget Sound Naval Shipyard is at Bremerton near Seattle). The main US shipyards today are Newport News (Virginia - aircraft carriers), Bath (Maine), Groton (Connecticut - submarines), Pascagoula (Mississippi), plus Philadelphia and San Diego (see Map 1).

10.6 Challenges of the US Steel Industry

  1. High cost of labour.
  2. Rising environmental consciousness (stricter emission and pollution norms).
  3. Depleting resources.
  4. Stagnant market.

A shift towards new technologies like robotics and AI could rejuvenate the steel industry.

[Correction] "Depleting resources" applies to high-grade ore: the rich Mesabi hematite ran out, but large taconite reserves remain, at a higher processing cost. Two major challenges are missing from the notes: import competition and global overcapacity (especially from China, Japan, South Korea and others), and ageing integrated plants, which lost ground to cheaper mini-mills - the decline of the "Rust Belt" (Pittsburgh, Youngstown, Buffalo) since the 1970s-80s. Production has shifted south, to mini-mills in the Southern States (Arkansas, Alabama, Texas, the Carolinas), with cheaper land, power and labour, and less unionisation.

[Additional] Recent policy (time-sensitive): a presidential proclamation of 3 June 2025 raised US Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminium imports from 25% to 50%, effective 4 June 2025, with the UK kept at 25% (White House). Nippon Steel completed its acquisition of U.S. Steel on 18 June 2025 under a National Security Agreement that gives the US Government a "golden share" (consent rights over the company's name, headquarters, plant closures and moving production abroad) and commits about US$11 billion of investment by 2028 (U.S. Steel). India link: US tariffs and EU safeguard/CBAM measures redirect global steel flows, raising the risk of cheap imports into India; India has used safeguard duties to protect domestic producers.

South America

1. Location and Extent

  • South America extends from the Panama Canal in the north to the Drake Passage in the south (between South America and Antarctica).
  • The Equator passes through the northern part of the continent and the Tropic of Capricorn through the southern part.
    • Equator: Ecuador, Colombia, Brazil.
    • Tropic of Capricorn: Chile, Argentina, Paraguay, Brazil.
  • West: Pacific Ocean; east: Atlantic Ocean; north (slightly east): Caribbean Sea.
  • Lies in the Southern and Western Hemispheres.

[Correction] South America begins at the Colombia-Panama border in the Darien region; Panama and the Panama Canal belong to Central America (North America). The Isthmus of Panama joins the two continents. Its northernmost point is Punta Gallinas (Colombia, about 12°N) and its southernmost is Cape Horn (Hornos Island, Chile, about 56°S); the Drake Passage separates Cape Horn from the Antarctic Peninsula. See Map 2.

[Correction] South America lies entirely in the Western Hemisphere but mostly in the Southern Hemisphere: Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, most of Colombia, and the northern parts of Ecuador and Brazil lie north of the Equator. The Caribbean Sea lies to the north (off Colombia and Venezuela), while the Atlantic borders the north-east and east.

[Additional] Brazil is the only country crossed by both the Equator and the Tropic of Capricorn. "Ecuador" is Spanish for "equator". The Prime Meridian does not cross South America - the whole continent lies roughly between 35°W and 81°W.

2.1 Western Cordillera (Andes)

  • The Western Cordillera is known as the Andes Mountains.
  • Uplifted due to the collision between the Nazca (oceanic) plate (Pacific Ocean) and the South American plate.
  • The Andes are the longest terrestrial (continental) mountain range in the world - approximately 8,000 km long.
  • Spread across 7 countries: Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina.

[Correction] The Andes formed by subduction (ocean-continent convergence), not a continent-continent "collision": the denser Nazca Plate sinks beneath the South American Plate along the Peru-Chile (Atacama) Trench, which explains the chain of volcanoes and frequent earthquakes (part of the Pacific Ring of Fire); the Antarctic Plate subducts in the far south. Britannica gives the length as about 8,900 km; many textbooks quote about 7,000 km - the figure depends on where the ends are drawn, so "about 7,000-8,900 km" is safest. The mid-ocean ridge system is far longer, which is why the Andes are called the longest continental range. See Figure 2 and PYQ 5.

Northern Andes - Petroleum

  • The Northern Andes region is rich in petroleum.
  • The world's largest proven oil reserves are in Venezuela (303 billion barrels).
  • Venezuelan oil is "sour oil" - high density and a high concentration of acidic compounds (hydrogen sulphide, naphthenic acid).
  • Petroleum reserves are concentrated in the Lake Maracaibo region and the Orinoco region.

[Correction] The notes merge three different crude properties. Heavy = high density (low API gravity); sour = high sulphur content (including H2S); high-TAN (acidic) = high naphthenic-acid content. Much Venezuelan crude, especially from the Orinoco Belt, is extra-heavy, sour and acidic, so it needs diluents and complex refineries with cokers. Location: the Maracaibo Basin lies between two Andean ranges (Sierra de Perija and the Merida Andes), but the Orinoco Belt lies in the Orinoco lowlands (Llanos) of eastern Venezuela, not in the Andes (see Map 3). The reserve figure is correct: about 303 billion barrels, roughly 17% of the world total, the largest of any country (US EIA, as of 2023), but production is small - about 742,000 barrels per day in 2023 (EIA), far below its 1990s level, because of under-investment and sanctions.

[Additional] Other Andean-margin oil: Colombia (Llanos basin), Ecuador (Oriente basin, Amazon side), Peru. Off the Guiana coast, Guyana has become a fast-growing producer from the offshore Stabroek block (production began in 2019). Venezuela claims Guyana's Essequibo region; the case is before the International Court of Justice [Verification Required] - check the current status. India link: Indian complex refineries (e.g. Jamnagar) can process heavy, sour crude, and ONGC Videsh holds stakes in Venezuelan projects; actual imports depend on US sanctions policy [Verification Required].

Central Andes - Altiplano, Salt Pans and Critical Minerals

  • Southern Peru, Bolivia, northern Chile and Argentina. This region is rich in critical minerals.
  • The region is the Altiplano plateau - a dry area; the Atacama Desert is spread in this region - one of the highest and driest areas.
  • It has multiple palaeo-lakes that now look like salt pans.
  • Currently only one lake is present - Lake Titicaca - and another lake recently dried up, around 2016. Titicaca is spread across the Peru-Bolivia border.
  • The salt pans of this region hold reserves of critical minerals - lithium, copper, tin, zinc.

[Correction] The Altiplano is a high interior basin (about 3,400-3,900 m) of Bolivia and Peru, between the Western and Eastern Cordilleras, with no drainage outlet to the ocean; its southern continuation in Chile and Argentina is the Puna de Atacama (Atacama Plateau). The Atacama Desert is a separate, coastal desert on the Pacific side of the Western Cordillera in northern Chile - not on the Altiplano (Britannica; see Figure 2). Its extreme dryness comes from the cold Humboldt Current, the subtropical high-pressure belt and the rain shadow of the Andes. Spelling: "Atacamo" = Atacama.

[Correction] The lake that dried up is Lake Poopo (Bolivia's second-largest lake), which NASA reported had essentially dried up by January 2016 (it is shallow, fed mainly by the Desaguadero River from Titicaca, and was hit by drought, El Nino and water diversion). Titicaca is not the only lake left - Poopo refills partly in wet years, and the Altiplano has many smaller lakes. The salt flats such as Salar de Uyuni (about 10,582 km², Bolivia's largest) and Coipasa are remains of large palaeo-lakes. Lake Titicaca (about 3,810 m; Peru to the west, Bolivia to the east) is the highest lake navigable by large vessels (Britannica).

The Lithium Triangle and other critical minerals:

  • The Lithium Triangle - spread across Argentina, Bolivia and Chile - is the largest lithium reserve in the world, with around 60% of global reserves. Chile has the highest reserves; Australia is the top producer of lithium.
  • Chile accounts for more than 20% of global copper production (No. 1 producer).
  • Bolivia is a major producer of tin (China is No. 1). Peru is a major producer of zinc and lead.
  • The Atacama Desert has reserves of uranium.

[Correction] Per USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (2025 data): world lithium reserves are about 37 Mt, of which Chile has 9.2 Mt (largest) and Argentina 4.4 Mt; Bolivia has no reported reserves - so the Triangle holds roughly 37% of reserves, not 60%. The "more than half" idea refers to resources, a broader category: of about 150 Mt of measured and indicated resources, Argentina has 28 Mt, Bolivia 23 Mt and Chile 13 Mt - roughly 43%. Production (2025): Australia 92,000 t (No. 1, mainly from hard-rock spodumene), China 62,000 t, Chile 56,000 t, Zimbabwe 28,000 t, Argentina 23,000 t. In the Triangle, lithium comes from brines pumped from beneath salars and concentrated by solar evaporation, which uses a lot of water in a desert - a source of conflict with indigenous communities.

[Correction] Rankings checked against USGS (2025 data): copper - Chile 5.3 Mt of 23 Mt, about 23%, No. 1; the DR Congo is No. 2 and Peru No. 3. Tin - China, Indonesia, Peru (No. 3), Brazil, DR Congo, then Bolivia (No. 6, 15,000 t); Bolivia's tin (Potosi, Oruro, Huanuni) is historically famous but no longer among the top three. Zinc - Peru No. 2 after China. Lead - Peru No. 3 after China and Australia.

[Correction] The Atacama is not a notable uranium region - Chile does not produce uranium. Its famous resources are copper (Chuquicamata, Escondida), nitrates ("Chile saltpetre", the basis of the 1879-84 War of the Pacific), iodine (Chile supplies about two-thirds of world iodine - 23,000 of 34,000 t in 2025, USGS), lithium and boron.

Heights, Peaks and Volcanoes

  • The average height of the Andes is around 4,000 m.
  • Mount Aconcagua, the highest mountain in Argentina, is the highest peak of South America - around 6,000 m.
  • Mt. Chimborazo (Ecuador): its peak is the farthest point from the centre of the Earth.
  • Ojos del Salado (Chile-Argentina border) - the highest volcano in the world.
  • Mt. Cotopaxi (Ecuador) - the 2nd highest volcano in the world.

[Correction] Cotopaxi (5,911 m) is among the world's highest active volcanoes (it erupted in 2015) and is Ecuador's second-highest peak after Chimborazo, but it is not the world's second-highest volcano: Llullaillaco (about 6,739 m) on the Chile-Argentina border, and even Chimborazo (6,268 m, an inactive volcano), are higher (Britannica). Ojos del Salado (6,893 m) is the world's highest volcano (Britannica); it is often called the highest active volcano because of fumarole activity, though it has no recorded historical eruption [Verification Required] for "active" status. Aconcagua: 6,959 m (official Argentine figure; a 2001 GPS survey gave 6,962 m), in Mendoza province, Argentina, close to the Chilean border - the highest peak outside Asia and in both the Western and Southern Hemispheres. Spelling: "Ojos de salado" = Ojos del Salado.

Agriculture and Animal Rearing in the Andes

  • Mediterranean agriculture is practised in central Chile.
  • In other parts of the Andes, traditional subsistence agriculture is practised.
  • In the tropical Andes, farmers cultivate cereals (corn, quinoa), potato, tropical fruits and cocoa. Potato, papaya, corn and cocoa were sourced from this area to the world.
  • Gene pool centre: areas where wild relatives of domesticated plants and animals are found.
  • Meso-America - a biological hotspot.
  • In the central Andes (Altiplano), animal rearing is practised: vicuna, llama, alpaca - camelids.

[Correction] Crop origins: potato (Altiplano, around Lake Titicaca) and quinoa (Andes) are Andean. Maize (corn) was domesticated in southern Mexico about 10,000 years ago from Balsas teosinte; papaya probably originated in Mexico and Central America (Mesoamerica); cacao (cocoa) is native to the lowland forests of the Amazon and Orinoco basins (east of the Andes), not the high Andes (Britannica). Other South American gifts to the world: tomato (wild ancestors in the Andes), cassava, groundnut, pineapple, rubber, chillies. Spelling: "quineo" = quinoa.

[Correction] The concept in the notes is Vavilov's centres of origin (centres of crop diversity) - regions where crops were first domesticated and where their wild relatives and greatest genetic diversity are found; they matter for breeding and gene banks. A biodiversity hotspot is a different idea (Conservation International: very high endemism and severe habitat loss). Mesoamerica (southern Mexico and Central America) is both a Vavilov centre (maize, beans, papaya) and a hotspot, but it is in North America; South America's equivalents are the Andean (Peru-Ecuador-Bolivia) centre and the Tropical Andes hotspot, one of the richest on Earth. Camelids ("camelets"): llama and alpaca are domesticated; vicuna and guanaco are wild (vicuna wool is among the finest and costliest).

[Additional] India link: India lies in Vavilov's Hindustan (Indo-Burma) centre (rice, sugarcane, mango, pigeon pea, black pepper) and has four of the world's biodiversity hotspots: Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka and Sundaland (Nicobar Islands). Potato, maize, tomato, chillies, groundnut and cashew all reached India from the Americas through the Portuguese.

2.2 Guiana Highlands

  • Spread across Venezuela, parts of Colombia, Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana and northern Brazil.
  • Part of the Brazilian shield, so rich in minerals - gold and bauxite.
  • Ecologically divided into three parts: monsoon, Amazon (equatorial) and Llanos (savanna climate - tropical grasslands).
  • Drained by the Orinoco river system - famous for Angel Falls, the highest waterfall in the world.

[Correction] The Guiana Highlands stand on the Guiana Shield - the northern part of the ancient (Precambrian) Amazonian Craton, which Britannica also calls the Brazilian shield; the Amazon basin separates it from the Central Brazilian Shield. Its sandstone tepuis (table mountains, e.g. Roraima, Auyan-tepui) are famous. Political status: French Guiana is not a country - it is an overseas department and region of France and therefore part of the European Union (it hosts the European spaceport at Kourou). Minerals: gold (widely mined, often illegally, with mercury pollution), bauxite (Guyana, Suriname, Venezuela), iron ore (Venezuela's Cerro Bolivar), diamonds and manganese.

[Correction] "Monsoon" here means the tropical monsoon-type (wet, short dry season) climate of the Guiana coast, produced by the trade winds and the seasonal shift of the ITCZ - not a true seasonal wind reversal like the Asian monsoon. The Llanos ("llanas") are the tropical savanna plains of the Orinoco basin in Venezuela and Colombia, lying north-west of the highlands, between them and the Andes. Angel Falls (979 m) is the world's highest uninterrupted waterfall; it drops from Auyan-tepui on the Churun River, which flows to the Caroni and then the Orinoco; Venezuela has renamed it Kerepakupai Meru (Britannica). The Caroni also feeds the large Guri hydroelectric dam.

Agriculture in the three zones:

  • Amazon (equatorial) part: slash-and-burn agriculture ("milpa") - subsistence agriculture of starchy food crops; sugarcane.
  • Monsoonal part: sugarcane (plantation).
  • Llanos: animal ranching, soybean and corn. Ranching along with corn and soybean is expanding in the Llanos because of rising demand for meat and edible oil. This change is attributed to (1) a decline in animal ranching in North America and (2) an increase in demand for meat in Asian markets. The Llanos are witnessing degradation of grasslands.

[Correction] Milpa is the name for shifting cultivation in Mexico and Central America. NCERT (Fundamentals of Human Geography, "Primary Activities") lists the regional names: Milpa (Mexico and Central America), Conuco (Venezuela), Roca (Brazil), Masole (Central Africa), Ladang (Indonesia), Ray (Vietnam) and Jhuming (north-east India). The starchy crops are cassava (manioc), yams and sweet potato. The coastal sugar plantations of Guyana ("Demerara" sugar) and Suriname were worked by Indian indentured labourers after slavery ended - the origin of the large Indian diaspora there.

[Correction] The main driver of ranching and soybean expansion in South America's tropical savannas (Llanos, Cerrado, Chaco) is export demand, above all from China - the world's largest importer of soybeans (for pig and poultry feed) and a major beef importer - together with cheap land, new roads and agribusiness investment. A shrinking US cattle herd is at most a secondary factor [Verification Required] for its role. Consequences: conversion of native grassland and gallery forest, loss of wetlands (esteros), soil erosion and biodiversity loss.

2.3 Brazilian Highlands

Minerals and Petroleum

  • Part of the Brazilian shield, rich in minerals: niobium, rare-earth metals, iron, manganese, bauxite, gold, tantalum, coltan.
  • Brazil is No. 1 in niobium, No. 2 in tantalum and among the top 2 in iron ore.
  • Found near the eastern coast in the Minas Gerais region, the Mato Grosso region and the Amazonia region.
  • Recently discovered huge petroleum reserves in the Santos Basin (offshore).

[Correction] USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 (2025 data): niobium - Brazil 104,000 of 112,000 t, about 93%, No. 1 (mainly from Araxa, Minas Gerais); tantalum - Brazil is No. 4 (after DR Congo, Rwanda and Nigeria), not No. 2; iron ore - No. 2 (420 Mt) after Australia (980 Mt); bauxite - No. 4; manganese - No. 5. Rare earths - Brazil has the second-largest reserves after China (21 Mt in MCS 2025) [Verification Required] for the 2026 update. Coltan is not a separate metal: it is columbite-tantalite ore, the source of niobium and tantalum. Places: Minas Gerais ("Menas Gerais") - the Iron Quadrangle (Quadrilatero Ferrifero) near Belo Horizonte; Carajas in Para (Amazonia) - one of the world's largest iron-ore mines; Urucum near Corumba in Mato Grosso do Sul ("Moto grosso") - iron and manganese. See Map 3.

[Additional] The pre-salt oil of the Santos and Campos basins (off Sao Paulo and Rio de Janeiro) lies in very deep water beneath a thick layer of salt; the first big discovery (Tupi, now the Lula field) was made in 2006-07, and pre-salt fields now drive Brazil's oil output, making Brazil Latin America's largest oil producer [Verification Required] for current production figures. India link: Brazil supplies crude oil to Indian refiners, and ONGC Videsh has had exploration interests offshore Brazil.

Rivers and Hydroelectricity

  • The Brazilian Highlands are drained by three river systems: (1) the Amazon (northern part), (2) the Sao Francisco (inner part) and (3) the Parana (southern part).
  • Rivers of this region have high hydroelectricity potential - Brazil has huge hydropower potential.
  • Hydropower projects are also a threat to the Amazon forest.

[Additional] Hydropower supplied about 57% of Brazil's electricity in 2024 (433.4 of 762.9 TWh including imports from Itaipu; 55.3% from domestic plants) - EPE, Balanco Energetico Nacional 2025. Key dams: Itaipu (Parana River, a Brazil-Paraguay binational project), Belo Monte (Xingu, Amazon), Tucurui (Tocantins). The Sao Francisco flows north through the dry interior before turning east to the Atlantic - a "river of national unity" used for irrigation and power (Sobradinho, Paulo Afonso). Problems with Amazon dams: flooding of forest, displacement of indigenous communities, methane from rotting vegetation in reservoirs, blocked fish migration, and vulnerability to drought.

Tropical Grasslands of Brazil - Cerrado

  • The interior of the Brazilian Highlands has a tropical grass cover called the Cerrado or Campos.
  • These grasslands are witnessing degradation due to the expansion of animal ranching, corn and soybean cultivation.
  • From the Cerrado, these activities are now expanding towards the Amazon forest.

[Correction] The Cerrado ("cerado") is a tropical savanna - a mosaic of grassland, scrub and dry woodland - covering about 2 million km², roughly one-fourth of Brazil (Britannica), mainly on the central plateau. "Campos" means open grassland: campo limpo is the most open type within the Cerrado, while the Campos Sulinos are the temperate grasslands of southern Brazil (part of the Pampa). So Cerrado and Campos are related but not synonyms. The Cerrado is the "cradle of waters", feeding the Sao Francisco, Tocantins-Araguaia and Parana basins, and it is now Brazil's main soybean, corn and cotton frontier (states of Mato Grosso, Goias, Bahia and the "MATOPIBA" region).

Threats to the Amazon Forest

  1. Agriculture (slash and burn).
  2. Soybean and animal ranching, along with plantation agriculture (oil palm and sugarcane).
  3. Mining of critical metals.
  4. Infrastructure projects like hydroelectric projects and transport corridors.
  5. Climate change and El Nino.

[Additional] Also: illegal logging; forest fires set to clear land; illegal gold mining (garimpo) with mercury poisoning of rivers and indigenous people (e.g. Yanomami lands); land grabbing. Most clearing is along the "arc of deforestation" on the southern and eastern edge, where roads (e.g. BR-163, the Trans-Amazonian Highway) meet the Cerrado frontier. Scientists warn of a tipping point at which parts of the rainforest could turn into degraded savanna, as deforestation reduces the moisture the forest recycles. Responses: the Amazon Soy Moratorium (2006), protected areas and indigenous territories, satellite monitoring by Brazil's INPE (PRODES, DETER), the Amazon Fund, and the Amazon Cooperation Treaty Organization (ACTO - Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Peru, Suriname, Venezuela).

Agriculture of Eastern and North-Eastern Brazil

  • Eastern and north-eastern Brazil are famous for the cultivation of coffee, sugarcane, cotton, tobacco and cashew nuts.
  • Brazil is the top producer of coffee, sugarcane and soybean in the world.
  • In Brazil, sugarcane is mainly used for ethanol production.
  • Equatorial climate.
  • Soybean products are exported to Indian markets.
  • India imports sunflower oil from Ukraine and Russia, soybean oil from the USA and Brazil, and palm oil from East Asia.

[Correction] North-eastern Brazil does not have an equatorial climate: the north-east coast (Zona da Mata) has a tropical wet-and-dry climate - the historic sugarcane plantation belt (Pernambuco, Alagoas) - while the interior (Sertao) is semi-arid, with thorny Caatinga vegetation and recurrent droughts (the "drought polygon"). The equatorial climate belongs to Amazonia. Crop regions are wider than "east and north-east": coffee is grown mainly in the south-east (Minas Gerais, Sao Paulo, Espirito Santo); sugarcane mainly in the centre-south (Sao Paulo state leads) and the north-east coast; soybean, corn and cotton in the Cerrado (Mato Grosso, Goias, Bahia); tobacco in the south (Rio Grande do Sul); cashew - native to north-east Brazil - in Ceara and neighbouring states.

[Correction] Rankings (time-sensitive): Brazil is the world's largest producer of coffee (63 million bags, about 35% of world output, 2025/26) and soybeans (180.5 Mt, about 42%, 2025/26) - USDA FAS; it is also the largest producer of sugarcane [Verification Required] for the latest share (FAOSTAT). Sugarcane is not "mainly" for ethanol: mills switch between sugar and ethanol depending on prices; in the 2025/26 season CONAB estimated 673.2 Mt of cane yielding 44.2 Mt of sugar and 27.33 billion litres of cane ethanol, with a larger share going to sugar than the year before. Brazil is the world's leading sugar exporter, and most of its cars are flex-fuel, able to run on any ethanol-petrol blend.

[Correction] India's edible-oil import sources: palm oil from Indonesia and Malaysia (South-East Asia, not East Asia); soybean oil mainly from Argentina and Brazil (the USA is a minor supplier); sunflower oil from Ukraine and Russia. PIB (2021) put the import mix at about 54% palm, 25% soybean and 19% sunflower oil; India imports roughly 55-60% of its edible-oil needs. Responses: the National Mission on Edible Oils - Oil Palm (2021) and NMEO-Oilseeds (2024). Shares shift year to year [Verification Required] for the latest import mix.

[Additional] India link: India and Brazil were founding members of the Global Biofuels Alliance launched during India's G20 presidency (2023). Brazil's flex-fuel and sugarcane-ethanol experience is a model for India's E20 (20% ethanol blending) programme. India and Brazil are the world's two largest sugar producers and rivals in WTO sugar-subsidy disputes.

Industrial South-East Brazil

  • The eastern coastal part of Brazil is the most industrialised part of South America - Rio de Janeiro, Sao Paulo, Minas Gerais.
  • Industries: steel, automobiles, aircraft manufacturing (Embraer), agricultural machinery, etc.
  • Minas Gerais has iron ore; charcoal from the Amazon.
  • Advantages: nearness to raw material, coastal location with access to the global market, and a domestic market for industry.
  • Challenges: import dependence for coal; environmental issues; forest conservation.

[Correction] Brazil's coking coal is imported (its own coal is poor), which is why charcoal has long been used as a reductant, especially by independent pig-iron producers in Minas Gerais and the Carajas corridor. Today most legal charcoal comes from planted eucalyptus forests; charcoal made from native Cerrado and Amazon forest has been a major illegal-deforestation and forced-labour problem - so "charcoal from the Amazon" describes the problem, not the norm. Integrated steel plants: CSN at Volta Redonda (1946, Brazil's first), Usiminas (Ipatinga), ArcelorMittal Tubarao (Vitoria); Brazil is Latin America's largest steel producer. Embraer is based at Sao Jose dos Campos (Sao Paulo state). Spellings: "Rio de Jenerio" = Rio de Janeiro; "Menes Gerais / Menas geras" = Minas Gerais.

2.4 [Additional] Amazon Basin

The class list names the Amazon Basin as a separate division; the rough notes cover it under the Brazilian Highlands (threats to the Amazon) and the Guiana Highlands (equatorial zone). A brief summary is added here; the rough notes' "4. Parana Basin" is therefore numbered 2.5 below.

  • A vast lowland between the Andes (west), the Guiana Highlands (north) and the Brazilian Highlands (south), drained by the Amazon - the world's largest river by discharge and drainage area. Its farthest sources rise in the Peruvian Andes (Ucayali-Apurimac and Maranon).
  • Equatorial climate (hot, humid, rain throughout the year) and the world's largest tropical rainforest (selva), extending into nine countries and territories (Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Bolivia, Ecuador, Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname and French Guiana).
  • At Manaus, the dark Rio Negro meets the muddy Solimoes (the upper Amazon) - the "Meeting of the Waters". Seasonal floods create varzea (flooded forest on fertile silt).
  • Traditional economy: shifting cultivation, rubber tapping, Brazil-nut gathering, fishing; now ranching, soy, mining and dams on the margins (see threats).

2.5 Parana-Plata Basin, Pampas, Pantanal and Patagonia

Parana Basin and the Rio de la Plata

  • Lies south of the Brazilian Highlands and east of the Andes.
  • Spread across Argentina, Uruguay, southern Brazil, Paraguay (landlocked) and parts of Bolivia.
  • Drained by the Parana river, also known as the La Plata (at the mouth); it has two tributaries - the Paraguay and the Uruguay.
  • The second most industrial region of South America. The La Plata has two capitals on its banks: Montevideo and Buenos Aires.
  • Most industries are agro-processing / agri-based industries.

[Correction] The Rio de la Plata is not another name for the Parana: it is the wide estuary formed where the Parana and the Uruguay rivers meet. The Paraguay is a tributary of the Parana (joining near Corrientes), but the Uruguay is not - it flows separately into the Plata. Together they form the La Plata basin, the second-largest river basin in South America after the Amazon. Buenos Aires (Argentina) lies on the southern shore and Montevideo (Uruguay) on the northern shore. Paraguay and Bolivia are South America's two landlocked countries; the Parana-Paraguay waterway (Hidrovia) gives them river access to the Atlantic. Spellings: "Parageua / Paraguya" = Paraguay; "Buenos aries" = Buenos Aires.

Pampas

  • The La Plata region is surrounded by temperate grasslands called the Pampas, spread across Argentina, Uruguay and southern Brazil.
  • The Pampas have chernozem soil, famous for commercial grain farming (wheat, corn and soybean).
  • The drier margins of the Pampas have animal ranching.

Upper Paraguay - Pantanal

  • The Pantanal wetland, the world's largest wetland, is present in the upper Parana basin. It is spread across three countries - Brazil (two-thirds), Paraguay and Bolivia.
  • This wetland is shrinking due to the spread of animal ranching and soybean from the Cerrado.

[Correction] The Pantanal lies along the upper Paraguay River (a sub-basin of the La Plata system), not the upper Parana itself. Britannica calls it one of the world's largest freshwater wetlands (about 140,000-210,000 km²), and it is often described as the largest tropical wetland; "world's largest wetland" overall is disputed, as some boreal peatlands are larger. It lies mainly in Brazil (Mato Grosso and Mato Grosso do Sul), extending into Bolivia and Paraguay; the commonly quoted Brazilian share is about 80%, more than two-thirds [Verification Required]. Part is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (Pantanal Conservation Area) and Ramsar sites protect parts of it. Other threats: large fires in drought years, upstream dams and waterway (Hidrovia) dredging, and silt from Cerrado farms.

Gran Chaco

  • The Gran Chaco is a savanna region.

[Additional] The Gran Chaco is a hot, semi-arid lowland of dry thorn forest, scrub and savanna in northern Argentina, western Paraguay and south-eastern Bolivia, between the Andes and the Paraguay-Parana rivers. It is known for quebracho trees (a source of tannin) and is now one of the world's fastest-deforesting dry forests, cleared for soybean and cattle.

Patagonia

  • The Patagonian Desert lies south of the Pampas, in Argentina and Chile. Because of the dry climate, sheep rearing is practised in this region.
  • Patagonia is rich in petroleum.
  • Tierra del Fuego Island is the southern extension of Patagonia.

[Correction] The Patagonian Desert (steppe) is on the Argentine side, east of the Andes: it is a rain-shadow desert, because the westerlies drop their moisture on the Chilean side. Chilean Patagonia, west of the Andes, is very wet - fjords, temperate rainforest and ice fields. Patagonia is the largest desert in the Americas and one of the few large deserts in the mid-latitudes on the eastern side of a continent. Oil and gas: the Neuquen basin with the Vaca Muerta shale formation in northern Patagonia, plus the Golfo San Jorge and Austral basins. In the US EIA's 2013 world shale assessment, Argentina had 801.5 trillion cubic feet of technically recoverable shale gas (2nd after China) and 27 billion barrels of tight oil (4th). Spelling: "Petagonia" = Patagonia.

[Correction] Tierra del Fuego ("Teradelfugo") is an archipelago, not a single island; its main island (Isla Grande) is separated from the mainland by the Strait of Magellan and is shared by Chile (west) and Argentina (east). Ushuaia (Argentina) is one of the world's southernmost cities and a gateway to Antarctica; Cape Horn lies on Hornos Island further south (Chile).

2.6 Islands

Falkland Islands

  • The Falkland Islands are disputed between Argentina and the UK. They have petroleum reserves.

[Additional] The Falklands (Spanish: Islas Malvinas) are a self-governing British Overseas Territory about 500 km east of Patagonia, claimed by Argentina; Argentina invaded on 2 April 1982 and surrendered about ten weeks later (Falklands War) (Britannica). Argentina also claims South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands. Oil (time-sensitive): the Sea Lion field in the North Falkland Basin (discovered 2010) has been sanctioned for development, with first oil targeted for the first quarter of 2028 (Rockhopper Exploration, August 2026); in October 2026 the company reported Argentine sanctions action and the withdrawal of two contractors [Verification Required] - the project timeline may change.

Galapagos Islands

  • Located on the Equator; they belong to Ecuador and are famous for Darwin's finches.

[Additional] The Galapagos lie about 1,000 km off the coast of Ecuador and were the first site inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List (1978) (UNESCO). They are volcanic hotspot islands on the Nazca Plate, at the meeting of several ocean currents (cold Humboldt, warm Panama Current, Cromwell undercurrent), which explains their unusual mix of species - giant tortoises, marine iguanas, Galapagos penguins (on the Equator) and Darwin's finches. Darwin's 1835 visit helped shape his theory of evolution by natural selection.

[Additional] Other islands: Trinidad and Tobago (an island country on South America's continental shelf, rich in oil and gas; large Indian-origin population); Juan Fernandez Islands and Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in the Pacific - both Chilean; Chiloe (Chile); Marajo (Brazil), a large river island at the mouth of the Amazon; and the Dutch Caribbean islands of Aruba, Curacao and Bonaire off Venezuela.

Africa

1. Introduction

  • The African continent is considered the origin of humans - humans evolved in Africa.

[Additional] Fossil and genetic evidence shows that Homo sapiens evolved in Africa about 300,000 years ago (Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History, Human Origins Program); earlier hominins also lived only in Africa for millions of years - e.g. "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis, Afar region of Ethiopia), and the stone-tool sites of Olduvai Gorge (Tanzania) and the Cradle of Humankind (South Africa, a UNESCO World Heritage Site). Modern humans later spread from Africa to the rest of the world ("Out of Africa" dispersal). The East African Rift Valley, where sediments and volcanic ash layers preserve and date fossils, is the richest source of early human remains.

[Additional] Location facts: Africa is the second-largest and second most populous continent. It is the only continent crossed by the Equator, both Tropics and the Prime Meridian; the Equator and Prime Meridian meet in the Gulf of Guinea (see Map 5).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • A blast furnace makes iron, not steel; the BOF or EAF makes steel. DRI is iron, not steel.
  • DRI is not always low-carbon - coal-based DRI (common in India) is carbon-intensive.
  • China's steel share is ~52% (2025), not 55%; India is No. 2, not Japan.
  • The Andes formed by subduction, not continental collision (unlike the Himalaya).
  • Panama is in North America; South America starts at the Colombian border.
  • The Atacama is a coastal desert, not on the Altiplano; Patagonia's desert is on the Argentine side.
  • The lake that dried up was Poopo, not Titicaca.
  • Australia produces the most lithium; Chile has the most reserves; Bolivia has huge resources but no reported reserves.
  • Cotopaxi is not the 2nd highest volcano; Llullaillaco is higher. Aconcagua is not a volcano.
  • Maize and papaya are Mesoamerican; potato and quinoa are Andean.
  • Milpa is the Mexican / Central American name; Brazil = Roca, Venezuela = Conuco.
  • French Guiana is French, not an independent country.
  • Brazil is No. 4 in tantalum, not No. 2; coltan is an ore, not a metal.
  • NE Brazil is semi-arid or wet-dry tropical, not equatorial; coffee is a south-eastern crop.
  • Rio de la Plata is an estuary (Parana + Uruguay), not another name for the Parana.
  • Palm oil comes from South-East Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia); soybean oil mainly from Argentina and Brazil.
  • Cape Agulhas, not the Cape of Good Hope, is Africa's southernmost point.
  • The Suez Canal has no locks; the Panama Canal does.

7. UPSC CSE Mains 2021 - General Studies Paper I

"Despite India being one of the countries of the Gondwanaland, its mining industry contributes much less to its Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in percentage. Discuss."

8. UPSC CSE Mains 2021 - General Studies Paper I

"Briefly mention the alignment of major mountain ranges of the world and explain their impact on local weather conditions, with examples."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: North-south ranges on western margins (Rockies, Andes) and east-west ranges in Eurasia (Alps, Himalaya). Effects: orographic rain on windward slopes and rain shadow on the lee side - the Atacama (with the cold Humboldt Current) and the Patagonian desert east of the southern Andes; the Andes block easterly moisture, giving wet eastern slopes; the Himalaya block cold Siberian winds and trap the monsoon; local winds (Chinook, Foehn, Zonda in Argentina). Links to Figure 2, c14 and c34. Verified question/year: Anantam IAS (GS1 2021, Q14).
