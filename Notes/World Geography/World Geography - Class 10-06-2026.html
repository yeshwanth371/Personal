8. Agriculture in North America

8.1 Nature of Agriculture - Commercial Agriculture

In North America, agriculture is predominantly commercial - crops and livestock are produced mainly for sale in the market, not for the farm family's own consumption.

  • Commercial agriculture focuses on both quantity and quality of output, which has led to improved, technology-based farming.
  • It is capital-intensive - machinery, irrigation, fertilisers, seeds, storage and data systems need heavy investment.
  • Economies of scale:
    • Large land holdings - the average farm size in the USA is about 460 acres (see correction below).
    • Agricultural belts - farmers over a whole region cultivate the same crop (regional specialisation), e.g. the wheat belt and the Corn Belt.
    • Large-scale production lowers cost per unit and raises profits.
  • Institutional strength: farmers are organised in cooperatives - generally multi-purpose - that are engaged in the entire chain from production to selling. Cooperatives eliminate middlemen and connect farmers to the global supply chain.
  • Contract farming connects farmers directly with buyers and gives them an assured market. Buyers are agro-processing and agro-marketing firms, which also provide inputs to farmers. It makes prices stable and is designed to be a win-win arrangement.

[Correction] "In North America agriculture is commercial" holds for the USA and Canada, but not for the whole continent. North America also includes Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, where subsistence farming survives - e.g. the milpa system (maize, beans and squash grown together, often by slash-and-burn) in southern Mexico and the Central American highlands. See Map 1.

[Correction] Updated farm-size data (time-sensitive): USDA reports an average US farm size of 466 acres (about 189 ha) in 2024 - 1.88 million farms on 876 million acres (USDA ERS, source). Canada's average is larger: about 809 acres (153.7 million acres / 189,874 farms, 2021 Census of Agriculture, Statistics Canada). India's average operational holding was 1.08 ha in the Agriculture Census 2015-16; results of the 11th Agriculture Census (2021-22) were still awaited as of July 2026 (PIB). An "average" also hides concentration: in 2024, small family farms were 86% of US farms, while large-scale family farms (about 5% of farms) produced about 50% of the value of production (USDA ERS). Economies of scale lower cost per unit; higher profit is not automatic - it depends on prices, subsidies and input costs.

[Correction] Contract farming is potentially win-win, not automatically so. It is a pre-harvest agreement fixing price (or a pricing formula), quantity, quality and delivery. USDA distinguishes marketing contracts (farmer owns the crop and supplies inputs) and production contracts (the contractor owns the commodity, supplies inputs such as feed, chicks or piglets, and pays the farmer a fee). In 2024 about 26% of the value of US agricultural production was under contract; production contracts dominate poultry/eggs and hogs, while under 20% of corn, soybean and wheat was under marketing contracts (USDA ERS). Risks: buyer dominance (monopsony), rejection on quality grounds, delayed payment, unequal bargaining power and exclusion of the smallest farmers - hence the need for dispute-settlement and price safeguards.

[Additional] Examples of US farmer cooperatives: CHS Inc. (grain, inputs, energy), Land O'Lakes and Dairy Farmers of America (dairy), Ocean Spray (cranberries), Sunkist (citrus). USDA notes that many US dairy farmers belong to producer-owned cooperatives, some of which own their own processing plants (vertical integration). India link: the Anand pattern (Amul) dairy cooperatives, the Ministry of Cooperation (2021), computerisation of PACS, and the scheme for 10,000 Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) aim at the same economies of scale for small holders (see PYQ 2, "Small Farmer Large Field").

  • Traditional subsistence agriculture → first stage of modernisation: involved irrigation, fertilisers, pesticides and machinery, which raised scale and land productivity (yield per hectare).
  • The first stage neglected the environment and led to environmental degradation (soil erosion and salinity, groundwater depletion, nutrient run-off, pesticide residues).
  • Second stage of modernisation (the present, ongoing wave) focuses on efficiency - the productivity of all factors, defined as total output (economic as well as environmental) divided by total input.

[Correction] "Productivity of all factors" is Total Factor Productivity (TFP) = total output / total inputs, where inputs are land, labour, capital and intermediate inputs (fertiliser, pesticides, energy). The standard TFP measured by USDA counts only marketed farm output (crops, meat, dairy); including environmental outcomes (emissions, nutrient losses) gives an environmentally adjusted ("green") TFP, an extension rather than the standard definition. Also, the second stage is the TFP / sustainable-intensification wave; precision farming is its leading technology, not the name of the stage itself. Evidence: US farm output in 2023 was nearly three times its 1948 level (1.52% growth a year), while aggregate input use grew only 0.12% a year - so TFP growth (about 1.40% a year) explains most output growth (USDA ERS, updated August 2026).

Single-factor productivity: output / one input (e.g. yield = tonnes per hectare; output per worker).
Total Factor Productivity: TFP = total output / total inputs (land + labour + capital + materials, aggregated). TFP growth ≈ output growth − input growth.

Likely impact of an FTA on Indian farmers:

  • Threats: cheaper, often subsidised imports can depress domestic prices (dairy powder, corn, soybean/soy oil, apples, nuts); competition from large-scale, high-TFP producers; concerns over GM feed and food; cultural concerns over milk from animals fed animal-derived feed; loss of livelihoods where farming is the safety net.
  • Opportunities: wider markets for Indian rice, spices, marine products, fruits and processed food; cheaper animal-feed inputs (e.g. DDGS) for poultry and dairy; investment and technology in food processing and cold chains.

[Additional] Current example (time-sensitive): the US-India Joint Statement of 7 February 2026 announced a framework for an Interim Agreement under the Bilateral Trade Agreement (BTA) negotiations: India would eliminate or reduce tariffs on all US industrial goods and a range of food and farm products, including dried distillers' grains (DDGs), red sorghum for animal feed, tree nuts, fresh and processed fruit, soybean oil, wine and spirits, while the USA would apply an 18% reciprocal tariff on Indian goods and remove it on a list including generic pharmaceuticals, gems and diamonds and aircraft parts (PIB). Most listed farm items are feed inputs or products India produces little of; the Government of India has stated that sensitive farm and dairy sectors are protected. [Verification Required] - check whether the Interim Agreement has since been signed or implemented and its final tariff schedules before quoting.

Steps to overcome the risks:

  1. Negotiating safeguards: exclusion / sensitive lists (dairy, staple cereals, pulses), tariff-rate quotas, phased tariff cuts, bilateral safeguard clauses, strict rules of origin.
  2. Standards as shields and passports: science-based sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) rules, labelling of GM content; at the same time, meet export standards (residue limits, traceability).
  3. Build scale without displacing farmers: FPOs, cooperatives and "Small Farmer Large Field" style aggregation; contract farming with dispute-settlement safeguards; land leasing for consolidation.
  4. Raise TFP: precision farming, micro-irrigation, seeds, extension, mechanisation through custom-hiring centres, digital agriculture.
  5. Value addition and infrastructure: cold chains, warehousing, food parks, branding and GI tags to capture higher-value export markets.
  6. Risk cover: crop insurance (PMFBY), price-stabilisation tools, income support; protect policy space at the WTO (public stockholding).
  7. Crop diversification towards products where India is competitive (spices, fruits, vegetables, marine, organic produce).

There are six agricultural systems practised across North America (see Map 1).

1. Commercial Grain Farming

  • Practised over the Mississippi plain and the Great Plains. Major crops: wheat, corn and soybean.
  • Wheat is cultivated on chernozem / black (prairie) soil.
  • Features: cluster-based (belts), monocropping, advanced technology, strong institutions - cooperatives and contract farming - and flexible land leasing.
  • Land leasing → land consolidation, which also moves the surplus workforce from agriculture to industry.

[Correction] Chernozem is typical of the drier wheat belt; the Corn Belt has other fertile prairie soils (Mollisols) formed on glacial till and loess under a wetter climate. "Monocropping" should read regional monoculture / specialisation: the Corn Belt actually rotates corn with soybean (soybean, a legume, fixes nitrogen). In Canada, canola (22.3 million acres) was the largest crop by area in 2021, ahead of spring wheat (16.0 million acres) (Statistics Canada). Spelling: "Clustere" = cluster.

[Additional] Land leasing lets an efficient farmer operate more land without buying it, so holdings consolidate while owners keep title; labour released from farming moves to industry and services - the classic structural transformation. Corn and soybeans made up 43.7% of US crop cash receipts in 2025 (USDA ERS). For the belts themselves, see the notes of 5 October 2026, sections 4.1-4.2.

2. Animal (Livestock) Ranching

  • Also a commercial activity: animals are reared for meat, wool and leather.
  • Practised over grasslands. Earlier practised in temperate grasslands, it is now extending to tropical grasslands.
  • Has vast pastures; in some parts fodder (oats, barley, etc.) is cultivated. Before slaughter, animals are fed corn and soybean - this is called fattening.
  • Produces for the urban market. It is an extensive type of agriculture, and so is highly dependent on transportation.
  • Ranching in the USA benefited from railway lines for meat packaging and processing, which led to agricultural modernisation.

[Correction] Spelling: "meet" = meat; "slaugtering" = slaughtering. In North America the spread into tropical grasslands is seen mainly in the Gulf Coast, Florida and the Central American lowlands, where pasture was often created by clearing tropical forest for beef exports (the "hamburger connection" debate); the largest tropical ranching frontiers are in South America (Llanos, Brazilian Cerrado and Amazon margins). Fattening (finishing) in the USA takes place mostly in feedlots, concentrated on the Great Plains close to corn and feed supplies. The railway link: cattle were driven to railheads and shipped to Chicago's Union Stock Yards (opened 1865); refrigerated rail cars in the late 19th century allowed dressed meat to reach eastern cities - the origin of the industrial meat-packing industry.

[Additional] Cattle and calves were the largest animal-product receipts in the USA in 2025: $133.7 billion, 44% of animal and animal-product receipts (USDA ERS). NCERT uses the term livestock ranching / commercial livestock rearing: ranches are large, fenced and scientifically managed (breeding, veterinary care, rotational grazing), unlike nomadic herding. Similar regions: Pampas (Argentina), Australia, New Zealand, South Africa.

3. Mediterranean Agriculture

  • Practised in the Mediterranean climate - rainy in the winter season.
  • Five regions practise this type of agriculture: California, Central Chile, the Cape region of South Africa, South-West Australia and the Mediterranean coast (see Map 3).
  • It is a climate-based agriculture. Farmers cultivate citrus fruits, grapes (viticulture) and wheat - "bread and wine culture".
  • Commercial and intensive type of agriculture, located near urban markets.

[Correction] The Mediterranean climate has mild, rainy winters and hot, dry summers (rain from the westerlies in winter; subtropical high pressure in summer), on the western sides of continents between about 30° and 45° latitude. The five regions are the Mediterranean Basin (not just a coast), California, Central Chile, the Western Cape (South Africa) and south-western and southern Australia (Perth and Adelaide) - and of these, only California lies in North America. Viticulture means grape cultivation, not citrus; citrus cultivation is citriculture. "Bread and wine culture" refers to wheat and grapes - with olives, the classic "Mediterranean triad". Because summer is the dry growing season, the farming depends on irrigation. Spellings: "Mediterrarian / Mediterrarean" = Mediterranean.

[Additional] California's Central Valley (Sacramento and San Joaquin valleys) is irrigated by the federal Central Valley Project and the California State Water Project; Napa and Sonoma are famous for wine. Crops: grapes, citrus, almonds, walnuts, olives, tomatoes, lettuce. The Mediterranean region supplies citrus fruits in winter to colder markets, and its fruit is often dried or turned into wine and juice (NCERT). USDA ERS shows that California's irrigated area fell from 8.8 to 8.2 million acres between 1997 and 2022, reflecting drought, groundwater depletion and competing urban demand. Contrast: Florida's citrus grows in a humid subtropical climate, not a Mediterranean one.

4 and 5. Market Gardening and Dairy Farming

Market gardening:

  • Cultivation of fruits, vegetables and flowers to cater to the urban market.
  • Practised near cities; intensive in nature.

Dairy farming:

  • Milk and meat production.
  • In the USA, practised in the Great Lakes, Florida and California regions. The Great Lakes region is the biggest dairy belt in the world.
  • These regions are also famous for market gardening, and both activities are intensive.
  • Dairying requires a cool, moderate climate along with a market.
  • The best milch breeds are found here.
  • Global warming is one of the challenges for this kind of dairy.
  • Feed conversion ratio is high when compared to India (low).
  • The dairy sector is labour-intensive; in the USA and Europe mechanisation has been introduced, which reduces labour intensity.

[Correction] Dairy farming produces milk and milk products (butter, cheese, milk powder, yogurt); meat (culled cows, veal) is only a by-product - beef comes mainly from ranching and feedlots. The US dairy regions in the notes need updating: the top five milk-producing States in 2025 were California, Wisconsin, Idaho, Texas and New York, together over 55% of US milk (USDA ERS). Florida is not a major dairy region - it is known for citrus and winter vegetables (market gardening). The Great Lakes - New England - St. Lawrence dairy belt (Wisconsin is "America's Dairyland") is the traditional dairy belt of North America and one of the world's major dairy regions with north-west Europe and New Zealand; calling it "the biggest in the world" cannot be verified, and today California produces more milk than any other US State. By country, India is the world's largest milk producer (247.87 million tonnes in 2024-25, up 3.58% from 239.30 million tonnes; Basic Animal Husbandry Statistics 2025, PIB). Spellings: "Diary" = dairy; "Claifornia" = California.

[Correction] The best milch breeds used in North America are of European origin, not native: Holstein-Friesian (Netherlands and northern Germany; most US dairy cows are Holsteins, which give the most milk per cow), Jersey (Channel Islands; richer milk with more fat and solids, increasingly popular), Brown Swiss and Guernsey. USDA notes that dairy cows perform best in dry, cool weather - which is why heat stress from global warming cuts yields. India link: indigenous breeds Gir, Sahiwal, Red Sindhi, Tharparkar (cattle) and Murrah (buffalo) are heat-tolerant; the Rashtriya Gokul Mission conserves and improves them. Spelling: "breads" = breeds.

[Correction] Feed conversion ratio (FCR) = feed consumed / output (e.g. kg of feed per kg of milk or meat), so a lower FCR means better efficiency. The intended point is reversed in the notes: North American dairy has better feed efficiency (lower FCR) because of high-yielding breeds, balanced rations and herd management, while Indian dairy generally has a higher (poorer) FCR and much lower milk yield per animal. "Labour Intensice" = labour-intensive.

[Additional] Truck farming: where regions specialise in vegetables for distant cities, the distance of the farms from the market is governed by how far a truck can travel overnight - hence the name (NCERT). Refrigerated transport lets Florida (winter vegetables, citrus) and California (Salinas Valley lettuce, Imperial Valley) supply the whole continent. The Atlantic seaboard between Boston and Washington (Megalopolis) has a dense ring of market gardens and greenhouses. Mechanisation in dairying: milking machines, robotic (automatic) milking systems, mixer-feed wagons and herd-management software; US dairy has consolidated into fewer, larger farms, and dairy receipts were $48.9 billion (16.1% of animal-product receipts) in 2025 (USDA ERS).

6. Plantation Agriculture

  • Introduced by Europeans in their colonies to secure the supply of raw materials for industries in their motherland.
  • The Industrial Revolution required inputs / raw materials, which led to plantation agriculture.
  • Generally practised in tropical and sub-tropical areas.
  • Extensive, monoculture, commercial; also called estate farming; generally agri-business-driven agriculture.
  • Crops: tea, coffee, cocoa, rubber, oil palm, sugarcane, indigo, cotton.
  • Generally, it is called a plantation when hired / wage workers work on it rather than the owner.
  • Labour-intensive and capital-intensive; generally known as capitalism in agriculture.
  • Introduced in hilly areas with large land holdings and low population density.
  • Southern USA: sugarcane, tobacco and cotton are cultivated in this form.

[Correction] Plantations are large-scale (large estates) but not "extensive" in the farming sense: they use high capital and labour inputs per hectare, scientific methods and on-estate processing (NCERT lists large estates, large capital investment, managerial and technical support, scientific methods, single-crop specialisation, cheap labour and a good transport network linking estates to factories and ports). Hilly areas apply only to some crops - tea and coffee on well-drained slopes (Assam hills, Darjeeling, Nilgiris, Sri Lanka); sugarcane, bananas, oil palm, rubber and cotton plantations are mostly on plains and coastal lowlands. Indigo is a historical plantation crop (colonial Bengal and Bihar - the Champaran Satyagraha, 1917; and colonial South Carolina), now replaced by synthetic dyes. Spelling: "ans" = and.

[Correction] In the Southern USA, the plantation system (17th-19th centuries) used enslaved African labour on cotton, tobacco, rice, indigo and sugarcane estates. After the Civil War (1865) it gave way to sharecropping and, from the mid-20th century, to mechanisation (mechanical cotton picker). Today these are mechanised commercial farms, not plantations in the classical sense; US cotton is now concentrated in Texas, sugarcane in Louisiana and Florida, and tobacco in North Carolina and Kentucky. Plantation agriculture in North America today is found mainly in the Caribbean (sugarcane - Cuba, Dominican Republic, Jamaica) and the Caribbean lowlands of Central America (bananas - Honduras, Costa Rica, Guatemala; coffee on the volcanic highlands) - see Map 1.

[Additional] NCERT examples: French cocoa and coffee in West Africa; British tea gardens in India and Sri Lanka, rubber in Malaysia, sugarcane and bananas in the West Indies; Spanish and American coconut and sugarcane in the Philippines; Dutch sugarcane in Indonesia. After slavery was abolished, Europeans moved Indian indentured labourers ("girmitiya") to plantations in the Caribbean (Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, Suriname, Jamaica), Mauritius and Fiji - the origin of large Indian diasporas there. In Central America, US fruit companies (e.g. United Fruit) controlled vast banana estates, giving rise to the term "banana republic". See also PYQ 3 on tea plantations in the Himalaya.

9. Manufacturing Industry

9.1 Meaning

  • Manufacturing is a value-addition process in which raw materials are processed into finished products / higher-value products.
  • Value addition generates employment.
  • It is a multi-step process.

[Additional] Manufacturing is a secondary activity. Value added = value of output − value of inputs (raw materials, energy, services). Classification by raw material: agro-based (cotton textiles, sugar, food processing), mineral-based (iron and steel, aluminium, cement), marine-based, forest-based; by size: cottage/household, small-scale, large-scale; by ownership: public, private, joint, cooperative (e.g. sugar cooperatives of Maharashtra). See PYQ 6.

India's cotton textile industry is highly decentralised - mills, power-looms and handlooms are spread over many States (see PYQ 4).

9.3 Industrial Corridors and Special Economic Zones

  • Industrial corridor - industrial zones / nodes connected through multimodal transportation. Example given in class: the Sagarmala project (India).
  • Special Economic Zones (SEZs).

[Correction] Sagarmala is a port-led development programme of the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways (launched 2015) - port modernisation, port connectivity, port-led industrialisation through Coastal Economic Zones, and coastal community development. It is related to corridors but is not itself an industrial corridor. India's industrial corridors are developed under the National Industrial Corridor Development Programme (NICDP) - e.g. the Delhi-Mumbai Industrial Corridor (DMIC) along the Western Dedicated Freight Corridor, the Amritsar-Kolkata, Chennai-Bengaluru and East Coast corridors. In August 2024 the Cabinet approved 12 new industrial nodes / smart cities under NICDP with an estimated investment of Rs 28,602 crore (PIB).

[Additional] An SEZ is a specifically delineated duty-free enclave, treated as foreign territory for trade operations, duties and tariffs, governed in India by the SEZ Act, 2005. Asia's first export processing zone was set up at Kandla (Gujarat) in 1965. Corridor vs SEZ: a corridor is a linear, transport-based development spine linking many nodes and cities; an SEZ is a bounded zone with special trade and tax rules.

9.4 Location of Industry

  • Industry looks for an optimum location - one that maximises profit.
  • It may be near the market or near raw materials.

[Additional] Factors of location: raw materials, power, labour, market, transport, capital, water, government policy and agglomeration economies. Alfred Weber's least-cost theory (1909) places a plant where total transport cost of raw materials and finished goods is lowest, adjusted for labour costs and agglomeration. Industries with no strong pull to either raw material or market are footloose (electronics, IT, diamond cutting). See PYQ 5 on the shifting location of iron and steel.

Material Index (MI) = weight of localised raw materials / weight of finished product.
MI > 1 (weight-losing) → raw-material-oriented location (sugar mills, cement, early iron and steel near coalfields).
MI < 1 (weight-gaining, perishable or fragile product) → market-oriented location (soft-drink bottling, bread, furniture).
Ubiquities (water, air, available everywhere) exert no pull.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Average farm size: USA 466 acres (2024, USDA); Canada ~809 acres (2021); India 1.08 ha (Agriculture Census 2015-16).
  • US contracts: about 26% of value of production (2024); production contracts dominate poultry and hogs.
  • TFP = total output / total inputs. US farm output 2023 ≈ 3 × 1948 level; inputs +0.12% a year; TFP +1.40% a year (USDA ERS).
  • Fertigation = fertiliser through irrigation water (usually drip); rock phosphate is insoluble and unsuitable.
  • Three farm laws: passed September 2020, stayed by the Supreme Court January 2021, repealed by the Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021 (29 November 2021).
  • Six NA systems: grain, ranching, Mediterranean, market gardening, dairy, plantation; subsistence milpa survives in southern Mexico and Central America.
  • Canada's largest crop by area: canola (2021), then spring wheat.
  • Mediterranean climate: winter rain, dry summer, west sides of continents 30°-45°; five regions - Mediterranean Basin, California, Central Chile, Western Cape, SW and S Australia.
  • Viticulture = grapes; citriculture = citrus; Mediterranean triad = wheat, grapes, olives.
  • Top US milk States (2025): California, Wisconsin, Idaho, Texas, New York. Most US dairy cows are Holsteins.
  • India: world's largest milk producer - 247.87 million tonnes (2024-25).
  • Lower FCR = better feed efficiency.
  • Truck farming: distance a truck can cover overnight.
  • Material Index > 1 → raw-material location; < 1 → market location (Weber, 1909).
  • Sagarmala (2015) = port-led development; NICDP: 12 new industrial nodes, Rs 28,602 crore (August 2024). SEZ Act 2005; Kandla EPZ 1965.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Not all North American agriculture is commercial - milpa subsistence farming persists in Mexico and Central America.
  • Viticulture = grapes, not citrus; Florida citrus is not Mediterranean agriculture (humid subtropical climate).
  • Mediterranean climate has winter rain, not summer rain; only California represents it in North America.
  • Dairy farming is not meat farming; beef comes from ranching and feedlots.
  • Florida is not a major dairy State; California is the top milk-producing State, Wisconsin second.
  • Higher FCR = less efficient.
  • Large does not mean extensive: plantations are large estates but intensive in capital and labour.
  • Not all plantations are on hills - only tea and coffee typically are.
  • The US South is no longer a plantation economy; today's plantations are in the Caribbean and Central America.
  • TFP is not yield: yield is a single-factor (land) productivity.
  • The 2020 farm laws are repealed - do not write them as current law.
  • Sagarmala is port-led development, not an industrial corridor; SEZ is a bounded zone, a corridor a linear spine.
  • Complex vs cluster: functional linkage vs spatial concentration.

30-Second Revision

NA farming = scale (US 466 acres) + belts + capital + cooperatives + contracts (26% of US output value).
Stage 1: inputs → yield but degradation; Stage 2: TFP = output/inputs → precision farming, fertigation, AI.
Six systems: grain (Plains), ranching (dry west), Mediterranean (California), market gardening (seaboard, Florida), dairy (Great Lakes; CA top State), plantation (Caribbean).
Farm laws 2020 → repealed 2021. FTA: protect dairy/staples, raise TFP, aggregate small farms.
Manufacturing = value addition; complex (linked) vs cluster (concentrated); corridor vs SEZ; Weber MI > 1 raw material, < 1 market.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020 - General Studies Paper I

What are the advantages of fertigation in agriculture?

  1. Controlling the alkalinity of irrigation water is possible.
  2. Efficient application of Rock Phosphate and all other phosphatic fertilizers is possible.
  3. Increased availability of nutrients to plants is possible.
  4. Reduction in the leaching of chemical nutrients is possible.

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1, 2 and 3 only
  2. 1, 2 and 4 only
  3. 1, 3 and 4 only
  4. 2, 3 and 4 only
Correct answer: (c) 1, 3 and 4 only. Fertigation delivers dissolved nutrients with drip irrigation directly to the root zone (statements 3 and 4), and the pH of the irrigation water can be adjusted (statement 1); rock phosphate is insoluble in water, so statement 2 is wrong. Links to precision farming, section 8.2. Verified question and answer: Testbook.

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2023 - General Studies Paper I

Which one of the following best describes the concept of 'Small Farmer Large Field'?

  1. Resettlement of a large number of people, uprooted from their countries due to war, by giving them a large cultivable land which they cultivate collectively and share the produce
  2. Many marginal farmers in an area organize themselves into groups and synchronize and harmonize selected agricultural operations
  3. Many marginal farmers in an area together make a contract with a corporate body and surrender their land to the corporate body for a fixed term for which the corporate body makes a payment of agreed amount to the farmers
  4. A company extends loans, technical knowledge and material inputs to a number of small farmers in an area so that they produce the agricultural commodity required by the company for its manufacturing process and commercial production
Correct answer: (b). Small and marginal farmers keep their land but synchronise operations (sowing, inputs, harvesting, marketing) to gain economies of scale - the small-holder answer to the large North American farm. Option (d) describes contract farming. Links to section 8.1. Verified question and answer: Testbook.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2014 - General Studies Paper I

"Whereas the British planters had developed tea gardens all along the Shivaliks and Lesser Himalayas from Assam to Himachal Pradesh, in effect they did not succeed beyond the Darjeeling area. Explain."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: Tea needs warm, humid conditions, well-distributed heavy rainfall, frost-free slopes with good drainage and acidic soils, plus abundant cheap labour and transport to ports. Eastward (Assam, Darjeeling) rainfall and humidity are high; westward the Himalaya gets less monsoon rain, colder winters with frost and snow, and labour and transport were scarcer - so only small pockets (e.g. Kangra) survived. Add market factors: Darjeeling's altitude and mist give a premium flavour. Links to plantation agriculture, section 8.5. Verified question/year: GKToday.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - General Studies Paper I

"Analyze the factors for highly decentralized cotton textile industry in India."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: Cotton is a pure (non-weight-losing) raw material, so mills are not tied to cotton fields; electricity replaced coal, railways widened markets; abundant labour and a nationwide market; handloom and power-loom traditions in many States; policy support for small units and clusters (Tiruppur, Surat, Bhiwandi, Ludhiana). Use the complex/cluster distinction and Weber's material index. Links to section 9.2 and section 9.4. Verified question/year: ACME IAS.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2014 - General Studies Paper I

"Account for the change in the spatial pattern of the Iron and Steel industry in the world."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: Early plants located on coalfields (Ruhr, Pittsburgh-Appalachian, Midlands) because coal was the heaviest weight-losing input; with more efficient furnaces, ore fields and lake/coastal break-of-bulk points gained (Great Lakes ports, tidewater plants in Japan and Korea); shift of production to Asia (China, Japan, India) with market growth and cheaper labour; electric-arc mini-mills near scrap and markets; current shift to green steel near cheap renewable power and hydrogen. Links to section 9.4 and Figure 2. Verified question/year: ACME IAS.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2019 - General Studies Paper I

"Can the strategy of regional-resource based manufacturing help in promoting employment in India?"

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: Define resource-based manufacturing (agro-processing, minerals, marine, forest products) located near raw materials to save transport cost (weight-losing inputs); employment gains in labour-intensive, rural and MSME clusters (One District One Product, food parks, textile clusters); limits - low value addition, infrastructure gaps, skills, market access; conclude with a mix of resource-based clusters and corridor-linked manufacturing. Links to section 9.1 and section 9.3. Verified question/year: LeanPrep.
