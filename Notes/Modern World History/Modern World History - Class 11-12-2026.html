8. The French Revolution and Napoleon (continued), 1791-1815

Continues from 8.4.7 Flight to Varennes and Pillnitz in the 11 November note. Overview of the stages: 8.3 of that note.

8.6 The Constitution of 1791 (Stage 1, concluded)

  • When: completed by the National (Constituent) Assembly on 3 September 1791; accepted by Louis XVI on 13 September and sworn on 14 September 1791 †.
  • Preamble: the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 August 1789).
  • Form of state: a constitutional monarchy. Sovereignty belongs to the nation; the King rules under the law (see Figure 1).
  • Separation of powers between the executive, legislature and judiciary (Montesquieu's idea; see the 11 November note):
    • Executive - the King: appoints and dismisses ministers; commands the army and navy; holds a suspensive veto over laws; cannot declare war without the legislature.
    • Legislature - the Legislative Assembly: a single chamber (unicameral) of 745 members, elected for two years †. It makes laws, subject to the veto, and declares war on the King's formal proposal †. The King could not dissolve it †.
    • Judiciary: independent, with elected judges; uniform courts replaced the old parlements; juries for criminal cases †.
  • Local government: elected councils for the new departments, districts and communes (83 departments created in 1790 †) [Additional].
  • Franchise: only active citizens could vote (c02).
[Correction] The rough notes say the constitution separated religion from politics and that "the Church got destroyed". It subordinated the Church to the state rather than separating them:
  • Church lands were nationalised in November 1789 † and sold to pay the debt.
  • The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (12 July 1790) made bishops and priests elected, state-salaried officials and redrew the dioceses.
  • The clergy had to swear an oath to the constitution (decree of 27 November 1790). Only 7 bishops and about half the parish priests took it, and Pope Pius VI condemned the Civil Constitution in 1791 (Britannica).
The result was a split between "constitutional" (juring) and "refractory" (non-juring) clergy, which drove many Catholics against the Revolution. The Church was weakened, not destroyed. Open attacks on Christianity (dechristianisation) came in 1793-94, the Church and state were formally separated in 1795 †, and Napoleon's Concordat (1801) restored a working relationship.
[Correction] The rough notes say "man allowed to vote, but only propertied men could contest".
  • Not every man could vote: only active citizens, men aged 25 and over paying direct tax worth at least three days' labour, voted in the primary assemblies.
  • Elections were indirect: the active citizens chose electors, who had to meet a higher property test (100-400 days' labour, depending on the place), and the electors chose the deputies (Conseil constitutionnel text).
  • The marc d'argent (a high tax test for deputies) was dropped in the August 1791 revision. The final text lets any active citizen be elected deputy "whatever his tax contribution".
So property mattered most at the level of electors, not candidates.

8.6.1 Women and the Constitution: Olympe de Gouges

  • The constitution was male-centred: women were passive citizens who could neither vote nor stand. Political power went mainly to men of property.
  • Olympe de Gouges replied with the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen (1791), modelled article by article on the 1789 Declaration (Britannica). It said that "woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights" †.
  • It is a founding text of French feminism. Gouges was guillotined on 3 November 1793 during the Terror (Britannica), and the Convention closed women's political clubs in October 1793 † [Additional].
  • Exam point: French women got the vote only in 1944 † [Additional].

8.7 The Legislative Assembly and the fall of the monarchy (October 1791 - September 1792)

  • First met: 1 October 1791 (Britannica). Members of the old Assembly could not stand for it (self-denying ordinance) † [Additional].
  • Groups:
    • Feuillants: constitutional monarchists, on the right †.
    • Girondins (then called "Brissotins" after their leader Jacques-Pierre Brissot): many deputies came from the Gironde department around Bordeaux. They were mostly wealthy, educated provincial men, leaning towards a republic, and wanted to spread the Revolution abroad through war.
    • A large uncommitted centre †.
    (c03)
  • Vetoes: the Assembly passed decrees against the emigres (November 1791) and the refractory priests (29 November 1791). Louis XVI vetoed both (Britannica), which made him look like an enemy of the Revolution.
  • War: on 20 April 1792 France declared war on Austria (formally on the "King of Hungary and Bohemia"). Prussia, Austria's ally, joined in July (c04). This began the French Revolutionary Wars (1792-1802).
    • Both sides wanted war. The Girondins hoped to spread liberty and expose traitors; the King and Queen hoped defeat would restore their power †.
    • The early campaign went badly, and the King was blamed.
  • Brunswick Manifesto (dated 25 July 1792; Britannica gives 27 July): the Prussian commander threatened to destroy Paris if the royal family was harmed. It enraged Parisians.
  • 10 August 1792: armed Parisians and provincial volunteers (federes) stormed the Tuileries palace. The Legislative Assembly suspended the King and called a National Convention elected by near-universal male suffrage (c05).
  • September Massacres (2-6 September 1792): crowds killed over a thousand prisoners in Paris, fearing a plot as the Prussians advanced † [Additional].
  • Turning of the war: the French halted the Prussians at Valmy (20 September 1792) and defeated the Austrians at Jemappes (6 November 1792), then overran the Austrian Netherlands (Belgium) (c06; Map 1).

[Correction] The rough notes say the Girondins "dominated" the Legislative Assembly, "treated the King with respect" and had the goal of "expansion of franchise". The Girondins were the most vocal and influential group, and briefly formed the King's ministry in spring 1792 †, but they were not a majority; the Feuillants and the uncommitted centre were larger †. Their defining goal was a war to spread the Revolution. The name "Girondins" was mostly used later; contemporaries said "Brissotins" †.

[Correction] France declared war only on Austria on 20 April 1792. Prussia entered the war as Austria's ally (Britannica).

[Correction] The monarchy was suspended because of the insurrection of 10 August 1792, not simply because of defeats. For the Convention elections (decree of 11 August 1792), men aged 21 could vote and men aged 25 could be elected; domestic servants were excluded (Assemblee nationale). Elections were still indirect, through electors †.

[Correction] The rough notes say Austria and Prussia "got defeated in the war in 1792". The French stopped the invasion at Valmy and won at Jemappes, but the war went on. Prussia made peace only in 1795 (Treaty of Basel †), and Austria in 1797 (Campo Formio).

8.8 Stage 2 - the National Convention and the Reign of Terror (1792-1795)

8.8.1 The Convention and the Republic (September 1792 - January 1793)

  • Met: 20 September 1792 (Britannica; first public session 21 September).
  • Groups in the Convention (c07):
    • Girondins: republicans, strong in the provinces, wary of Paris crowds.
    • Montagnards ("the Mountain", seated high on the left): radical Jacobins such as Robespierre, Danton and Marat, backed by the Paris sans-culottes. They wanted a highly centralised republic.
    • The Plain (or "Marsh"): the uncommitted majority.
  • 21 September 1792: the Convention abolished the monarchy. The Republic dates from 22 September 1792 (Year I of the revolutionary calendar †). This was the First French Republic (1792-1804).
  • It set up a committee to draft a republican constitution.
  • Trial of the King: after the discovery of his secret papers in the armoire de fer (iron chest) †, "Citizen Louis Capet" was tried by the Convention, convicted of treason and guillotined on 21 January 1793 (Britannica).

[Correction] The Convention was not Jacobin-dominated from the start. Until mid-1793 the Girondins and the Montagnards competed for the votes of the Plain. Both groups were republican, and many Girondins had also been Jacobin Club members †. The Montagnards took control only after the Paris insurrection of 31 May - 2 June 1793, which forced the Convention to expel and arrest the Girondin leaders (Britannica).

8.8.2 External danger: the First Coalition (1793)

  • France itself declared war on Britain and the Dutch Republic (1 February 1793) and on Spain (7 March 1793) (Britannica) (c08).
  • The First Coalition (1792/93-1797): Austria, Prussia, Britain, the Dutch Republic, Spain, Sardinia (Piedmont; allied with Britain from April 1793) and others †.
  • Conscription: a levy of 300,000 men (February 1793) and then the levee en masse (23 August 1793), which put the whole nation on a war footing (c09).

[Correction] The rough notes say European powers "declared war on France". Austria and Prussia were already at war (since April 1792). It was France that declared war on Britain, the Dutch Republic and Spain in February-March 1793, after the King's execution and the French annexations and the opening of the river Scheldt alarmed Britain †.

[Correction] "Compulsory military service" in 1793 means the levy of 300,000 (February-March 1793; Britannica articles differ on the month) and the levee en masse of 23 August 1793, which conscripted unmarried men aged 18-25 †. A permanent conscription law (the Jourdan law) came only in 1798 †.

8.8.3 Internal danger: the Vendee and the federalist revolts (1793)

  • Vendee (west of France): peasants were in open revolt by March 1793 (Britannica), set off by the levy of 300,000. They also resented attacks on the Church and refractory priests. The war in the Vendee was suppressed with great brutality †.
  • Federalist revolts (summer 1793): after the purge of the Girondins, cities such as Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux and Toulon (and parts of Normandy †) rose against the Paris-based Convention (Britannica).
  • Historians say the internal threat amplified the external one: rebels in some places welcomed foreign help.
  • Toulon: royalists handed the naval base, with much of the French Mediterranean fleet, to an Anglo-Spanish fleet under Admiral Hood on 27-28 August 1793 (Britannica) (c10).
[Correction] The rough notes say "people of southern France revolted" for three reasons: distance, destruction of the Church and conscription.
  • The Vendee is in western France. Its rising was driven mainly by conscription and religion.
  • The federalist revolts (Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, Toulon) were in the south and south-west. They were mainly against Parisian domination and the purge of the Girondins.
  • Toulon was handed over by royalists in the town, not by "the people of South France" generally.

8.8.4 Committee of Public Safety and the rise of Bonaparte at Toulon

  • Committee of Public Safety: created on 6 April 1793 as the Convention's executive committee, with extraordinary powers. It later had 12 members †; Robespierre joined on 27 July 1793 (Britannica) (c11).
  • Revolutionary government: the Constitution of 1793 (approved 24 June 1793, very democratic) was never put into effect; it was shelved "until the peace" (Britannica). The government was declared "revolutionary until the peace" in October 1793 †.
  • Napoleon Bonaparte (1769-1821):
    • Born at Ajaccio, Corsica, on 15 August 1769 (Britannica), a year after Genoa transferred Corsica to France (Treaty of Versailles, 15 May 1768).
    • Trained at French military schools (Brienne, Ecole Militaire) †; an artillery officer.
  • Siege of Toulon (September - December 1793):
    • Bonaparte arrived as an artillery captain and was given command of the siege artillery † (c12).
    • His plan was to seize the heights commanding the harbour, so that guns could drive the Anglo-Spanish fleet out.
    • The British evacuated on 17-18 December and the Republicans entered Toulon on 19 December 1793 (Britannica).
    • Bonaparte was promoted brigadier general on 22 December 1793, aged 24 (Britannica).
    The victory made him known and removed a dangerous foothold for the enemy in the south.
[Correction] The rough notes say the Convention "suspended constitution-making" because of Toulon and set up the Committee with Robespierre as its head.
  • The Committee was created in April 1793, before Toulon fell.
  • A constitution had already been adopted in June 1793 and was then suspended.
  • Robespierre was the Committee's most prominent member, never its formal head. Its decisions were collective (Britannica).

[Correction] Bonaparte was born at Ajaccio (Corsica) and was not an "ordinary soldier": he was a trained artillery officer (captain) at Toulon, rising to major and adjutant general during the siege. Sources give slightly different dates for these steps (Britannica).

8.8.5 The Reign of Terror (September 1793 - July 1794)

  • Law of Suspects (17 September 1793): passed by the Convention (c13). It allowed the arrest of anyone suspected of being an enemy of the Revolution.
  • Scale:
    • At least 300,000 people were arrested (another Britannica article says "well over 200,000").
    • About 17,000 were officially executed.
    • Perhaps 10,000 more died in prison or without trial (Britannica) (c14).
  • Victims included Queen Marie Antoinette (guillotined 16 October 1793 †), the Girondin leaders (October 1793 †), Olympe de Gouges (3 November 1793), and later Danton (April 1794 †).
  • Other measures [Additional]:
    • the Maximum (price controls on bread and other goods) †;
    • the revolutionary calendar †;
    • abolition of slavery in the colonies (4 February 1794) †;
    • dechristianisation, and Robespierre's Cult of the Supreme Being (June 1794) †.
  • Results: the Terror and the levee en masse saved the Republic militarily. The victory at Fleurus (26 June 1794) † [Additional] removed the immediate danger, which made the Terror harder to justify.

[Correction] The Law of Suspects was passed by the National Convention, not "enacted" by Robespierre alone. It was drafted by Merlin de Douai †. Robespierre was its most famous defender, not its formal author.

[Correction] The rough notes give "30-40K" killed. The best-attested figures are about 17,000 official executions plus perhaps 10,000 deaths in prison, so roughly 27,000 or more (Britannica). Higher totals depend on whether summary killings are included †. The civil war in the Vendee killed far more people and is usually counted separately †.

8.8.6 Fall of Robespierre - 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794)

  • Robespierre's enemies in the Convention and on the committees feared they were next. On 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) the Convention ordered his arrest. He was guillotined the next day, 28 July 1794, with his close allies (Britannica). This ended the Reign of Terror.
  • Contemporaries accused him of seeking a dictatorship. Historians still debate how far that was true; the Committee's power was collective [Additional].

8.9 The Thermidorian Reaction and the Constitution of Year III (1794-1795)

  • After Thermidor the Convention moved from revolutionary to constitutional politics (the Thermidorian Reaction, 1794-1795):
    • the Jacobin Club was closed (November 1794) †;
    • the Maximum was abolished †;
    • suspects were released †;
    • a "White Terror" against former Jacobins broke out in the south †.
  • Jacobin and sans-culotte risings of Germinal and Prairial (April and May 1795), driven by hunger, were put down by the army (Britannica) (c16).
  • Constitution of Year III, dated 5 Fructidor Year III (22 August 1795) (Conseil constitutionnel):
    • France remained a republic.
    • A bicameral legislature (the first in France):
      • the Council of Five Hundred proposed and drafted laws;
      • the Council of Ancients (250 members) could only approve or reject them as a whole (articles 86 and 95) (c15).
    • Executive - the Directory: five Directors, chosen by the Ancients from a list of 50 names (ten times the number of places) drawn up by the Five Hundred (article 133).
    • The vote returned to a tax-paying basis with indirect elections, so the property owners were back in control † [Additional].
    • The "Two-Thirds Decree" kept two-thirds of the new deputies from the outgoing Convention † [Additional]. This angered the royalists.
  • 13 Vendemiaire Year IV (5 October 1795): a royalist rising in Paris against the Convention was crushed by troops under Bonaparte, famously with a "whiff of grapeshot" †. It made him a political general; he soon received command of the Army of Italy (March 1796).
  • The Directors were elected at the end of October 1795 and the Directory took office in early November 1795 †.

[Correction] The rough notes say the upper house could "accept / amend / reject". Under the Constitution of Year III the Council of Ancients could not amend: it could only approve or reject a bill as a whole (articles 86 and 95, Conseil constitutionnel). The rest of the note is right: the Five Hundred drew up a list of 50, and the Ancients chose the 5 Directors.

[Correction] The rough notes say "Jacobin supporters and royalists attacked the National Convention; Napoleon saved it". These were two separate episodes. The Jacobin / sans-culotte risings were in April-May 1795 and Napoleon was not involved. Napoleon crushed the royalist rising of 13 Vendemiaire (5 October 1795) (Britannica).

8.10 Stage 3 - the Directory (1795-1799)

8.10.1 A weak government

  • Divided executive: five Directors shared power, so there was constant infighting and decisions were slow.
  • Corruption: several Directors (notably Barras †) and their circle were corrupt.
  • Problems left unsolved:
    • financial - paper money (assignats) collapsed and the state partly defaulted in 1797 †;
    • religious - divisions over the clergy continued.
  • Attacked from both sides: by the Left, in the Conspiracy of the Equals of Babeuf (1796), an early communist plot † [Additional]; and by the royalists.
  • Relied on the army: the Directory used coups to annul elections it disliked, such as 18 Fructidor (September 1797) against the royalists † [Additional]. This made the generals the arbiters of politics.

8.10.2 Aggressive foreign policy and the Italian campaign (1796-1797)

  • The Directory wanted to defeat the remaining enemies (Sardinia, Austria and Britain) and make the war pay for itself.
  • Bonaparte took command of the Army of Italy in March 1796 (napoleon.org) (Map 2).
    • Sardinia (Piedmont): knocked out within weeks. Armistice of Cherasco (28 April 1796); by the Treaty of Paris (15 May 1796) Sardinia ceded Savoy and Nice to France.
    • Austria in Italy: Bonaparte defeated the Austrians at Lodi (10 May 1796), entered Milan, besieged Mantua, and won at Arcole (15-17 November 1796) and Rivoli (14 January 1797). Mantua fell in February 1797 †.
    • Towards Vienna: he crossed the Alps and reached Leoben in Styria, within striking distance of Vienna. Austria signed the Preliminaries of Leoben (18 April 1797) (napoleon.org) (c17).
  • Treaty of Campo Formio (17 October 1797 per Britannica; signed overnight, so napoleon.org gives 18 October):
    • Austria ceded Belgium (the Austrian Netherlands) to France;
    • Austria recognised the Cisalpine Republic (a French "sister republic" in northern Italy);
    • Austria received Venice, Istria and Dalmatia, so the old Venetian Republic was ended;
    • France kept the Ionian Islands;
    • a secret article concerned the left bank of the Rhine (Britannica).
    This ended the First Coalition; only Britain remained at war.
  • Sister republics [Additional]: the Directory set up dependent republics such as the Batavian (Netherlands), Cisalpine, Ligurian (Genoa), Roman, Helvetic and Parthenopean (Naples) Republics †.

[Correction] The rough notes speak of a separate "Austrian campaign 1796-97" in which Austria was "invaded". The fighting against Austria was part of the Italian campaign: it took place mainly in northern Italy, followed by an advance across the eastern Alps to Leoben (April 1797). Belgium was ceded by the Treaty of Campo Formio (October 1797), after the preliminary peace at Leoben.

8.10.3 The Egyptian campaign (1798-1801)

  • Aim: Britain could not be invaded, so the Directory and Bonaparte struck at Egypt, an Ottoman province, to threaten Britain's route to India and its Levant trade (napoleon.org) (Map 3).
  • Course:
    • The fleet sailed from Toulon on 19 May 1798 and seized Malta (10-11 June 1798).
    • The army landed at Alexandria (1-2 July †) and beat the Mamluks at the Battle of the Pyramids (21 July 1798).
    • The French fleet was then destroyed by Nelson at the Battle of the Nile (Aboukir Bay, 1 August 1798), which trapped the army in Egypt.
    • An invasion of Syria failed at the siege of Acre (March-May 1799), and plague hit the troops.
  • End: Bonaparte left Egypt secretly on 22 August 1799 and landed at Frejus on 9 October 1799. The army he left behind surrendered in 1801 (Cairo, June; Alexandria, September) (napoleon.org).
  • Scholarly side [Additional]: about 150 scholars went with the army; the Rosetta Stone was found in 1799 and began modern Egyptology †.
India link [Additional]:
  • The Egyptian expedition alarmed the East India Company. Governor-General Lord Wellesley (1798-1805) used fear of the French to push his Subsidiary Alliance system (see the reading list in the 11 November note).
  • He fought the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799), in which Tipu Sultan, who had sought French help, was killed at Seringapatam (4 May 1799) †.
  • A letter from Bonaparte to Tipu (early 1799) is often cited, but it was not confirmed from an opened source for this note [Verification Required].
  • Arthur Wellesley, the Governor-General's brother and the later Duke of Wellington who won at Waterloo, served in that war †.

8.10.4 The Second Coalition and the coup of 18 Brumaire (1799)

  • Second Coalition (1798/99-1802): Britain, Austria, Russia and the Ottoman Empire (Britannica), with Naples and Portugal †. French armies were pushed out of most of Italy in 1799, and the Directory became extremely unpopular.
  • Bonaparte's return: he landed in France in October 1799, his failure in Egypt hidden by his earlier victories.
  • Coup of 18-19 Brumaire Year VIII (9-10 November 1799): with two Directors, Sieyes and Roger Ducos, Bonaparte overthrew the Directory. When the Council of Five Hundred resisted, troops cleared the hall †. The three became provisional Consuls (Britannica; Conseil constitutionnel).
  • Constitution of Year VIII (dated 22 Frimaire, 13 December 1799; in force from 25 December 1799):
    • The Consulate had three Consuls: Bonaparte (First Consul), Cambaceres and Lebrun.
    • Real power lay with the First Consul.
    • It was approved by plebiscite in early 1800 †.

8.11 Napoleon in power: the Consulate and the Empire (1799-1804)

  • The Consular constitution created a highly centralised government, a form of dictatorship behind republican forms; Bonaparte was First Consul.
  • Peace abroad:
    • Russia had already left the Second Coalition in late 1799 (c18).
    • Bonaparte crossed the Alps and defeated Austria at Marengo (14 June 1800), near Alessandria in Piedmont. Moreau's victory at Hohenlinden (3 December 1800) finished the job. Austria signed the Treaty of Luneville (9 February 1801) (Britannica).
    • Peace with Britain followed in the Treaty of Amiens (27 March 1802) (Britannica). It broke down in May 1803 †.
  • Consul for life (1802) by plebiscite (Britannica).
  • Emperor: he was proclaimed Emperor of the French by a senatus-consultum of 18 May 1804, confirmed by plebiscite. He crowned himself at Notre-Dame on 2 December 1804, with Pope Pius VII present (napoleon.org) (c19).
  • The First French Republic thus lasted 1792-1804.

[Correction] The rough notes put "Russia quit the war" after Marengo and Amiens. Russia under Paul I left the Second Coalition in late 1799 (Britannica), before Marengo (1800). The peace with Austria was the Treaty of Luneville (1801); Amiens (1802) was the peace with Britain.

[Correction] Napoleon did not simply "declare himself" emperor. The Senate proclaimed the Empire (senatus-consultum of 18 May 1804), and a plebiscite approved it. He crowned himself at the coronation of 2 December 1804 (napoleon.org).

8.11.1 "Child of the Revolution" and "product of the reaction against it"

  • Child of the Revolution: the Revolution opened army careers to talent, created mass armies and wars, and destroyed the old order. This let an obscure Corsican officer rise to rule France.
  • Product of the reaction against it: he centralised power, censored the press, restored a court, nobility and titles, and ended elections that mattered. In this way he answered the chaos and radicalism of 1789-1799.
  • Balance [Additional]:
    • He kept key gains of 1789: equality before the law, the end of feudalism, the property settlement and careers open to talent.
    • He reversed political liberty and representative government.
    Historians call this "consolidating" the Revolution's social gains while ending its political phase (see the PYQs).

8.12 Reforms of Napoleon

Napoleon's reforms had two aims: (1) peace, order and stability at home, and (2) restoring confidence in and respect for government. Most date from the Consulate (1799-1804).

8.12.1 Political and administrative reforms

  • Centralisation: elected local councils lost power. Each department was run by an appointed prefect (from 1800 †) answerable to Paris, chosen for ability and loyalty. The prefect system still exists.
  • Merit: officials were appointed on merit. This created a new elite of talent and service, a "meritocratic aristocracy"; an imperial nobility followed in 1808 †.
  • Legion of Honour (19 May 1802) (Britannica): an order to reward military and civil merit. It still exists as France's highest order.
  • Reconciliation: an amnesty for emigres (26 April 1802) let most of them return, with about 1,000 exceptions (napoleon-series.org) (c20).
  • Police state [Additional]: a powerful police under Fouche, press censorship and plebiscites in place of real elections †.

[Correction] The rough notes say Napoleon "repealed atrocious laws like the Law of Suspects, 1793". The Law of Suspects had already lapsed under the Thermidorian Convention, around October 1795 (repeal date not confirmed for this note †). What Napoleon did was relax the laws against emigres: names were struck off the lists from 1800, and a general amnesty followed on 26 April 1802.

8.12.2 The Civil Code (Napoleonic Code), 1804

  • When: promulgated on 21 March 1804; renamed the Code Napoleon in 1807 (Britannica). Napoleon chaired many of the drafting sessions †.
  • What: it codified French civil law, replacing several hundred local legal systems † with a single code.
  • Sources (c21): Roman law (used in the south), customary law (used in the north, especially Paris), royal ordinances, court decisions, canon law on marriage and revolutionary laws, all organised on the principles of reason (Britannica).
  • Content:
    • equality of all citizens before the law;
    • individual liberty and freedom of conscience;
    • a secular state;
    • protection of private property (confirming the sale of Church and emigre lands);
    • freedom of contract;
    • abolition of feudal rights.
    But it subordinated wives to husbands and children to fathers, and it was harsh on workers †.
  • Effects: the law became uniform, clear and accessible, so justice was faster and cheaper. Judges must decide every case: Article 4 makes a judge who refuses to rule "on the pretext of the silence, obscurity or insufficiency of the law" guilty of a denial of justice (Legifrance). This made judicial interpretation unavoidable, though Article 5 forbids judges to lay down general rules †.
  • Spread: carried by French armies and later copied, it influenced the law of most of continental Europe and Latin America (Britannica), as well as Louisiana and Quebec †. The class figure of "about 100 countries" was not confirmed [Verification Required].
  • Napoleon at St Helena is reported to have called the Code his true glory, which would outlast his forty victories †. It is often called the "crown jewel" of his work.
[Correction] The rough notes say the Code was based on "ancient Roman law, reason and rationalism". Roman law was one source, but the Code equally drew on northern customary law (the Coutume de Paris), royal ordinances and revolutionary legislation (Britannica). It was a compromise between the old and the new.

8.12.3 Social and religious reforms

  • Concordat with Pope Pius VII (15 July 1801; promulgated at Easter 1802) (Britannica):
    • Catholicism was recognised as the religion of the "great majority of French citizens". It was not made the state religion, and Protestants and Jews kept freedom of worship.
    • The Pope accepted the loss of Church property sold since 1789.
    • The state paid the clergy's salaries.
    • The First Consul nominated bishops and the Pope instituted them.
    • Napoleon added the Organic Articles unilaterally to tighten state control.
  • Why: Napoleon used religion as a "social cement" and a political tool. The Concordat ended the religious split that had fuelled the Vendee and royalism.
  • Education:
    • lycees (state secondary schools) were created by the law of 1 May 1802;
    • the Imperial University was created by the law of 10 May 1806, given effect by decrees of 17 March 1808 (napoleon.org) (c22).
    The aim was to train loyal officials, officers and engineers.

[Correction] The rough notes say Napoleon created the "University of Paris, 1808". The University of Paris dates from the Middle Ages. Napoleon created the Imperial University (law of 1806, decrees of 1808): a single state body controlling all public education in France, not a single university (napoleon.org).

8.12.4 Economic reforms

  • Revenue: tax collection was centralised and made regular, so revenue rose and the deficit fell †.
  • Banque de France: founded on 18 January 1800 (Banque de France); it later received the monopoly of note issue in Paris (1803) †.
  • Currency: the franc germinal (law of 7 April 1803) fixed the franc at 5 grams of silver, nine-tenths fine, with gold coins alongside it (a bimetallic system). It gave France a stable currency after the inflation of the assignats (FranceArchives; Banque de France) (c23).
  • Development: public works (roads, canals, ports) †; protection and encouragement of agriculture, industry and trade.
  • Interpretation: some textbooks say he "laid the foundation for the Industrial Revolution in France". Historians are more cautious: war and the Continental System also hurt French trade and ports, and French industrialisation came mainly after 1830 [Additional].

[Correction] The franc replaced the livre in 1795 (law of 28 Thermidor Year III, 15 August 1795), before Napoleon (FranceArchives). Napoleon's contribution was the franc germinal of 1803, which fixed its metal value and kept it stable for over a century †.

8.13 Napoleonic Wars: from Trafalgar to Tilsit (1803-1807)

  • Third Coalition (1805): Britain, Austria, Russia and Sweden †.
  • Ulm (25 September - 20 October 1805): Napoleon encircled an Austrian army, which capitulated on 20 October (Britannica).
  • Trafalgar (21 October 1805): off south-west Spain, Nelson destroyed the Franco-Spanish fleet under Villeneuve; Nelson was killed.
    • It ended Napoleon's plans to invade Britain.
    • It confirmed British naval supremacy for a century †.
  • Austerlitz (2 December 1805), in Moravia, the "Battle of the Three Emperors": Napoleon defeated Austria and Russia. Austria signed the Treaty of Pressburg (26 December 1805) (Britannica).
  • Confederation of the Rhine (July 1806): Napoleon grouped the south and west German states (Bavaria, Wurttemberg, Baden and others) into a confederation under his protection (Britannica).
    • Francis II gave up the title of Holy Roman Emperor (6 August 1806 †). The Holy Roman Empire ended after about a thousand years (c24).
    • Germany was reduced from some 300 states to about 40 †, which later helped German unification.
  • Fourth Coalition (1806-07):
    • Prussia was crushed at Jena-Auerstedt (14 October 1806).
    • Russia was beaten at Friedland (14 June 1807).
    • Treaties of Tilsit (7 July 1807 with Russia, 9 July with Prussia) (Britannica). Russia joined the Continental System, and Prussia lost about half its territory †. From Prussia's Polish lands Napoleon created the Duchy of Warsaw.

[Correction] Napoleon did not "conquer most of the Holy Roman Empire". After Austerlitz the south German princes were his allies, rewarded with territory and royal titles, and they joined the Confederation of the Rhine of their own accord. Its creation led Francis II to dissolve the Holy Roman Empire in 1806.

8.13.1 The Continental System (1806-1812)

  • What: economic warfare against Britain, the "nation of shopkeepers". It was declared by the Berlin Decree (21 November 1806), issued after Jena (c25), and extended by the Milan Decree (17 December 1807) (Britannica).
  • Aim: to close Europe to British goods, ruin British exports and credit, and so weaken Britain's economy and its ability to pay for coalitions.
  • Britain's reply: the Orders in Council (1807), a counter-blockade of neutral trade with France † [Additional]. This later contributed to the Anglo-American War of 1812 †.
  • Enforcement led to more wars: to make the blockade work, Napoleon had to control every coast. That drew him into Portugal and Spain (1807-08), the annexation of the Netherlands and north German coast (1810) †, and finally Russia (1812).
  • Effects:
    • Britain suffered but survived, thanks to sea power, smuggling and new markets in the Americas and Asia.
    • Continental ports and consumers suffered more, which turned opinion against France.
    • British merchants' search for new markets also helped end the EIC's Indian trade monopoly in the Charter Act of 1813 † (see the 11 November reading list).

[Correction] The rough notes put the Continental System before Jena. The order was: Jena-Auerstedt (14 October 1806), then Napoleon entered Berlin, then the Berlin Decree (21 November 1806). Prussia was defeated first and then forced to apply the blockade.

8.13.2 Portugal, Spain and Italy (1807-1814)

  • Portugal, Britain's old ally, refused to join the blockade. By the Treaty of Fontainebleau (October 1807) Spain agreed to help. Junot's army marched through Spain and reached Lisbon on 30 November 1807; the Portuguese royal family fled to Brazil † (Britannica).
  • Spain: in 1808 Napoleon forced the Spanish Bourbons to abdicate at Bayonne and made his brother Joseph Bonaparte King of Spain. The Madrid rising of 2 May 1808 (Dos de Mayo) was crushed (Britannica).
  • Peninsular War (1808-1814):
    • Spanish irregulars fought a "little war", the guerrilla; the word comes from this war (Britannica).
    • British forces under Arthur Wellesley (Wellington) helped them.
    • About 200,000-300,000 French troops were tied down for years †. Napoleon called it his "Spanish ulcer" †.
    (c26)
  • Italy (c27) was reorganised:
    • the Kingdom of Italy in the north (1805, with Napoleon as King);
    • the Kingdom of Naples in the south (Joseph, then Murat);
    • Piedmont, Genoa, Tuscany and Rome were annexed to France †.

[Correction] The Spanish campaign lasted 1808-1814, not 1808-1812. Wellington's victory at Vitoria (June 1813) † drove the French out of Spain, and the war ended with the Allied invasion of south-west France in 1814 (Britannica: 5 May 1808 - March 1814).

[Correction] The rough notes say Napoleon set up "a series of small Italian republics" during the Spanish war. The sister republics (Cisalpine, Ligurian, Roman, Parthenopean) belong to the Directory period, 1797-99. Under the Empire, Italy was organised into kingdoms and annexed departments:
  • Kingdom of Italy, 1805;
  • Kingdom of Naples, 1806;
  • annexations up to 1809-10 †.
All were French-controlled satellite or annexed territories.

8.14 The fall of Napoleon (1812-1821)

8.14.1 The Russian campaign, 1812

  • Cause: Tsar Alexander I (1801-1825) broke with the Continental System. A ukase of 31 December 1810 opened Russian ports to neutral ships, which carried British goods, and taxed French imports (Britannica) (c28).
  • Invasion: the Grande Armee crossed the river Neman on 24 June 1812. It was a multinational army: about 450,000 at first and about 612,000 over the whole campaign (Britannica).
  • Russian strategy: retreat and scorched earth, destroying crops and supplies, which starved the invaders.
  • Borodino (7 September 1812): the bloodiest battle of the war. It was a costly, indecisive French victory: the Russians withdrew in order (c28).
  • Moscow: Napoleon entered on 14 September 1812, but the city was set on fire † and the Tsar refused to negotiate.
  • Retreat: it began on 19 October. Hunger, disease, Cossack raids and then the Russian winter destroyed the army; the crossing of the Berezina (26-29 November 1812) was a disaster.
  • Losses: of about 612,000 men who entered Russia, only about 112,000 came back (Britannica). This total includes flank corps; the main body that recrossed the Neman was far smaller (c28).
[Correction] Three points in the rough notes need adjusting.
  • Russia did more than "continue trade with Britain": it formally withdrew from the Continental System (31 December 1810).
  • Borodino was a narrow, costly French success rather than a clear victory.
  • The figure of "only 30K troops" left is roughly right only for the core of the main army †. Britannica's overall figure is about 112,000 survivors out of 612,000.
Many of the losses came before the winter, from hunger, disease and desertion.

8.14.2 Leipzig, abdication and Elba (1813-1814)

  • Sixth Coalition: Russia, Prussia, Britain, Sweden and, from August 1813, Austria †.
  • Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations" (16-19 October 1813): Napoleon was decisively defeated (Britannica). The Confederation of the Rhine collapsed.
  • 1814: the Allies invaded France and entered Paris on 31 March 1814.
    • Napoleon abdicated on 6 April 1814.
    • By the Treaty of Fontainebleau (11 April 1814) he received the island of Elba as a sovereign principality (Britannica).
  • Bourbon Restoration:
    • Louis XVIII, brother of Louis XVI, became King.
    • The First Treaty of Paris (30 May 1814) reduced France to its frontiers of 1 January 1792 (Britannica).
    • The King granted a constitution, the Charter of 4 June 1814, making France a limited constitutional monarchy with a two-chamber parliament (Conseil constitutionnel) (c29).

[Correction] The rough notes say "the French empire had been reduced to a French kingdom by Russia". Russia's campaign broke Napoleon's army, but the Empire ended only after Leipzig (1813), the Allied invasion of France and the abdication of April 1814. The Charter of 1814 was granted by the King (octroyee), not drawn up by the people, and the vote was restricted to the very rich †.

8.14.3 Congress of Vienna, the Hundred Days and Waterloo (1814-1815)

  • Congress of Vienna (September 1814 - June 1815; Final Act 9 June 1815) (Britannica): the great powers redrew the map of Europe. Its principles were legitimacy (restoring "rightful" dynasties), compensation and the balance of power (8.16.1). Leading figures were Metternich (Austria), Castlereagh (Britain), Alexander I (Russia), Hardenberg (Prussia) and Talleyrand (France) †.
  • Return from Elba: Napoleon left Elba on 26 February 1815, landed at Golfe-Juan near Cannes on 1 March and entered Paris on 20 March, as Louis XVIII fled. This began the Hundred Days (c30).
  • Belgian campaign (June 1815): he struck into Belgium, then part of the new United Kingdom of the Netherlands, before the Allied armies could unite. He was decisively defeated at Waterloo (18 June 1815) by Wellington's Anglo-allied army and Blucher's Prussians (Britannica).
  • End:
    • He abdicated again on 22 June 1815.
    • He was exiled as a British prisoner to St Helena, a remote island in the South Atlantic, landing in October 1815 (15 October per Britannica; one chronology gives 17 October).
    • He died there on 5 May 1821.
  • The Second Treaty of Paris (20 November 1815) † [Additional] cut France back to its 1790 frontiers and imposed an indemnity and occupation.

[Correction] The rough notes say Napoleon "invaded Belgium and thus started the 100 days campaign". The Hundred Days began with his return to France (1 March / 20 March 1815) and ended with the second Bourbon restoration in July. The invasion of Belgium (Ligny and Quatre-Bras on 16 June, Waterloo on 18 June †) was its last military act, not its start.

8.15 After Napoleon: the revolutions of 1820, 1830 and 1848 [Additional - requested in class]

The class asked for details from the Spanish revolution of 1820 to the July Revolution of 1830 and the February Revolution of 1848. These are the main facts; a later class on the 1848 revolutions and national unification may expand them.

  • Background - the Vienna system: after 1815 the great powers tried to keep the restored order through the Concert of Europe, a system of regular congresses †. The Quadruple Alliance (Britain, Austria, Prussia, Russia) † and the Tsar's Holy Alliance (1815) † backed it. Austria's chancellor Metternich led the effort to suppress liberalism and nationalism (the "Metternich system").
  • Why revolutions continued: the ideas of 1789 (constitutions, rights, nationalism) had spread under Napoleon, while the restored rulers tried to rule as before.

8.15.1 The revolutions of 1820-1823

  • Spain (1820): on 1 January 1820 Colonel Rafael del Riego, with troops near Cadiz waiting to sail against the rebel colonies in Latin America, rose in revolt. King Ferdinand VII was forced to restore the liberal Constitution of 1812 (the Cadiz Constitution) (PARES, Spanish state archives).
  • Spread: Naples (July 1820) (Britannica), Portugal (1820) † and Piedmont (1821) † also forced constitutions on their rulers.
  • Reaction:
    • At the congresses of Troppau (October-December 1820) and Laibach (1821) the eastern powers claimed the right to intervene against revolutions (Troppau Protocol) (Britannica). Austria crushed the risings in Naples and Piedmont in 1821 †.
    • The Congress of Verona (1822) authorised France to act in Spain. A French army (the "Hundred Thousand Sons of Saint Louis") crossed into Spain on 7 April 1823 (Britannica) and restored Ferdinand VII's absolute rule; the fort of the Trocadero at Cadiz fell in August 1823 †. Riego was executed †.
    • Britain under Canning refused to join these interventions, which weakened the Concert †.
  • Greece (1821-1830): the Greek War of Independence against the Ottomans was the exception. Britain, France and Russia intervened, destroying the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at Navarino in 1827 †, and Greece became independent by the London Protocol of 1830 †. Nationalism won, with great-power help.

8.15.2 The July Revolution, 1830

  • Cause: Charles X (King 1824-1830), the ultra-royalist brother of Louis XVIII, issued the July Ordinances, published on 26 July 1830. They suspended press freedom, dissolved the newly elected Chamber and narrowed the franchise (Britannica).
  • The "Three Glorious Days" (27-29 July 1830): Paris rose and the barricades won. Charles X abdicated on 2 August, and the Duke of Orleans was proclaimed King Louis-Philippe on 9 August 1830, the "King of the French" (Britannica).
  • Nature: the July Monarchy (1830-1848) was a constitutional monarchy of the wealthy bourgeoisie, not a democracy. The vote stayed limited to a small propertied class †. It got rid of the clerical, aristocratic Bourbon style, not of privilege by wealth (see the 2015 PYQ).
  • Echoes:
    • Belgium revolted against Dutch rule in August 1830 † and became independent; its neutrality was guaranteed in 1839 †.
    • Poland: the November Uprising against Russia began on 29 November 1830 and was crushed in 1831 (Britannica).
    • Unrest also broke out in parts of Italy and Germany.
India link [Additional]: Raja Rammohan Roy was thrilled by the July Revolution. His biographer Sophia Dobson Collet wrote that he "could think and talk of nothing else" (Collet, Life and Letters of Raja Rammohun Roy). On the voyage to England (sailed 19 November 1830) he insisted on visiting French ships flying the tricolour at Cape Town and was hurt in a fall. Versions of this anecdote differ [Verification Required].

8.15.3 The February Revolution, 1848, and the "Springtime of Peoples"

  • Causes:
    • bad harvests (1845-47) and an industrial depression †;
    • the narrow franchise and corruption of the Guizot ministry;
    • growing socialist and republican ideas;
    • a ban on the reformers' "banquet campaign" for a wider vote †.
  • Course (22-24 February 1848): street protests and barricades in Paris; Guizot was dismissed on 23 February; Louis-Philippe abdicated on 24 February 1848. The Second Republic was proclaimed (L'Histoire; Britannica).
  • Measures of the Provisional Government:
    • universal male suffrage (March 1848 †);
    • National Workshops to give work to the unemployed (set up 27-29 February 1848), associated with the socialist Louis Blanc †;
    • abolition of slavery in the colonies (April 1848) †.
  • June Days (23-26 June 1848): closing the Workshops provoked a workers' rising, crushed by General Cavaignac (Britannica). Middle-class republicans and workers split.
  • Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, Napoleon's nephew, was elected President on 10 December 1848, seized power in a coup on 2 December 1851 (Britannica) and became Emperor Napoleon III of the Second Empire in 1852 †.
  • Across Europe: risings followed in Vienna, where Metternich resigned on 13 March 1848 (Britannica), and in Berlin, Milan, Venice, Budapest and Prague. The Frankfurt Parliament tried to unite Germany †. Almost all of these revolutions failed by 1849, but serfdom was abolished in the Austrian Empire and the ideas of nationalism and democracy survived to shape Italian and German unification †.

8.16 Key terms: balance of power, Realpolitik and Blitzkrieg [Additional - requested in class]

Three terms often confused
TermMeaningOrigin and examples
Balance of powerNo single state should become strong enough to dominate the others. If one tries, the others combine against it.
  • An old principle of European diplomacy, written into the Peace of Utrecht (1713) †.
  • It explains the coalitions against Napoleon and the Congress of Vienna, which strengthened France's neighbours (the Netherlands, Prussia on the Rhine, Piedmont) as barriers †.
  • Britain followed it as a policy towards the Continent.
Realpolitik ("politics of reality")Policy based on power and practical interests, not on ideals, ideology or moral principles.
  • Coined by the German liberal Ludwig August von Rochau in Grundsatze der Realpolitik (1853) (German History in Documents and Images), after the failure of the idealistic 1848 revolutions.
  • Classic practitioners were Bismarck (German unification through "blood and iron") and Cavour †.
Blitzkrieg ("lightning war")Rapid war by concentrated tanks, motorised infantry and aircraft. They break through at one point and drive deep, causing collapse before the enemy can react.
  • Associated with Germany's campaigns in Poland (1939) and France (1940) in World War II †.
  • It was not an official German doctrine (c31).
  • Its failure in Russia (1941) mirrors Napoleon's 1812 †.

[Correction / clarification] "Blitzkrieg" is often described as Nazi Germany's official doctrine, and its first use is often credited to Time magazine in 1939. The word was hardly used in official German terminology; it appeared in German military journals in the 1930s (1935 and 1938) (War History; following K.-H. Frieser, The Blitzkrieg Legend). The "doctrine" is largely a later label for improvised operational success.

Mains Points

  • Why the Revolution radicalised (1791-94):
    • the King's untrustworthiness (Varennes, vetoes);
    • war from April 1792 and foreign threats (the Brunswick Manifesto);
    • pressure from the Paris sans-culottes;
    • economic crisis;
    • civil war (the Vendee, federalism).
    "Internal threat amplified external threat", and the Terror was justified as "emergency government".
  • The Terror - assessment: it saved the Republic (the levee en masse, victories by 1794) and pioneered state economic controls. But it destroyed liberty, killed tens of thousands and discredited radical democracy for a generation. Its legacy is debated as "circumstance" against "ideology".
  • Directory - why it failed:
    • a divided executive;
    • a narrow franchise;
    • corruption;
    • financial collapse;
    • attacks from both Left and Right;
    • dependence on generals, which paved the way for Brumaire.
  • Napoleon - child of the Revolution and product of the reaction (8.11.1):
    • He consolidated civil equality, the end of feudalism, the property settlement and meritocracy (the Code, prefects, lycees, Banque de France).
    • He reversed liberty and representative government (plebiscites, censorship, a new nobility).
    "His conquests were ephemeral but his civil work was built on granite."
  • Continental System - why it failed:
    • British sea power and new markets;
    • smuggling;
    • harm to continental economies;
    • enforcement required endless conquest (Portugal, Spain, Russia).
    Many historians call it a "monument of misdirected energy" and a key cause of the fall.
  • Causes of Napoleon's fall:
    • overextension;
    • British naval and financial power;
    • the Spanish ulcer;
    • Russia in 1812;
    • the nationalism he provoked (Spain, Prussia, Germany);
    • coalitions united by the balance-of-power principle;
    • war-weariness in France.
  • Impact on Europe:
    • the end of the Holy Roman Empire;
    • the simplification of Germany and Italy, a seed of unification;
    • the spread of the Code and of the end of feudalism;
    • the rise of nationalism against French rule.
  • India link: the French threat (Egypt, Tipu) justified Wellesley's aggressive expansion and the Subsidiary Alliance. The Continental System indirectly contributed to the Charter Act of 1813. Rammohan Roy welcomed the July Revolution.
  • 1820-1848: a cycle of liberal and national revolutions against the Vienna settlement. They succeeded in 1830 (France, Belgium) and largely failed in 1848, but the ideas survived. The shift to Realpolitik after 1848 explains how unification was later achieved.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Three assemblies: National (Constituent) Assembly 1789-91 → Legislative Assembly 1791-92 → National Convention 1792-95.
  • Constitutions: 1791 (monarchy, unicameral) - 1793 (never applied) - Year III 1795 (Directory, bicameral) - Year VIII 1799 (Consulate).
  • Girondins vs Jacobins: both republican; the difference was Paris centralism and radicalism. The Girondins were not monarchists.
  • Robespierre was a member of the Committee of Public Safety, never its "head"; the Law of Suspects was a Convention law.
  • Vendee = west (a royalist-Catholic peasant revolt) vs federalist revolts = Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, Toulon.
  • 13 Vendemiaire (1795) = royalist rising, crushed by Bonaparte; 18 Brumaire (1799) = Bonaparte's coup; 9 Thermidor (1794) = Robespierre's fall.
  • Campo Formio (1797, Austria) vs Luneville (1801, Austria) vs Amiens (1802, Britain) vs Pressburg (1805, Austria) vs Tilsit (1807, Russia and Prussia).
  • Battle of the Nile (Aboukir Bay, 1798) was a naval defeat; the Pyramids (1798) was a land victory.
  • Trafalgar (21 Oct 1805) came one day after Ulm and before Austerlitz.
  • Continental System = Berlin Decree (Nov 1806) + Milan Decree (Dec 1807); Orders in Council = Britain's reply.
  • The Concordat did not make Catholicism the state religion.
  • Imperial University (1808), not the University of Paris; the franc dates from 1795, the franc germinal from 1803.
  • Hundred Days began with the return from Elba (March 1815), not the Belgian campaign. Waterloo was in Belgium (then the United Kingdom of the Netherlands).
  • Elba (Mediterranean, 1814-15) vs St Helena (South Atlantic, 1815-21).
  • July Revolution 1830 (Charles X out, Louis-Philippe in) vs February Revolution 1848 (Louis-Philippe out, Second Republic).
  • Realpolitik (power-based diplomacy, 1850s) vs balance of power (a system goal) vs Blitzkrieg (a military method, WWII).

30-Second Revision

1791-92:
  • The 1791 Constitution set up a constitutional monarchy, with a single chamber, a suspensive veto and active citizens only; Olympe de Gouges protested.
  • The Legislative Assembly, with the Girondins prominent, went to war with Austria (Apr 1792).
  • The Tuileries was stormed (10 Aug), the King suspended and a Convention elected by men 21+.
  • Valmy (20 Sep) and the Republic (22 Sep 1792) followed.
1793-95:
  • The King was executed (21 Jan 1793), and the First Coalition, the Vendee and federalist revolts followed.
  • The Committee of Public Safety (Robespierre) ran the Law of Suspects and the Terror (about 17,000 executed); Toulon was retaken by Bonaparte (Dec 1793).
  • Thermidor (27 Jul 1794) led to the Constitution of Year III and the Directory (1795).
1796-99:
  • The Italian campaign brought Savoy and Nice, and Campo Formio gave France Belgium.
  • The Egyptian campaign failed after the Nile (threatening India and Tipu).
  • Brumaire (9 Nov 1799) made Bonaparte First Consul.
1800-04: Marengo, Luneville, Amiens, the Concordat, the Legion of Honour, prefects, the Banque de France, the franc germinal, the Civil Code and the Empire (1804). 1805-12:
  • He lost at sea at Trafalgar but won on land at Ulm and Austerlitz.
  • He created the Confederation of the Rhine and ended the HRE; won at Jena, launched the Continental System, and won at Friedland and Tilsit.
  • Spain became an "ulcer" (1808-14), and Russia's withdrawal led to the 1812 invasion, Borodino, Moscow and the retreat.
1813-21: Leipzig, Elba, Louis XVIII, Vienna, the Hundred Days, Waterloo and St Helena. Later: revolutions in 1820 (Spain), 1830 (July) and 1848 (February); Realpolitik followed.

1. UPSC CSE Mains 2004 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(b) (20 marks, 200 words)

Comment on: 'Napoleon was the child of the Revolution, but in many ways he reversed the aims and principles of the movement from which he sprang'.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Child: rose through careers open to talent and revolutionary wars (Toulon, Vendemiaire, Italy) (8.8.4, 8.11.1).
  • Kept: civil equality, the end of feudalism, the property settlement, the Code (8.12.2).
  • Reversed: liberty, elections, press freedom, secularism (Concordat), a new nobility, hereditary empire (8.11).
  • Conclude: the heir of 1789's social revolution, the gravedigger of its political one.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2026 - History Optional, Paper II, Q6(b) (20 marks)

The conquests of Napoleon were ephemeral but his civilian work was built on granite. Explain.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Ephemeral: the empire of 1812 collapsed by 1815, and France was cut back to its 1790/1792 frontiers (8.14; Map 4).
  • Granite: the Civil Code, prefects, lycees, the Imperial University, the Banque de France, the franc germinal, the Concordat (until 1905 †) and the Legion of Honour (8.12).
  • Indirect legacies abroad: the end of the HRE, the simplification of Germany and Italy, legal codes.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2022 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(b) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: 'The codification of French Law was perhaps the most enduring of Napoleon's achievements'.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Date, sources and content of the 1804 Code; its uniformity; Article 4; its spread to Europe and Latin America (8.12.2).
  • Critically: it was patriarchal and pro-property, and other legacies also endured (prefects, the Banque de France, lycees).

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2021 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(b) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: Napoleon's continental system may be reckoned as the greatest blunder and may be described as a "monument of misdirected energy".

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Logic: Britain could not be invaded after Trafalgar, so economic war (8.13.1).
  • Failure: British sea power, smuggling, harm to the Continent, Orders in Council.
  • Blunder: enforcement led to Portugal and Spain and then Russia (8.13.2, 8.14.1).
  • Balance: it did protect some French industries †, and Britain was hurt in 1810-12 †.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2020 - History Optional, Paper II, Q6(a) (20 marks)

"Napoleon was not a revolutionary but he solidified many of the revolutionary changes of 1789-1791 and he himself supported most of the ideas and proposals of Enlightenment philosophers." Substantiate.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Solidified: the abolition of feudalism, equality before the law, the land sales, the departments, meritocracy (8.12).
  • Enlightenment: rational codification, a secular state, toleration (Concordat for "the majority", not a state religion), state education. He was close to the "enlightened despot" model.
  • Limits: no liberty or popular sovereignty in practice.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(b) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: "The spirit behind the great reforms of Napoleon's Consulate at home was the transference of the methods of Bonaparte the general to the task of Bonaparte the statesman."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Military method in civil life: centralisation and hierarchy (prefects), speed, merit, discipline, plebiscites as "orders from above" (8.12.1).
  • Critically: many reforms built on revolutionary drafts (the Code, the franc of 1795) and on Old Regime traditions, and the reforms involved compromise (the Concordat).

7. UPSC CSE Mains 2026 - History Optional, Paper II, Q6(a) (20 marks)

The Vienna Settlement of 1815 was based on three principles, restoration, legitimacy and compensation. Elucidate.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Restoration and legitimacy: the Bourbons in France, Spain and Naples (8.14.2).
  • Compensation and balance of power: barrier states around France, gains for Prussia on the Rhine, Austria in Lombardy-Venetia † (8.14.3, 8.16).
  • Consequence: nationalism and liberalism were ignored, leading to 1820, 1830 and 1848 (8.15).

8. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(c) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: "The whole episode that is known as the July Revolution (1830) was fought and won not for the establishment of an extreme democracy but to get rid of the aristocratic and clericalist attitude of the restored Bourbons."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Charles X's ultra-royalism and the July Ordinances (8.15.2).
  • The result was a bourgeois monarchy with a narrow vote, not democracy; the workers who fought gained little.
  • But it established parliamentary sovereignty over the King and inspired Belgium and Poland.

9. UPSC CSE Mains 2023 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(c) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: "The Revolutions of 1848 were shaped by the ideas of democracy and nationalism."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Democracy: universal male suffrage in France.
  • Nationalism: Germany (Frankfurt), Italy, Hungary, the Czech lands.
  • Also: socialism (National Workshops, June Days) and economic crisis (8.15.3).
  • They failed because of divisions between liberals and radicals and among nationalities.

Corrections Made

Each link returns to the explanation, which keeps the rough-note claim. Spelling and grammar fixes are not listed.

  1. 1791: the Church was subordinated (Civil Constitution of the Clergy, 1790), not "destroyed"; formal separation came only in 1795.
  2. 1791 franchise: only active citizens voted, indirectly; the marc d'argent for deputies was dropped.
  3. Girondins were prominent, not a majority, in the Legislative Assembly; their aim was war to spread the Revolution.
  4. War was declared on Austria only (20 Apr 1792); Prussia joined as Austria's ally.
  5. Monarchy suspended after the 10 August 1792 insurrection; Convention voters 21+, deputies 25+, servants excluded.
  6. Valmy and Jemappes did not end the war; Prussia made peace in 1795, Austria in 1797.
  7. The Convention was not Jacobin-dominated from the start; the Montagnards took over after June 1793.
  8. France declared war on Britain, the Dutch and Spain in 1793, not the reverse.
  9. "Compulsory military service" = the levy of 300,000 and the levee en masse; permanent conscription came in 1798.
  10. The Vendee is in the west; federalist revolts were separate; royalists handed over Toulon.
  11. Committee of Public Safety created April 1793; Robespierre never its formal head; the 1793 constitution was adopted and then suspended.
  12. Bonaparte was born at Ajaccio and was an artillery officer, not an ordinary soldier.
  13. The Law of Suspects was passed by the Convention, not by Robespierre alone.
  14. Terror deaths: about 17,000 executed plus about 10,000 in prison, not "30-40K" as a settled figure.
  15. The Council of Ancients could not amend bills, only approve or reject them.
  16. The Jacobin risings (spring 1795) and the royalist rising of 13 Vendemiaire (crushed by Bonaparte) were separate.
  17. The "Austrian campaign" was part of the Italian campaign; Belgium was ceded at Campo Formio after Leoben.
  18. Russia left the Second Coalition in 1799, before Marengo; Luneville (1801) was the peace with Austria.
  19. The Senate proclaimed the Empire (18 May 1804), confirmed by plebiscite; Napoleon crowned himself on 2 Dec 1804.
  20. The Law of Suspects had lapsed by 1795; Napoleon's measure was the emigre amnesty of 1802.
  21. The Civil Code drew on customary and revolutionary law as well as Roman law.
  22. Imperial University (1806/1808), not the "University of Paris".
  23. The franc replaced the livre in 1795; Napoleon's franc germinal dates from 1803.
  24. The Holy Roman Empire was not conquered; allied German princes formed the Confederation of the Rhine.
  25. Jena-Auerstedt (Oct 1806) came before the Berlin Decree (Nov 1806).
  26. The Peninsular War lasted 1808-1814, not 1808-1812.
  27. Italian sister republics belong to 1797-99; under the Empire, Italy was organised into kingdoms and annexed departments.
  28. Russia formally left the Continental System (Dec 1810); Borodino was indecisive; about 112,000 of 612,000 returned.
  29. The Empire ended after Leipzig and the 1814 invasion, not "by Russia"; the Charter of 1814 was granted by the King.
  30. The Hundred Days began with the return from Elba; the Belgian campaign was its end.
  31. Blitzkrieg was not official German doctrine, and the term predates Time magazine's 1939 use.
