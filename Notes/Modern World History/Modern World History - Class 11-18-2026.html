13. The Inter-war Period and the Rise of Dictators (1919-1939)

13.0 Outline

  2. Challenges to world peace (13.2)
  3. Efforts to improve international affairs (13.3)
  4. The Great Depression and the global economic crisis (13.4, 13.5)

"Inter-war period" means the twenty years between the end of World War I (Armistice, 11 November 1918; Treaty of Versailles, 28 June 1919) and the start of World War II (German invasion of Poland, 1 September 1939). The historian E.H. Carr called it "the twenty years' crisis" (1939) †.

13.1 The inter-war period as an era of great progress

  • Science and technology developed impressively, through the work of figures such as Albert Einstein (theory of relativity; Nobel Prize for Physics 1921), Alexander Fleming (discovery of penicillin, 1928) and Sigmund Freud (psychoanalysis, whose ideas spread widely in the 1920s) †.
  • Automobiles altered society. Innovations of the Ford Motor Company (the Model T, 1908, and the moving assembly line, 1913) made cars cheap and widely available. Mobility became easier and lifestyles changed: suburbs, roadside businesses, holidays by car. The USA was the world leader.
  • Aircraft transformed international travel. The inter-war years saw great improvements in aviation technology and the rise of commercial airlines (KLM, 1919; Imperial Airways, 1924) †. Charles Lindbergh's solo non-stop flight from New York to Paris (20-21 May 1927) † showed what was possible. International travel became faster and the world "shrank".
  • Radio reached millions. After the war radio became a medium of entertainment and news for ordinary people: regular public broadcasting began with KDKA in Pittsburgh (November 1920) and the BBC in Britain (1922) †.
  • Movies revolutionised entertainment. Motion pictures were produced in many countries and became a new mass entertainment. The world centre of the film industry was Hollywood in the USA. The first feature-length "talkie", The Jazz Singer, came in 1927 †.
  • Political empowerment of women. In recognition of women's support, contribution and sacrifices during the war, women were given the vote in Britain (Representation of the People Act, 1918, for women over 30 who met a property qualification; equal franchise at 21 in 1928) and the USA (19th Amendment, 1920) †. Germany (1918) and Soviet Russia (1917) also gave women the vote; France did so only in 1944 †. [Additional]

Because of these developments the inter-war period is called an era of progress. But progress had a darker side:

  • Advances in aviation also meant the birth of modern air forces and of aerial warfare (for example, the bombing of Guernica in the Spanish Civil War, April 1937) †.
  • Radio and cinema became propaganda tools for dictators (Goebbels' Ministry of Propaganda in Nazi Germany, 1933; Leni Riefenstahl's film Triumph of the Will, 1935) †.

[Correction] The notes say that "till World War I radio technology was used for military purposes". Before 1914 wireless was already used commercially, mainly for point-to-point telegraphy with ships (Marconi's companies; the Titanic distress calls of 1912) as well as by navies. What was new after the war was public broadcasting of voice and music to home receivers, from 1920.

[Correction] The notes give the USA "approximately 70%" of world motor manufacturing. Contemporary figures put it higher: in 1926 the USA (with American-owned plants in Canada) made about 4.4 million motor vehicles, against about 0.56 million for all of Europe, i.e. roughly 85-90% of world output; the USA also owned about 80% of the world's motor vehicles in 1927. (Figures from a 1927 trade bulletin reproduced by 1920-30.com; treat them as approximate.)

[Verification Required] The notes say Hollywood made "90%" of the world's films. No reliable figure for a world production share was found in this session. It is safer to write that Hollywood dominated world cinema and that American films filled most screen time in many countries in the 1920s.

13.3 Efforts to improve international affairs (1920-1932)

13.3.1 The League of Nations (1920)

  • The League of Nations was established in 1920 (the Covenant came into force on 10 January 1920; headquarters at Geneva) to maintain peace and order through collective security, arbitration and disarmament. Its founding, organs and the mandate system are in the 16 November 2026 note (11.11).
  • [Additional] India link: India was an original member of the League (and of the ILO), even though it was not self-governing; it was represented by British-nominated delegations †.

Who called it: US Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes. Why: to end a naval arms race and to relieve tension in East Asia and the Pacific. The three main naval powers were the USA, Britain and Japan, and their positions were:

[Correction] The notes present the conference as a meeting "by the US, Britain and Japan" in which "3 countries agreed to maintain naval strength of 5:5:3". In fact nine countries were invited: the USA, Britain, Japan, France and Italy for the naval talks, and China, Belgium, the Netherlands and Portugal for the Far East talks. The 5:5:3 ratio applied to the USA, Britain and Japan, but France and Italy were also bound (1.75 each), and the limits covered capital ships (battleships and battle-cruisers) and aircraft carriers †, not total naval strength; cruisers were left free, which started a new race (renewed talks in 1927 and 1930).

[Correction] "Promised not to establish military bases close to Japan" should be stated more exactly. Article XIX of the Five-Power Treaty kept the status quo on fortifications and naval bases in certain Pacific islands of all three powers: for example the US Philippines, Guam and the Aleutians, British Hong Kong, and Japan's outlying islands (Kuriles, Bonins, Formosa (Taiwan), the mandated islands) †. It did not cover Hawaii, Singapore or the Japanese home islands.

[Correction] Two causal links in the notes need care. (1) "USA agreed to export oil to Japan" was not a term of any Washington treaty; it is true that Japan depended on American oil in the inter-war years, and it was the US oil embargo of July-August 1941 † that pushed Japan towards war. (2) The treaties did not simply give Japan "domination of the Pacific". The 5:5:3 ratio left Japan weaker than the USA and Britain combined, though Article XIX made it the strongest naval power in the western Pacific. Japanese nationalists resented the "inferior" ratio; Japan gave notice in December 1934 and left the treaty system when it expired at the end of 1936 †. The road to Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941) ran mainly through Manchuria (1931), the war with China (from 1937) and the oil embargo, not through the Washington treaties alone.

[Additional] India link: India signed the Washington treaties as part of the British Empire delegation; V.S. Srinivasa Sastri represented India at the conference †.

13.3.3 The Genoa Conference (10 April - 19 May 1922) and the Treaty of Rapallo

  • Who and where: organised at the initiative of the British Prime Minister David Lloyd George, at Genoa in Italy, with 34 European countries. For the first time since the war, Germany and Soviet Russia were invited.
  • Aims: the economic reconstruction of Europe, stable currencies (a gold exchange standard), a settlement with Soviet Russia (tsarist debts, nationalised foreign property), and, in the background, war debts and reparations.
  • War debts: Britain wanted the USA to give concessions on the Allies' war debts. The USA did not attend, so nothing could be settled on war debts. (In August 1922 Britain's Balfour Note said that Britain would collect from its debtors only as much as it had to pay the USA.)
  • Reparations ("war fines"): Germany owed most of its reparations to France, Britain and Belgium and wanted them reduced. France and Belgium refused; France would not let the conference revise the Versailles settlement †.
  • Rapallo: on 16 April 1922, on the sidelines, Germany and Soviet Russia signed the Treaty of Rapallo: diplomatic relations, mutual cancellation of claims and economic cooperation. It shocked the Western powers, and the conference ended without agreement.

[Correction] The notes call it the "Geneva conference, 1922". It was the Genoa (Italy) Economic and Financial Conference of April-May 1922. Geneva (Switzerland) was the seat of the League of Nations; the Geneva Protocol (1924) and the Geneva Disarmament Conference (1932-34) are different events.

13.3.4 The occupation of the Ruhr (January 1923 - August 1925) and hyperinflation

  • Germany threatened to stop payments and fell behind on deliveries in kind (timber, coal). When the Reparation Commission declared Germany in default, France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr, Germany's main coal and steel region, on 11 January 1923 †, to take payment directly.
  • The German government ordered passive resistance: workers and officials refused to cooperate, so industrial production in the Ruhr stopped. The government paid the idle workers by printing money.
  • Result: hyperinflation. By November 1923 a US dollar cost about 4.2 trillion marks †; savings were wiped out and the economy collapsed. The crisis also produced Hitler's failed Beer Hall Putsch in Munich (November 1923). [Additional]
  • Stresemann (Chancellor, then Foreign Minister) ended passive resistance (September 1923) and stabilised the currency with the Rentenmark (November 1923) †. [Additional]

[Correction] The notes run Genoa directly into the Ruhr ("Germany threatened to stop payments; France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr; Germans boycotted industrial production"). The trigger was the Reparation Commission's formal declaration of German default (on deliveries in kind) at the turn of 1922-23, and the "boycott" was a government-ordered passive resistance, financed by printing money; that, not the occupation alone, turned inflation into hyperinflation. The occupation lasted from January 1923 to August 1925.

13.3.5 The Dawes Plan (1924)

  • Who: a committee of experts under the American banker Charles G. Dawes, set up by the Reparation Commission; proposed in April 1924, in force from 1 September 1924.
  • Terms: Germany's annual payments were reduced at first and raised gradually as its economy recovered (from about 1 billion marks to 2.5 billion a year by 1928) †; the total was left undetermined; the Reichsbank was reorganised under foreign supervision and the Reichsmark adopted; France and Belgium would leave the Ruhr; and new foreign loans were given to Germany, starting with a $200 million loan floated by J.P. Morgan.
  • The circular flow: American loans to Germany, so Germany's economy revived and it paid reparations to France, Britain and Belgium, so their economies improved and they repaid their war debts to the USA, which lent again (Figure 2).
  • Success: the plan worked for a few years. France ended the Ruhr occupation in 1925, Europe enjoyed a period of economic prosperity (the German "Golden Twenties", 1924-29), and optimism returned. Dawes shared the Nobel Peace Prize for 1925.

[Correction] The notes say the Dawes Plan "gave a moratorium of 2 years to Germany". It did not suspend payments; it reduced and rescheduled them, with smaller annual instalments at first that rose over about five years. The best-known moratorium is the later Hoover Moratorium (June 1931), a one-year suspension of all reparation and war-debt payments.

13.3.6 The Locarno Treaties (1925) and Germany in the League (1926)

  • The economic recovery led to the Locarno Conference (Locarno, Switzerland, 5-16 October 1925). Seven treaties were initialled there on 16 October and formally signed in London on 1 December 1925.
  • Parties: Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Italy, Poland and Czechoslovakia. Key figures: Stresemann (Germany), Briand (France), Austen Chamberlain (Britain).
  • Rhineland Pact: Germany, France and Belgium accepted their western borders as fixed at Versailles (Germany gave up claims to Alsace-Lorraine) and the demilitarised Rhineland, and promised not to go to war over them; Britain and Italy were guarantors.
  • The east: Germany signed only arbitration treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia. It did not guarantee its eastern borders, because it was dissatisfied with the loss of the Polish Corridor and Upper Silesia; France gave Poland and Czechoslovakia separate guarantees.
  • The "spirit of Locarno" brought Germany into the League of Nations in September 1926 with a permanent seat on the Council. Stresemann, Briand and Chamberlain received Nobel Peace Prizes (1925-26).
  • [Additional] A.J.P. Taylor: "Its signature ended the First World War; its repudiation eleven years later marked the prelude to the Second" - Hitler marched into the Rhineland on 7 March 1936.

[Correction] The list of Locarno powers in the notes leaves out Italy, which with Britain guaranteed the Rhineland Pact. "Agreed to respect new boundaries created after WWI" applies only to Germany's western borders; the eastern borders received no German guarantee, which is why Locarno is often called a "half-peace".

13.3.7 The Kellogg-Briand Pact (27 August 1928) [Additional]

  • Also called the Pact of Paris; proposed by French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand and US Secretary of State Frank Kellogg. The signatories (15 at first, later over 60 †) renounced war as an instrument of national policy.
  • It had no means of enforcement, but it later served as a legal basis for the charge of "crimes against peace" at Nuremberg †.

13.3.8 The Young Plan (1929)

  • Who: a committee under the American Owen D. Young (head of General Electric), which met in Paris from February 1929; its report of 7 June 1929 was adopted at The Hague conferences (1929-30) and came into force in 1930.
  • Terms: the reparations total was fixed and reduced, to about 121 billion Reichsmarks (Britannica; other accounts give about 112 billion), payable in 59 annual instalments (to 1988); annual payments were about 20% lower than under Dawes; foreign controls over the German economy ended; a Bank for International Settlements (Basel) was created; and the Allies left the Rhineland in 1930, five years early.
  • Germany's nationalists, including Hitler's Nazis, campaigned against this "enslavement for two generations"; the campaign gave Hitler national publicity †.

[Correction] The notes say the Young Plan "reduced Germany's war fines by 20%". The 20% reduction refers to the annual payments compared with the Dawes annuities. The total fell from the 132 billion gold marks of the 1921 London Schedule to about 121 billion (or 112 billion, depending on the source and on whether interest is included), a cut of roughly 8-15%. "Over 59 years" is right: 59 annuities, ending in 1988.

13.3.9 The end of reparations: Hoover Moratorium (1931) and Lausanne (1932) [Additional]

  • The Depression made payment impossible. In June 1931 US President Herbert Hoover proposed a one-year moratorium on all reparation and war-debt payments.
  • At the Lausanne Conference (July 1932) the European creditors agreed to cancel reparations except for a final payment of 3 billion marks, which was never made; Hitler paid nothing after 1933. By mid-1933 all European debtors except Finland had defaulted on their war debts to the USA.

13.4 The Great Depression (1929) and the global economic crisis (1929-1939)

13.4.1 Background: an interdependent world economy

  • Advances in transport and communication during the inter-war period "shrank the world". The world powers became economically interdependent, and the economic health of the world depended on the well-being of all the major powers, especially the USA.
  • The USA was a creditor nation: by 1929 American factories turned out nearly half of the world's industrial goods, and the growing prosperity of Europe was built on American loans (Figure 2). The world economy was "like a delicately balanced house of cards", and American prosperity was the card that held up the rest.

13.4.2 Causes: a flawed US economy

Despite the boom of the 1920s ("the Roaring Twenties"), three weaknesses caused serious problems:

  1. Uneven distribution of wealth. The richest 5% of Americans received 33% of all personal income in 1929, while 60% of families earned less than $2,000 a year. Most families were too poor to buy the goods being produced.
  2. Overproduction by industry and agriculture. Unsold goods made shops cut orders, factories cut production and laid off workers, and families then bought even less: a downward spiral. On the farms, scientific methods and machinery raised yields, while competition from Australia, Latin America and Europe created a world surplus; prices fell, farmers could not repay bank loans, and rural banks failed.
  3. Falling demand for consumer goods, much of which had been bought on credit.

[Additional] Two more weaknesses are usually added: speculation on the stock market with borrowed money, and a fragile banking system of thousands of small banks with no deposit insurance.

13.4.3 The Wall Street Crash (October 1929)

  • Wall Street in New York was the financial capital of the world. Optimism drove share prices up, and many middle-income people bought shares "on margin": they paid a small part of the price and borrowed the rest from a stockbroker. This worked only while prices rose.
  • In September 1929 some investors began to sell, believing prices were too high. On Thursday 24 October 1929 ("Black Thursday") the slide became a panic; on Tuesday 29 October 1929 ("Black Tuesday") the market collapsed. Everyone wanted to sell and no one wanted to buy; billions of dollars of "paper wealth" vanished.
  • The Depression has been called "the largest earthquake ever to be measured on the economic historian's Richter scale".
  • Effects in the USA: by 1932 factory production was cut in half; thousands of businesses and banks failed (about 9,000 banks between 1930 and 1933 †); around 9 million people lost their savings; farmers lost their land; and by 1933 one-fourth of American workers had no jobs.

13.4.4 How the crisis became global

  • Loans recalled: American banks stopped lending to Europe and demanded repayment of their loans; American investors withdrew their money.
  • Tariffs: the US Congress raised import duties (the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, June 1930) to keep dollars at home. Other nations retaliated with their own tariffs ("beggar-thy-neighbour"), and countries that depended on exports to the USA suffered.
  • World trade collapsed: the value of world trade fell by about two-thirds between 1929 and 1933 (League of Nations figures for 75 countries, in gold dollars; part of the fall was in prices, part in the volume of goods).
  • Central Europe: because of war debts and dependence on American loans, Germany and Austria were hit hardest. In May 1931 Austria's largest bank, the Creditanstalt, failed; the panic spread to Germany (banks closed in July 1931) and to Britain, which left the gold standard in September 1931.
  • Unemployment soared: about 20-25% in the industrial countries; around 30 million people were out of work worldwide by 1932 †. The jobless depended on charity and soup kitchens; some lived in shanty towns ("Hoovervilles" in the USA).
  • Primary producers in Latin America, Asia and Africa were ruined as the prices of wheat, rubber, coffee, cotton and other commodities fell steeply.

13.4.5 Germany and the political consequences

  • Germany was hit hardest. About 6 million Germans were registered unemployed by early 1932, an unemployment rate of around 30%.
  • The Dawes and Young plans failed; economic uncertainty produced political instability, as governments ruled by emergency decree.
  • The Nazis under Hitler used these conditions to their advantage: their vote rose from about 2.6% (1928) to about 37% (July 1932) †, and Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933. He eventually paved the way for World War II.
  • Elsewhere too, the Depression strengthened militarists and dictators: Japan seized Manchuria in September 1931, and democracy collapsed across much of central and eastern Europe.
[Additional] India link: the Depression hit India through falling agricultural prices: India's exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934 and wheat prices fell by about 50%, while the colonial government kept land revenue unchanged. Peasant distress fed the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-34). Britain's imperial preference (Ottawa, 1932) also covered India †. (For detail, see the Modern Indian History notes.)

13.5 Measures taken to counter the Great Depression

The crisis exposed the weaknesses of unrestricted (laissez-faire) capitalism and forced governments of every kind to intervene directly in the economy. Each country responded according to its political system, economic structure and social conditions.

13.6 The rise of dictators

13.6.1 General characteristics of the dictators

  • Men of strong ability and great will power; usually good writers (Hitler's Mein Kampf) and powerful orators.
  • Lovers of strict discipline; supporters of militarism and aggressive nationalism.
  • Used force and fraud to capture power, and curtailed fundamental rights in the name of the national interest.
  • Used religion as a political tool where it suited them (Mussolini's Lateran Treaty with the Pope, 1929 †); Stalin, by contrast, suppressed religion.
  • Goal: a single-party, totalitarian (or authoritarian) regime.

Totalitarian government: a government that establishes total, centralised control over every aspect of public and private life. [Additional] An authoritarian regime controls politics but leaves more of society and the economy alone; a totalitarian one tries to control thought itself (Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR are the classic examples; Fascist Italy coined the word but was less complete in practice †).

13.6.2 Fascism

  • Name: from the Latin fasces, the bundle of rods with an axe carried before Roman magistrates as a symbol of authority (and of strength through unity). Mussolini founded the Fasci di Combattimento in Milan in March 1919 and came to power after the March on Rome (October 1922) †.
  • Roots: the economic and social turmoil left by World War I, rapid industrialisation, and fear of socialism and communism, especially in Italy and Germany. The Great Depression of the 1930s created further fertile ground.
  • Promoted by Benito Mussolini (Italy) and Adolf Hitler (Germany; Nazism was the German, racial form of fascism). Fascism had no strict doctrine: its leaders were opportunists who shaped the ideology to their needs.
  • It presented itself as a bulwark against socialism and communism, and so won the support of conservatives, industrialists, landowners and the frightened middle classes.
  • Thinkers who influenced it: Georges Sorel (violence and myth as political tools) and Friedrich Nietzsche (the "superman", contempt for democracy). [Additional] Nietzsche's influence was largely through selective and distorted readings of his work; he was not a nationalist or an anti-Semite, so present him as "appropriated" by fascists †.

Key features of Fascism:

  1. Extreme nationalism; loyalty to the state and total obedience to its leader.
  2. Denial of individual rights; one-party dictatorship (anti-democratic and anti-liberal).
  3. Nations must struggle; peaceful states are doomed to be conquered: militarism, the glorification of war and an aggressive foreign policy.
  4. Promises of economic revival, restored national pride and punishment of those blamed for hard times.
  5. Faith in Social Darwinism: only superior people or groups survive; extreme forms such as Nazism stressed racial purity (racism).
  6. Opposes communism and socialism.
  7. Seeks to integrate and control capitalism to serve the state (anti-capitalist only to some extent).

13.6.3 Hitler's aims and the policy of appeasement

  • Hitler wanted to reunite all Germans in Europe in one Reich and to give them sufficient "living space" (Lebensraum) through expansion: Austria, the Sudetenland (the German-speaking border lands of Czechoslovakia), Poland and the other Slavic lands of the east (Map 1).
  • Policy of appeasement: the practice of giving economic and military concessions to a country in order to avoid an immediate military conflict with it. Britain (Neville Chamberlain) and France followed it towards Hitler and Mussolini in the 1930s.
  • [Additional] Main steps: rearmament announced (March 1935); Rhineland remilitarised (7 March 1936); Anschluss with Austria (12-13 March 1938); Munich Agreement (29-30 September 1938) handing the Sudetenland to Germany ("peace for our time"); occupation of the rest of Czechoslovakia (March 1939); invasion of Poland (1 September 1939) after the Nazi-Soviet Pact. Details are left for the class on World War II.

[Correction] The notes say Hitler planned to win Austria and the Sudetenland "through military conquests". In the event both were taken without war: Austria by the Anschluss (German troops entered unopposed, March 1938, confirmed by a controlled plebiscite) and the Sudetenland by the Munich Agreement signed by Britain, France, Italy and Germany (September 1938), a product of appeasement. Poland (September 1939) was the first state Hitler attacked militarily, and it started World War II.

13.7 Joseph Stalin (1878-1953): rise, dictatorship and totalitarian rule

Stalin presided over one of the most radical and brutal transformations in modern history. Under him the USSR changed from a fragile revolutionary state into a great industrial and military power, but at the cost of repression, mass suffering and the destruction of political and personal freedom. He did not merely inherit Lenin's system; he turned it into a fully developed totalitarian regime in which ideology, bureaucracy, violence and fear became instruments of absolute power.

13.7.1 Stalin's rise as dictator

  • As General Secretary of the Communist Party (from April 1922) he controlled appointments, placed loyalists in key posts and gradually neutralised rivals after Lenin's death (21 January 1924).
  • Trotsky was outmanoeuvred, expelled from the party (1927), exiled (1929) and finally murdered in Mexico by a Soviet agent (August 1940) †. Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin and other leading Bolsheviks were disgraced and later executed after show trials (1936-38).
  • He took control of the Politburo and the party. Fundamental rights existed only on paper, and single-party, single-candidate elections were an eyewash. By the end of the 1920s Stalin ruled unchallenged, having merged party, state and security apparatus into one centre of power.
  • The Great Purge (1936-1938): party leaders, Red Army generals, officials, intellectuals and huge numbers of ordinary citizens were arrested, tortured, shot or sent to the Gulag (forced-labour camps). Soviet records show about 680,000 executions in 1937-38 alone †. Terror became a permanent tool of government and social engineering, not a temporary tactic.

13.7.2 Why Stalin became a dictator

  1. The nature of Soviet communism: it rejected liberal democracy and made the party the vanguard of history. Lenin had already built the pillars of authoritarian rule: one-party dominance, censorship and coercion "to defend the revolution" (12.16.4 of the 17 Nov note). Stalin expanded them; for him dictatorship was a revolutionary necessity.
  2. Economic and industrial backwardness: the USSR entered the 1920s poor and backward. Stalin saw rapid industrialisation as the only way to survive among hostile capitalist powers ("We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years", 1931 †). Pluralism or gradual reform would mean delay and disaster.
  3. Physical and ideological threats: Stalin saw enemies everywhere, real or imagined: old revolutionaries, kulaks, intellectuals, ordinary citizens. Dissent, even hesitation, was treason; fear and suspicion became permanent.
  4. Fascism and the fear of a German invasion: Hitler's anti-communism and Lebensraum openly threatened the USSR. Only a militarised, disciplined and united state, Stalin believed, could survive.
  5. Appeasement by Britain and France: allowing Hitler to rearm, take the Rhineland, absorb Austria and dismantle Czechoslovakia convinced Stalin that the Western democracies wanted to turn Hitler east. The failure of the League and of collective security left the USSR alone; this led to tighter internal control, military preparation and finally the Molotov-Ribbentrop (Nazi-Soviet) Pact of 23 August 1939 to buy time.

13.7.3 Economic reforms: the command economy

  • End of the NEP (1928): Stalin ended the New Economic Policy in 1928 and introduced a command economy in which the state directed all production, labour and distribution.
  • "Socialism in One Country": he made building socialism inside the USSR the priority, rather than spreading world revolution (Trotsky's "permanent revolution").
  • Collectivisation: from 1929 agriculture was forcibly collectivised into large collective farms (kolkhozes) and state farms (sovkhozes). Private farming was eliminated and the kulaks (better-off peasants) were targeted as class enemies, deported or killed.
  • Famine: the disruption caused catastrophic famine in 1931-34, worst in Ukraine, where it is called the Holodomor ("death by hunger", 1932-33), and in Kazakhstan. Stalin presented the suffering as the "price of progress".
  • Planned economy and rapid industrialisation: the First Five-Year Plan (1928-1932) replaced markets with central directives and gave priority to heavy industry: iron, steel, machinery, coal, defence. The Second Five-Year Plan (1933-1937) continued this, with somewhat more attention to transport and consumer goods. By the late 1930s the USSR was one of the world's major industrial powers, at the cost of harsh labour discipline, forced mobilisation and the suppression of individual rights.

[Correction] The extra notes say the Holodomor "killed 6-7 million". The most detailed demographic estimate for Soviet Ukraine is about 3.9 million deaths (scholarly range about 3.5-5 million); the figure of 5.5-8.7 million (Britannica: "at least 5 million") is for the whole Soviet famine of 1930-34, including Kazakhstan, the Volga and the Kuban. Whether the Holodomor was genocide is still debated: Ukraine (2006) and many states recognise it as such, while some historians see it as a man-made catastrophe without specific ethnic intent.

[Correction] The extra notes date the Second Five-Year Plan "1932-1937" and say it "focused on consumer goods". The First Plan (October 1928) was declared fulfilled early, at the end of 1932; the Second Plan ran 1933-1937 †. Heavy industry and defence remained the priority; consumer goods got more attention than before, but not the main focus.

[Correction] "Socialism in a State" in the extra notes should read "Socialism in One Country", Stalin's doctrine of 1924-25 †, set against Trotsky's "permanent revolution".

13.7.4 Social control and ideological domination

  • A political culture of obedience and conformity. Education was free but state-controlled; youth were indoctrinated with socialist ideas.
  • Propaganda built a cult of personality around Stalin as the "Father of Peoples". Education, literature (socialist realism), cinema and youth organisations were instruments of indoctrination.
  • Religion was suppressed, private life politicised, and fear permeated daily life. Citizens lived under constant surveillance by the NKVD (secret police) and psychological control.

[Correction] The extra notes say propaganda glorified Stalin as the "Father of Nations". The usual Soviet title was "Father of Peoples" (also "the Great Leader", "Leader and Teacher") †. "Father of the Nation" is a title associated with other leaders, for example Gandhi in India.

13.7.5 The rise of the USSR under Stalin

  • The USSR became a great industrial, economic and military power. Its rise created fear in the West of world communism.
  • Because of Stalin's ruthless methods (industry in the Urals and beyond, a mobilised society), the USSR not only defended itself against the Axis invasion in World War II (from June 1941) but emerged victorious. Victory raised Stalin's international standing and deepened his domestic power.
  • After the war the USSR became the communist superpower, though at an enormous cost in human lives.
[Additional] How historians judge Stalin: the "totalitarian" school stresses terror and the continuity from Lenin; others stress the pressure of backwardness and foreign threat, and the genuine upward mobility for millions of workers. Either way, the human cost (famine, purges, Gulag) is not in dispute. Present industrial success and victory in 1945 alongside these costs.

The class stopped here. Mussolini's and Hitler's rise to power in detail, the failure of the League in the 1930s and the causes of World War II are expected in the next classes.

Mains Points

  • Era of progress with a dark side: technology shrank the world and made economies interdependent, but also gave dictators propaganda and air power (13.1, Figure 1).
  • Why peace was fragile: revisionist powers, a League without the USA, reparations and war debts, minorities in new states, fear of communism, failed disarmament and economic nationalism (table).
  • The 1920s efforts: naval limitation and Pacific stability (Washington), financial stabilisation (Dawes), political reconciliation (Locarno, Germany in the League), idealism (Kellogg-Briand). Their weakness: they depended on American credit and on German goodwill, and left the eastern borders open (13.3, Figure 3).
  • The circular flow of loans, reparations and war debts explains why a crash in New York broke Europe (Figure 2).
  • Causes of the Depression: unequal income, overproduction, speculation, weak banks, the gold standard and protectionism (13.4, Figure 4).
  • Responses: state intervention everywhere, democratic (New Deal, managed capitalism) or authoritarian (rearmament, corporatism, planning); the end of laissez-faire (table).
  • Depression to dictatorship: mass unemployment discredited democracy, especially Weimar Germany; Japan turned to conquest; economic nationalism became territorial expansion (13.4.5).
  • Fascism and communism: opposed ideologies but similar methods of rule; distinguish theory from practice (table, table).
  • Stalin: built on Lenin's one-party state but added the command economy, collectivisation, the cult of personality and mass terror; the result was an industrial superpower at a huge human cost (13.7, Figure 5).
  • Appeasement: rewarded aggression and convinced Stalin to deal with Hitler (13.6.3).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Genoa (1922 economic conference, Italy) is not Geneva (League headquarters, Switzerland). Rapallo is near Genoa.
  • Locarno guaranteed only Germany's western borders; the eastern ones had only arbitration treaties. Italy was a guarantor with Britain.
  • 5:5:3 is USA : Britain : Japan for capital ships; France and Italy were 1.75 each. The conference had nine powers.
  • Four-Power Treaty ended the Anglo-Japanese Alliance; the Nine-Power Treaty dealt with China.
  • Dawes rescheduled payments (no moratorium); Hoover gave the one-year moratorium (1931); Lausanne (1932) ended reparations in practice.
  • Young Plan "20%" = cut in annual payments, not in the total.
  • Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) outlawed war as policy but had no enforcement; do not confuse it with the League Covenant.
  • Black Thursday (24 Oct) and Black Tuesday (29 Oct 1929) are both part of the Crash.
  • The fall of world trade (about two-thirds) is in value; volume fell less.
  • Austria and the Sudetenland were gained without war; Poland was the first military conquest.
  • The Holodomor (Ukraine, about 3.9 million) is part of the wider Soviet famine (5.5-8.7 million).
  • "Socialism in One Country" (Stalin) v "permanent revolution" (Trotsky).
  • Second Five-Year Plan = 1933-37, still heavy-industry led.
  • Dictators "used religion": true of Mussolini (Lateran Treaty, 1929), not of Stalin, who persecuted religion.
  • Totalitarian (control of all life) is not the same as authoritarian (control of politics).

30-Second Revision

The 1920s brought cars, planes, radio, cinema and votes for women, but also air power and propaganda. Peace was fragile: a resented Versailles, a League without the USA, reparations and fear of communism. Efforts to fix it: Washington (1921-22, 5:5:3, Four- and Nine-Power treaties), Genoa (1922, failed; Rapallo), the Ruhr crisis and hyperinflation (1923), the Dawes Plan (1924, US loans in a circle of reparations and war debts), Locarno (1925, western borders only) and Germany in the League (1926), Kellogg-Briand (1928) and the Young Plan (1929). The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) broke the circle: loans recalled, tariffs, bank failures, trade down two-thirds, mass unemployment. States intervened (New Deal, managed capitalism, rearmament, corporatism, Five-Year Plans), and dictators rose: Mussolini, Hitler (1933) and Stalin, who ended the NEP (1928), collectivised (famine, Holodomor), industrialised and purged (1936-38). Appeasement let Hitler take Austria and the Sudetenland (1938); Poland (1939) meant war.

  3. c03 - The Washington Conference had nine powers, not three; 5:5:3 (with 1.75 each for France and Italy) limited capital ships, not total naval strength.
  4. c04 - Article XIX froze fortifications in specified Pacific possessions of the USA, Britain and Japan; it excluded Hawaii, Singapore and Japan's home islands.
  5. c05 - US oil exports to Japan were not a Washington treaty term; the treaties did not by themselves give Japan Pacific domination or cause Pearl Harbor (the 1941 oil embargo, Manchuria and the China war did).
  6. c06 - The 1922 conference was at Genoa (Italy), not Geneva.
  7. c07 - The Ruhr was occupied (11 Jan 1923 - Aug 1925) after a formal default on deliveries in kind; the "boycott" was government-ordered passive resistance financed by printing money.
  8. c08 - The Dawes Plan gave no two-year moratorium; it reduced and graduated the annual payments. The moratorium was Hoover's (1931, one year).
  9. c09 - Italy was also a Locarno power and guarantor; only Germany's western borders were guaranteed.
  10. c10 - The Young Plan's 20% cut was in the annual payments; the total fell to about 121 (or 112) billion marks over 59 annuities.
  11. c11 - Austria (Anschluss, March 1938) and the Sudetenland (Munich, September 1938) were taken without war; Poland (1939) was the first military conquest.
  12. c12 - The Holodomor killed about 3.9 million in Soviet Ukraine (range 3.5-5 million); 5.5-8.7 million is the estimate for the whole Soviet famine.
  13. c13 - The Second Five-Year Plan ran 1933-1937 and still put heavy industry first.
  14. c14 - Stalin's doctrine was "Socialism in One Country", not "Socialism in a State".
  15. c15 - Stalin's usual title was "Father of Peoples", not "Father of Nations".
