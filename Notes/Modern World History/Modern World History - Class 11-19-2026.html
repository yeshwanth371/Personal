14. Benito Mussolini (1883-1945) and Fascist Italy

Mussolini was the founder of Fascism and the first European dictator of the 20th century (in power from October 1922, a dictator by 1925-26). His regime became the model for authoritarian nationalism: the state glorified above the individual, dissent suppressed, democratic institutions replaced by a cult of the leader, militarism and mass propaganda. He rose by exploiting post-war chaos, nationalist resentment and the fear of socialism, and turned liberal, parliamentary Italy into a one-party state.

14.1 Early life and background

  • Born on 29 July 1883 at Predappio (Romagna, northern Italy) † into a lower-middle-class family: a socialist blacksmith father and a Catholic schoolteacher mother. A turbulent, aggressive childhood; expelled from school for violence †. Worked briefly as a teacher, then turned to journalism and politics. He had great writing and speaking skills.
  • Early on he was a committed Marxist socialist in the Italian Socialist Party (PSI) and editor (1912-1914) of its newspaper Avanti! ("Forward!") †, attacking capitalism, monarchy, the Church and militarism.
  • The break with socialism (1914): the PSI opposed the war, but Mussolini came out for Italian intervention, arguing that war would revive the nation. He was expelled from the PSI (November 1914) and founded his own newspaper, Il Popolo d'Italia ("The People of Italy", first issue 15 November 1914) †. He served in the army and was wounded in 1917 †.

14.3 Ideological evolution: from socialism to Fascism

  • War changed Mussolini: he now fused socialist mass-mobilisation techniques with radical nationalism and violence.
  • On 23 March 1919, in Piazza San Sepolcro, Milan, he founded the Fasci Italiani di Combattimento ("Italian Fighting Leagues"), a movement of war veterans, nationalists and anti-communists. Its members, from their uniform, were called Blackshirts (camicie nere); armed squads were the squadristi. The ideology came to be known as Fascism (from Latin fasces, the bundle of rods carried before Roman magistrates).
  • The movement became a party, the National Fascist Party (Partito Nazionale Fascista, PNF), in November 1921 †. In the elections of May 1921 the Fascists won 35 seats, standing in the government-backed "National Blocs".
  • Creed: "Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state." (Mussolini's formula of the mid-1920s †.)
  • Nature of Fascist ideology: it rejected democracy, individual liberty and Marxism, and promoted the absolute supremacy of the state, loyalty to the leader (Il Duce), militarism, discipline and obedience, nationalism and imperial ambition, and state-directed capitalism with private property retained. Where communism promised class equality, Fascism demanded national unity under an authoritarian leader (see the full feature list in the 18 November note, 13.6.2).

[Correction] The notes say "1919 - Mussolini launched party - Fascist Party". What Mussolini founded in March 1919 was a movement, the Fasci Italiani di Combattimento; it won no seats in the November 1919 election †. It became a formal party, the PNF, only in November 1921.

14.4 Mussolini's rise to power (1919-1922)

14.4.1 The "Charter of Demands": the Fascist Manifesto (1919)

The programme of the Fasci (drafted after the founding meeting, published in Il Popolo d'Italia on 6 June 1919; co-authored by the syndicalist Alceste De Ambris and the Futurist poet F.T. Marinetti) was radical, aimed at disgruntled veterans and workers. It was divided into political, social, military and financial demands.

[Correction] The extra notes list the 1919 demands as including "abolition of monarchy and establishment of a republic", "nationalisation of factories" and "land for peasants through compulsory requisition". The published text of 6 June 1919 (Italian Wikisource; ANPI) asks for a National Assembly to decide the form of the state's constitution (implying, not stating, a republic), the nationalisation of arms and explosives factories only, and the seizure of church congregations' property and war profits; it has no land-for-peasants clause — land redistribution was a separate slogan of the period. Of the whole programme only the corporative idea lasted; the rest was dropped as Fascism moved right.

14.4.2 Squad violence and elite support (1920-1922)

  • From late 1920 Fascist squadristi launched "punitive expeditions" against socialists, trade unions and peasant leagues, especially in the Po valley, often with police looking away and with money from landowners and industrialists [Additional].
  • Fascism thus gained legitimacy as the "bulwark against Bolshevism", winning the support of the propertied classes, the army, veterans and much of the middle class.

14.4.3 The Naples congress and the March on Rome (October 1922)

  • Who / what: at the Fascist congress in Naples (24 October 1922) Mussolini presented his demands, including his appointment as Prime Minister, and declared, "Our programme is simple: we want to rule Italy." Fascist columns (fewer than 30,000 men) then converged on Rome: the March on Rome (27-29 October 1922).
  • Why it succeeded: Prime Minister Luigi Facta asked for a state of siege, but King Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign it, fearing civil war and believing he could control Mussolini. On 29 October he invited Mussolini to form a government; Mussolini was sworn in on 30-31 October †.
  • Consequence: Mussolini came to power legally, as head of a coalition (the Fascists had only 35 deputies and about 10 Nationalist allies). The march was not the "conquest of power" Fascist myth later celebrated but a transfer of power within the constitution, made possible by the surrender of the authorities to intimidation.

14.5 Consolidation of the dictatorship (1922-1926)

  1. Fusion with the Nationalists (1923): the PNF absorbed the Italian Nationalist Association (ANI), a monarchist, imperialist group with support among conservatives, Catholics and business †. With them came the backing of royalists, industrialists, the military and veterans.
  2. The Acerbo Law (passed November 1923): the list that won the largest share of the vote, provided it had at least 25%, received two-thirds of the seats; the rest were shared proportionally. Its aim was a secure Fascist majority. This was the legal foundation of the dictatorship.
  3. The election of 6 April 1924: Mussolini's National List (Fascists plus allied liberals and conservatives) won about 65% of the vote and 374 of 535 seats amid violence, intimidation and fraud. It was Italy's last multi-party election until 1946.
  4. The Matteotti crisis: the Socialist leader Giacomo Matteotti denounced the fraud in parliament (30 May 1924) and was kidnapped and murdered by Fascist militiamen on 10 June 1924. Opposition deputies walked out (the Aventine Secession) but the King did nothing. In a speech of 3 January 1925 Mussolini took responsibility and began open dictatorship †.
  5. The "most Fascist laws" (leggi fascistissime, 1925-1926): a law of December 1925 made the Prime Minister "Head of the Government", responsible to the King, not to Parliament †; the press was censored; non-Fascist trade unions and strikes were banned; in November 1926 all other parties were dissolved, opposition deputies expelled and a Special Tribunal set up †. A secret police, the OVRA, operated from the late 1920s †. Fundamental rights were suspended.
  6. Society: Fascist youth organisations (the Balilla) indoctrinated children; a personality cult grew around Il Duce ("the Leader"). By 1926 Italy was a one-party dictatorship. From 1928 elections became a single-list plebiscite (Rocco Law).

[Correction] The notes say the elections "were manipulated and the Fascist party got 25% votes; became PM and banned other parties". In fact (a) 25% was only the threshold in the Acerbo Law; the National List won about 65% in April 1924 (by violence and fraud); (b) Mussolini had been PM since October 1922, so he did not "become PM" after the election; and (c) the ban on other parties came only in November 1926, after the Matteotti crisis.

14.6 Church and state: the Lateran Treaty (11 February 1929)

  • Who / where: signed by Mussolini and Cardinal Pietro Gasparri (for Pope Pius XI) in the Lateran Palace, Rome.
  • Background: the "Roman Question" since Italy took Rome in 1870; Popes had refused to recognise the Italian state and called themselves "prisoners of the Vatican".
  • Three parts: (1) a political treaty recognising the State of Vatican City (about 44 hectares) as a sovereign state under the Pope; (2) a financial convention paying compensation of 750 million lire in cash and 1 billion lire in 5% state bonds for the loss of the Papal States; (3) a Concordat confirming Catholicism as the state religion, religious teaching in schools and church marriage with civil effect.
  • Result: the Church recognised the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital and, in effect, the Fascist regime, lending it Catholic legitimacy. It was arguably Mussolini's greatest diplomatic success at home.

[Verification Required] The notes give the compensation as "90 million dollars". The treaty figure is in lire (750 million cash plus 1 billion in bonds, about 1.75 billion lire); a dollar equivalent of roughly $90 million is often quoted but could not be confirmed against a source in this session. The notes also say the Church "agreed not to criticise the Fascists": the Concordat kept clergy out of party politics and required bishops to swear loyalty to the state †, but it was not a blanket gag; the Church and the regime later clashed (for example over Catholic Action in 1931 and the racial laws of 1938) †.

14.7 Economic and social policies

  • A liberal start (1922-1925): the finance minister Alberto De Stefani, a classical liberal, cut taxes and spending, balanced the budget and liberalised trade; he was dismissed in July 1925 [Additional].
  • High-tariff policy: from 1925 the regime turned protectionist to shield Italian industry and agriculture (wheat tariffs of the "Battle for Grain"), and in the 1930s, especially after the League sanctions of 1935, towards autarky (self-sufficiency).
  • The "battles" (propaganda campaigns): Battle for Grain (1925; self-sufficiency in wheat, which rose but at the cost of other crops); Battle for the Lira (1926-27, "Quota 90": revaluation to 90 lire to the pound, which hurt exports) †; Battle for Births (1927; raise the population); Battle for Land (1928; land reclamation, e.g. the Pontine Marshes †).
  • Public works (roads, railways, land drainage) reduced unemployment, though much relied on spectacle over substance.
  • The Corporate State: a "third way" between capitalism and socialism. Employers and workers were organised in state-controlled corporations (22 corporations created in 1934 †); non-Fascist unions and strikes were banned; wages and conditions were set under state supervision. In practice it served industrial and military interests. In 1939 parliament was replaced by the Chamber of Fasci and Corporations †.
  • Depression response: the state rescued banks and industry through the IRI (Institute for Industrial Reconstruction, 1933) †, making Italy's state sector one of the largest in Europe outside the USSR.

[Correction] The extra notes date the high-tariff policy to 1922. In 1922-25 Mussolini's government followed a liberal, free-trade policy under De Stefani; protectionism began with the grain tariffs of the Battle for Grain (1925) under De Stefani's successor Giuseppe Volpi, and turned into autarky in the 1930s.

Mussolini also implemented a system of social control: censorship, youth movements, controlled leisure (the Dopolavoro) †, and a cult of Rome and the Duce.

14.9 The aggression of Japan and Italy, and the failure of the League

14.9.1 Japan in Manchuria (1931-1933)

  • When / where: after the Mukden incident (18 September 1931), a staged explosion on the Japanese-run South Manchuria Railway, the Japanese Kwantung Army occupied Manchuria.
  • It set up the puppet state of Manchukuo (proclaimed 18 February, established 1 March 1932) under the last Qing emperor Puyi †; Jehol province was added in 1933 †.
  • The League's Lytton Commission condemned the invasion; the League Assembly adopted its report on 24 February 1933 and Japan announced its withdrawal from the League (27 March 1933) †. No sanctions were imposed.
  • Why: the Depression hit Japan's silk exports; the army and nationalists wanted Manchuria's coal, iron and land, and a buffer against the USSR.

14.9.2 Italy in Ethiopia (1935-1936) and the League's sanctions

  • Pretext: the Walwal incident (December 1934), a clash at an oasis on the Ethiopia-Italian Somaliland border.
  • Invasion: Italian troops crossed from Eritrea on 3 October 1935, used aircraft and poison gas against soldiers and civilians, took Addis Ababa on 5 May 1936; Emperor Haile Selassie went into exile and appealed to the League at Geneva on 30 June 1936.
  • League sanctions (Article 16): the League declared Italy an aggressor (7 October 1935). From 18 November 1935 members applied an arms embargo, a ban on loans, a ban on imports from Italy and on some exports to Italy. But oil, coal, iron and steel were not embargoed, the Suez Canal stayed open, and non-members (the USA, Germany) went on trading. Sanctions were lifted on 15 July 1936; Italy was not even expelled.
  • The Hoare-Laval Plan (December 1935): the British Foreign Secretary Sir Samuel Hoare and the French Prime Minister Pierre Laval secretly proposed giving Italy most of Ethiopia in return for a truce. When it leaked there was public outrage and it was dropped, but it showed that the two leading League powers were not serious about stopping Mussolini.
  • Italy left the League in December 1937.

[Correction] The notes say sanctions "failed as Italy started trading with Germany" and that "Britain and France signed treaties with Italy" that betrayed the League. Two repairs: (1) sanctions failed mainly because they excluded oil, coal, iron and steel, the Suez Canal was left open, and non-members such as the USA and Germany continued trading (American oil shipments to Italy rose sharply), not because of German trade alone; (2) the Anglo-French move was the Hoare-Laval Plan (December 1935), a secret proposal that was never signed and was abandoned after it leaked. Britain did later recognise the conquest in the Anglo-Italian Agreement of April 1938 †.

Consequence: the aggression of Japan and Italy, and the League's failure to control them, encouraged Hitler to be aggressive. Britain and France, the key League members, put their own interests (and fear of war) above collective security, which undermined the League's authority.

14.10 Assessment and downfall [Additional]

  • Mussolini pioneered Fascist authoritarianism: centralised power, the suppression of democracy and the glorification of the state, delivered through propaganda, economic theatrics and imperial adventures.
  • It ended in war, humiliation and collapse: defeats in Greece and North Africa (1940-41); after the Allied landing in Sicily (10 July 1943) the Fascist Grand Council voted against him and the King dismissed and arrested him (24-25 July 1943). Rescued by German commandos, he headed the puppet Italian Social Republic (Salo) in the north, and was captured and shot by Italian partisans on 28 April 1945 †.
  • Historians debate how "totalitarian" Fascist Italy was: the monarchy, the Church, the army and big business kept a good deal of autonomy, so many describe it as less complete than Nazi Germany or Stalin's USSR [Additional].

15. Adolf Hitler (1889-1945) and Nazi Germany

Hitler built one of the most destructive totalitarian regimes in modern history. Rising from the humiliation of Germany's defeat in 1918 and the collapse of the Weimar Republic, he blended extreme nationalism, racial hatred, militarism and charismatic mass politics. Under him Germany became a police-terror state committed to expansion and racial extermination, and plunged the world into World War II and the Holocaust.

15.1 The collapse of Imperial Germany and the birth of the Weimar Republic (1918-1919)

  • Defeat: Germany's collapse in 1918 came from military exhaustion, economic breakdown, the Allied naval blockade, the entry of the USA (April 1917) and plummeting morale; the failed spring offensive of 1918 broke the army †.
  • Revolution: naval mutinies (Kiel, early November 1918), strikes and anti-Kaiser protests by the SPD (Social Democratic Party) and the radical left. On 9 November 1918 the Kaiser's abdication was announced and a republic proclaimed; Kaiser Wilhelm II fled to the Netherlands †. A provisional government (the Council of People's Deputies) was formed under the SPD leader Friedrich Ebert.
  • Surrender and Versailles: the new government's representatives signed the Armistice (11 November 1918) and later accepted the Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919): territorial losses, reparations, disarmament and the war-guilt clause (Article 231). To nationalists this was betrayal.

[Correction] The notes say "the Kaiser refused to surrender after the loss". It was not a question of surrender: the army leadership itself told the Kaiser the war was lost (September 1918). What Wilhelm II resisted was abdication; it was announced over his head by the Chancellor, Prince Max of Baden, on 9 November 1918, and he fled to the Netherlands (formal abdication 28 November) †. The armistice was then signed by the new republic's delegate, Matthias Erzberger, on 11 November.

  • The "stab-in-the-back" myth (Dolchstosslegende): the false claim that the army had not been defeated in the field but betrayed by civilians at home - socialists, communists, democrats and Jews. Spread by Hindenburg, Ludendorff and the right, it blamed the "November criminals" of Weimar for surrender and Versailles, delegitimised the republic and fuelled anti-socialist, anti-communist and anti-Jewish feeling. It became a central Nazi propaganda tool.

15.2 The Weimar Republic (1919-1933): a republic built on conflict

  • Constitution: drafted at Weimar (adopted August 1919). The President was head of state (directly elected for seven years, with emergency powers under Article 48); the Chancellor headed the executive. Universal suffrage (women included), civil liberties and proportional representation.
  • Weakness: PR and polarisation produced many small parties and unstable coalition governments, which collapsed frequently and governed inefficiently.
  • Two Presidents: Friedrich Ebert (1919-1925) and Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg (1925-1934). Twelve Chancellors governed between February 1919 and January 1933 (Bundesarchiv), heading 20 cabinets.
  • From the left - the Spartacist uprising (January 1919): the Spartacists (Spartacus League, which founded the KPD, the Communist Party, at the turn of 1918-19), led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht, tried to seize power in Berlin. The SPD government crushed it using the right-wing Freikorps; Luxemburg and Liebknecht were murdered (15 January 1919) †. The lasting SPD-KPD hostility split the left and weakened democracy.
  • From the right - the Kapp Putsch (March 1920): nationalists and Freikorps tried to restore authoritarian rule in Berlin; it collapsed in a general strike †, but showed the fragility of the republic and emboldened the radical right.
  • Weak roots: Imperial Germany had been authoritarian; the army, judiciary, civil service and conservative elites had little respect for democracy. The republic was born of defeat and stigmatised by the stab-in-the-back myth.
  • Economic crisis and social breakdown: war debt, reparations, the failure of the Genoa Conference (1922), the Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation (1923), which wiped out middle-class savings. Stability returned through American loans under the Dawes Plan (1924) and the Young Plan (1929), but this prosperity depended on foreign credit. The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) ended it: factories shut, banks failed, unemployment spiralled. The government looked helpless, and from 1930 Chancellors ruled by presidential decree under Article 48.

[Correction] The notes give the Weimar Republic as "1919-1934" with "12 chancellors and 21 governments". The republic is conventionally dated 1919-1933: it ended in substance with Hitler's appointment (30 January 1933) and the Enabling Act (March 1933); 1934 is the year Hitler merged the presidency into the office of Führer. The Bundesarchiv and Bundestag count 12 Chancellors (Scheidemann to Schleicher); the published cabinet list gives 20 cabinets (21 only if the revolutionary Council of People's Deputies of 1918-19 is counted).

15.3 Hitler's early life, personality and ideas

  • Born on 20 April 1889 at Braunau am Inn, Austria † - an Austrian German - in a lower-middle-class household; conflict with an authoritarian father (a customs official) and deep attachment to his mother - a problematic childhood.
  • Academic failure and rejection by the Vienna Academy of Fine Arts (1907 and 1908) † fostered resentment and insecurity. Living in poverty in Vienna, he absorbed pan-German nationalism, anti-Semitism and Social Darwinism - history as a racial struggle in which "superior" races must dominate.
  • World War I was the turning point: he served in the Bavarian army on the Western Front as a regimental runner, was wounded and gassed, and was a decorated soldier (Iron Cross, First Class, 1918) †. He interpreted defeat as treason by internal enemies, Marxists and Jews.

15.4 The Nazi Party and its radicalisation (1919-1925)

  • DAP to NSDAP: in September 1919 the army sent Hitler, then an army informant, to monitor the small German Workers' Party (DAP) in Munich; he joined it. In February 1920 it was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) - the Nazi Party; Hitler left the army (March 1920) to work for it full time, and in July 1921 became its leader, the Führer, with dictatorial powers in the party †.
  • Its members were called Nazis; its storm troopers, the SA (Sturmabteilung, 1921), were the Brownshirts (strictly, "Brownshirts" means the SA, not all party members). Oratory, street violence and propaganda built a mass following.
  • Nazism = Fascism + racism (a useful exam formula): Fascist methods and state worship plus biological racism and anti-Semitism at the core.
  • Beer Hall Putsch (8-9 November 1923): imitating Mussolini's March on Rome, Hitler and Ludendorff tried to seize power in Munich. It failed; Hitler was tried, sentenced to five years and served about nine months in Landsberg prison †. Lesson: power must be won through constitutional means - enter the system, exploit its weaknesses and destroy it from within.

15.4.1 Mein Kampf ("My Struggle", 1925-26): Hitler's programme

  1. Herrenvolk (master-race) theory: racially "pure" Germans as Aryans, the master race destined to rule.
  2. Führerprinzip (leader principle): the supreme authority of the leader.
  3. Racial hierarchy and anti-Semitism: Slavs (Poles, Russians and others) were "sub-humans" (Untermenschen) fit only to serve or be removed; Jews were cast as the arch-enemy of the race; Roma (Gypsies) and others were also persecuted.
  4. Autarky: economic self-sufficiency.
  5. Revenge for Versailles, and union of all Germans in one Reich.
  6. Lebensraum ("living space") through the conquest of Eastern Europe.
  7. Destroy communism (anti-Marxism), ultimately by invading the USSR.

[Correction] The notes group "Jews, Poles, Slavs, Roma, Gypsies" as one "slave race" with "two options - serve the masters or die". Two repairs: (1) Roma and "Gypsies" are the same people ("Gypsy" is the older outsiders' name), and Poles are Slavs; (2) Nazi ideology did not treat all these groups alike. Slavs were to be subjugated, enslaved or expelled to make room for German settlers; Jews were not a "slave race" at all but the principal racial enemy to be removed from Europe, which ended in the policy of extermination (the Holocaust). Roma were also targeted for genocide.

15.5 The rise of Nazism (1929-1933)

  • Why the Nazis stayed small in the 1920s: the success of the Dawes Plan and the "golden years" of Stresemann gave them little traction (2.6% in 1928).
  • The Great Depression (from 1929) derailed recovery: mass unemployment (about 6 million, some 30% of the workforce, in 1932) and economic uncertainty. Chancellor Brüning's austerity deepened it.
  • Political deadlock: parliament could not form majorities; from March 1930 Chancellors (Brüning, Papen, Schleicher) governed by presidential decree, hollowing out democracy; the elites' contempt for mass politics reinforced the idea that only a strong leader could save Germany.
  • Fear of Bolshevism: the growth of the KPD and street battles between Communists and Nazis terrified industrialists, landowners, the middle class and the army; as in Italy, Fascism gained legitimacy as a "bulwark against communism".
  • Propaganda: Joseph Goebbels ran mass rallies, radio, film, uniformed youth movements and rituals that projected Hitler as Germany's saviour; Hitler flew between rallies in the 1932 campaigns †. The stab-in-the-back myth and the promise of "work and bread", national revival and an end to humiliation drew voters from all classes.
  • Vote share: 18.3% (September 1930), 37.3% (31 July 1932), making the NSDAP the largest party (230 seats); it fell to about 33% in November 1932 †. The vote doubled between 1930 and 1932, but the Nazis never won a majority in a free election (Figure 2).
  • Appointment (30 January 1933): after Papen and Schleicher failed to build a mass base, Papen and Hindenburg's circle persuaded the President to appoint Hitler Chancellor of a coalition of the NSDAP and the conservative DNVP (with only three Nazis in the cabinet), believing they could "box him in". Hitler thus came to power legally, through a backstairs deal, not an electoral majority.

15.6 Seizure of power and the totalitarian state (1933-1934)

  • Reichstag fire and emergency decree: on 27 February 1933 the Reichstag building burned (a Dutch ex-communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested; the full story is still debated †). Hitler claimed a communist uprising was imminent. On 28 February Hindenburg signed the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties and allowing arrest without trial, censorship and police repression. Communist leaders were imprisoned; constitutional protections ended.
  • Election of 5 March 1933: held amid terror, the Nazis still won only about 44% †.
  • The Enabling Act (23 March 1933): passed by the Reichstag (with the KPD deputies arrested or excluded and the Centre Party's support), it let the government make laws without parliament for four years. This was the legal foundation of Hitler's dictatorship; he ruled by decree behind a façade of legality.
  • Elimination of opposition - Gleichschaltung ("co-ordination"): trade unions abolished (2 May 1933) and replaced by the German Labour Front (DAF); all parties except the NSDAP banned (law of 14 July 1933); the states subordinated to Berlin, ending federalism †.
  • Night of the Long Knives (30 June - 2 July 1934): code-named Operation Hummingbird (Kolibri) †, the SS murdered the SA leader Ernst Röhm and other SA leaders and rivals (including the former Chancellor Schleicher) †. It removed a threat inside the movement and reassured the army.
  • Führer and army oath: when Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934, Hitler merged the offices of Chancellor and President and became Führer und Reichskanzler; the army swore a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler, not to the state or constitution. A plebiscite (19 August 1934) approved it †.
  • Security state: the SS under Heinrich Himmler became an elite force loyal only to Hitler; the Gestapo (secret state police, 1933) arrested without judicial oversight; concentration camps (Dachau, March 1933) began for political opponents and grew into instruments of terror, forced labour and, later, genocide.

15.7 Economic policy: recovery for rearmament

  • Hjalmar Schacht (Reichsbank president 1933, Economics Minister 1934-37) financed public works and secret rearmament (Mefo bills) and controlled trade through bilateral deals (the New Plan, 1934) †.
  • Public works: the Autobahnen (motorways), housing and land reclamation; the Reich Labour Service (RAD), compulsory for young men from 1935.
  • Breaking Versailles: Germany walked out of the Disarmament Conference and the League of Nations (14 October 1933). On 16 March 1935 Hitler announced conscription and an army of 36 divisions (about 550,000 men, against the 100,000 allowed); the existence of the air force, the Luftwaffe, was revealed the same month.
  • Four-Year Plan (1936): under Hermann Göring, to make the army and economy ready for war within four years and achieve autarky (synthetic oil and rubber, "guns before butter"). It failed in its aim: in 1939 Germany still imported about a third of its raw materials.
  • Unemployment fell from about 6 million (early 1933) to under half a million (1938) - roughly 75% by 1936 - through public works, rearmament and conscription, but also through "invisible unemployment": women and Jews pushed out of jobs were not counted, and workers lost free unions, the right to strike and freedom to change jobs. The DAF's Strength through Joy (KdF) offered cheap leisure and the Volkswagen savings scheme.

[Correction] The notes describe the Four-Year Plan as "infrastructure developments". The infrastructure drive (Autobahnen, work-creation schemes) began in 1933; the Four-Year Plan of 1936 under Göring was a war-economy plan whose aims were autarky and readiness for war by 1940.

  • The notes say the army reached about 4 million soldiers by 1939. [Verification Required] The peacetime army was some 1.4 million by 1939 according to one teaching source, and the mobilised Wehrmacht of September 1939 is usually put at several million; the exact "4 million" figure could not be confirmed in this session.
  • The notes say Hitler prioritised the air force over the navy and that this was a strategic mistake because of its limited range. This is an interpretation, not a settled fact: the Luftwaffe was built mainly as a tactical air force to support the army, without a long-range heavy bomber, which hurt it in the Battle of Britain (1940) and over the USSR; the navy's large-ship programme (Plan Z, 1939) came too late †.

15.8 The racial state and the Holocaust

  • Indoctrination: schools, youth organisations (the Hitler Youth and the League of German Girls), press, radio, film, art and religion became tools of ideology; textbooks glorified militarism and "racial science"; churches were pressured to conform. Independent thought was replaced by loyalty to the Führer. Goebbels ran the Ministry of Propaganda (March 1933) †.
  • Women: pushed towards "children, kitchen, church" (Kinder, Küche, Kirche) and motherhood.

  • Nuremberg Laws (1935): Jews lost citizenship; marriage and sexual relations between Jews and "Aryans" were criminalised; Jewish identity was defined by ancestry, not religion.
  • Kristallnacht ("Night of Broken Glass", 9-10 November 1938): a state-directed pogrom destroyed synagogues, homes and businesses; about 30,000 Jewish men were sent to concentration camps - the shift from legal exclusion to open violence.
  • Ghettos and shootings: after the invasions of Poland (1939) and the USSR (1941), Jews were confined in starving ghettos, and SS death squads (Einsatzgruppen) shot hundreds of thousands behind the front †.
  • The "Final Solution" (1942-45): planned at the Wannsee Conference (20 January 1942) †; industrial-scale genocide in death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka and Sobibor. The Holocaust (Hebrew Shoah) shows how a modern state with bureaucracy, technology and propaganda can turn genocide into policy.
  • Historians debate whether the Holocaust followed a long-held plan (intentionalists) or emerged step by step from competing agencies and the radicalisation of war (functionalists); most now combine the two †.

15.11 Japan, China and the USSR (1937-1941)

  • Second Sino-Japanese War: after a clash at the Marco Polo Bridge near Peking (Beijing) on 7 July 1937, Japan launched a full invasion of China; Shanghai fell after heavy fighting (November 1937) and Nanking (Nanjing) was captured in December 1937, followed by the Nanking Massacre. The Nationalist government retreated to Chungking (Chongqing) (Map 2).
  • Clash with the USSR: Japan fought Soviet forces at Lake Khasan (1938) and on the Mongolian border at Khalkhin Gol (Nomonhan), May-September 1939, where Soviet and Mongolian forces under Georgy Zhukov encircled and defeated the Japanese (August 1939).
  • Consequence: Japan dropped the "strike north" option and turned south towards South-East Asia and the Pacific. Japan and the USSR signed a Neutrality Pact on 13 April 1941 for five years, so that neither faced a two-front war. It held until the Soviet declaration of war on Japan in August 1945 †.

The notes say Japan "attacked Mongolia, 1939". More precisely, Japan's Kwantung Army fought along the disputed border between its puppet Manchukuo and the Mongolian People's Republic, a Soviet ally under a 1936 mutual-assistance pact; the battle took its name from the Khalkhin Gol river.

15.12 India link [Additional]

  • Nehru and Fascism: in early March 1936 Jawaharlal Nehru, returning through Rome after his wife Kamala's death, refused a pressing invitation to meet Mussolini, because the meeting would be used for Fascist propaganda during the Abyssinian war (The Discovery of India). In 1938 he also declined an invitation to visit Nazi Germany and went to Czechoslovakia instead.
  • Solidarity: Nehru called for an Abyssinia Day, observed on 9 May 1936, in sympathy with Ethiopia. Nehru wrote that the Congress had been consistently anti-fascist and anti-Nazi, standing for Republican Spain, Czechoslovakia and China.
  • A different approach: Subhas Chandra Bose sought help from the Axis powers against Britain (he met Mussolini in the 1930s and Hitler in May 1942) † - see the Indian Modern History notes.
  • War: Viceroy Linlithgow declared India at war on 3 September 1939 without consulting Indian leaders; the Congress ministries resigned in October-November 1939 † (see the Modern History notes).

16. World War II (1939-1945) and the Rise of the Superpowers: Introduction and Causes

  • What: the largest, deadliest, costliest and most widespread war in human history, fought between the Axis powers (Germany, Italy, Japan and their allies) and the Allied powers (Britain and its Empire, France, and from 1941 the USSR and the USA, with China and others).
  • When: 1 September 1939 (German invasion of Poland) to 2 September 1945 (Japan's formal surrender in Tokyo Bay). In Asia, China dates its war of resistance from July 1937 (or even 1931) †.
  • Result: Allied victory.
  • Significance: the decline of the European powers that had dominated the world for the previous four centuries, and the rise of the USA and USSR as superpowers. The end of the war and the start of the superpower era intensified anti-imperial feeling in the colonial world and paved the way for decolonisation (India 1947 onwards).
  • Names: the Second World War; in the USSR and Russia the war of 1941-45 is the Great Patriotic War; in Japan it was officially the "Greater East Asia War" †.

16.3 Causes of World War II

16.3.1 Harsh and unfair peace treaties

The peace treaties of 1919-20 (Versailles with Germany, Saint-Germain with Austria, Trianon with Hungary, Neuilly with Bulgaria, Sevres with Turkey) and the resulting territorial changes and reparations satisfied the Allies, but left Germany and its former allies crippled, humiliated and dissatisfied. Italy and Japan, though on the winning side, were also aggrieved. These harsh decisions created new conflicts and helped bring another devastating war within two decades. The French Marshal Foch is reported to have said of Versailles, "This is not peace. It is an armistice for twenty years" †. (Details: 16 November note, 11.10.)

16.3.2 Extremist ideologies and dictators

World War I planted the seeds of political and socio-economic decline in Europe, which led to the rise of extremist ideologies and dictators with brutal regimes in Italy (section 14) and Germany (section 15), and militarists in Japan. These leaders preached violence, embraced militarism and encouraged aggressive nationalism, and directly caused the war.

16.3.3 The Great Depression

The Great Depression of 1929 in the USA caused a global economic crisis (1929-39) that maimed the already war-ravaged economies of Europe. It caused the failure of the Dawes and Young Plans, adding fuel to the fire, and the resulting economic uncertainty and political instability became stepping stones for Hitler (18 November note, 13.4). It also pushed Japan towards Manchuria and Italy towards Ethiopia.

16.3.4 The aggression of Japan and Italy

Japan occupied Manchuria and set up the puppet state of Manchukuo (1932); condemned by the League, it left in 1933. The League imposed economic sanctions on Italy when Mussolini invaded Ethiopia (1935); Mussolini survived them by trading with non-members and was helped by the Anglo-French Hoare-Laval Plan, and then left the League (1937). The aggression of Japan and Italy encouraged Hitler to be aggressive (14.9).

16.3.5 The failure of the League of Nations

The League was an international association to maintain world peace and order, but its inability to control aggressive nations (Japan, Italy, Germany) and the betrayal of key members such as Britain and France, which put national interest before collective security, undermined its authority. The USA never joined, a huge blow; Germany (1933), Japan (1933) and Italy (1937) left, and the USSR was expelled in December 1939 after attacking Finland †. The League had no army and needed unanimity for most decisions.

16.3.6 Hitler's aggression

The League's inability to stop Hitler from violating the Treaty of Versailles convinced him to take great risks. He rearmed, remilitarised the Rhineland (1936), concluded alliances with Italy and Japan, and went on to annex Austria (1938) (15.9).

16.3.7 The policy of appeasement

  • Hitler's aggression shocked Britain and France, but they were unwilling to risk war and so appeased him. Reasons: memories of 1914-18 and public pacifism; military unpreparedness; the economic burden of rearmament during the Depression; a feeling that Versailles had been too harsh; reliance on the Empire and the Dominions, which did not want war; the belief that Hitler had limited aims; and fear of communism - some hoped to channel his aggression against the USSR.
  • They signed the Munich Agreement (29-30 September 1938) and allowed him to occupy the Sudetenland. Neither Czechoslovakia nor the USSR was invited.
  • By March 1939 Hitler controlled the rest of Czechoslovakia and took Memel; Mussolini occupied Albania (7 April 1939). Britain and France then guaranteed Poland (31 March 1939) †, abandoning appeasement.

16.3.8 The Molotov-Ribbentrop (Nazi-Soviet) Pact, 23 August 1939

  • Who / where: signed in Moscow by the foreign ministers Vyacheslav Molotov (USSR) and Joachim von Ribbentrop (Germany).
  • What: a ten-year non-aggression pact with a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe into spheres: western Poland to Germany; eastern Poland, Finland, Estonia, Latvia (later Lithuania) and Bessarabia to the Soviet sphere †.
  • Why Stalin signed: Munich and appeasement convinced him the West wanted to turn Hitler east; Anglo-French talks with Moscow in summer 1939 were half-hearted; the pact bought time and territory (18 November note, 13.7.2). Why Hitler signed: to avoid a two-front war while he attacked Poland.
  • Consequence (the immediate cause of the war): Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 after a staged "border incident" at Gleiwitz (31 August) †; Britain and France declared war on 3 September 1939; the USSR invaded eastern Poland on 17 September 1939.

16.4 Interpretations: who was to blame? [Additional]

  • Hitler's war (the orthodox view): the war followed from Hitler's programme of expansion set out in Mein Kampf and his secret statements (for example the Hossbach Memorandum, November 1937) †; the Nuremberg trials took this view.
  • A.J.P. Taylor, The Origins of the Second World War (1961): Hitler was an opportunist who exploited the mistakes of others, especially the Versailles settlement and appeasement; a deliberately provocative view †.
  • Structural views: the war grew out of an unstable international system - Versailles, the Depression, the failure of collective security and the ideological rivalry of liberal democracy, Fascism and communism. Some Soviet-era writers stressed the Western powers' wish to turn Hitler against the USSR; Western critics stress the Nazi-Soviet Pact.
  • Exam balance: Germany (Nazi Hitler) bears the primary responsibility for the war of 1939 in Europe, but others created the conditions that made aggression possible (compare PYQ 1). These are interpretations, not settled facts.

Mains Points

  • Why Fascism triumphed in Italy: "mutilated victory", economic crisis, the Biennio Rosso and fear of socialism, a weak parliamentary system, squad violence tolerated by the state, and elites (King, army, business, Church) who thought they could use Mussolini (Table 14.1, Figure 1).
  • The "legal" road to dictatorship in both Italy and Germany: appointment by the head of state, then electoral manipulation (Acerbo Law; Enabling Act), terror (Matteotti; Night of the Long Knives) and co-ordination of institutions (14.5, 15.6, Figure 3).
  • Programme versus practice: the radical 1919 manifesto was dropped for order, monarchy, Church and property (Table 14.2).
  • Weimar's weakness: born of defeat, burdened with Versailles and the stab-in-the-back myth, PR coalitions, Article 48, enemies on left and right, and dependence on American credit (15.2).
  • Depression to dictatorship: the Nazi vote tracked unemployment; conservative elites handed power to Hitler (15.5, Figure 2).
  • The totalitarian racial state: SS, Gestapo, camps, propaganda, youth, and the escalation from Nuremberg Laws to genocide (15.8).
  • The failure of collective security: Manchuria and Ethiopia exposed the League; Hoare-Laval showed that Britain and France would not enforce it (14.9).
  • Appeasement: reasons (fear of war, unpreparedness, guilt over Versailles, anti-communism) and consequences (Munich, the loss of Czechoslovakia's defences, Stalin's turn to Hitler) (16.3.7, Figure 4).
  • Causes of World War II as long-term, intermediate and immediate; Germany primarily responsible for 1939 in Europe, but within a system others helped break (Table 16.2, Figure 5, 16.4).
  • India link: the Congress's anti-fascism (Abyssinia Day, Spain, Czechoslovakia, China) and Nehru's refusal to meet Mussolini; India dragged into war in 1939 (15.12).

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Benito Mussolini (1883-1945): founder of Fascism; Italian PM 1922-43, dictator ("Il Duce").
  • Victor Emmanuel III (r. 1900-1946): King of Italy who appointed Mussolini in 1922 and dismissed him in 1943.
  • Gabriele D'Annunzio: nationalist poet; "mutilated victory"; seized Fiume 1919-20.
  • Giacomo Matteotti: Socialist deputy murdered by Fascists, 10 June 1924.
  • Giacomo Acerbo: author of the 1923 electoral law.
  • Alberto De Stefani: liberal finance minister, 1922-25.
  • Pius XI / Cardinal Pietro Gasparri: Pope and his Secretary of State at the Lateran Treaty, 1929.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Fasci (1919) vs PNF (1921): movement first, party later.
  • Acerbo Law threshold 25% vs the actual 1924 result of about 65%.
  • Mussolini PM (1922) vs dictator (1925-26): he was appointed legally; dictatorship came after Matteotti.
  • Treaty of London (1915) (Italy joins the Allies) vs Treaty of Rome (1924) (Fiume) vs Treaty of Rapallo: there are two Rapallos - Italy-Yugoslavia (November 1920, Fiume a Free State) and Germany-Soviet Russia (April 1922).
  • Lateran Treaty (1929) (Italy-Vatican) vs the Reichskonkordat (1933) (Germany-Vatican).
  • Hoare-Laval Plan was a proposal, never signed; the Stresa Front (1935) was against Germany.
  • Anti-Comintern Pact: Germany-Japan 1936; Italy joined 1937. Rome-Berlin Axis (1936) is not a military alliance; the Pact of Steel (1939) is.
  • Italy entered WWII on 10 June 1940, before the Tripartite Pact (27 September 1940).
  • Weimar Republic 1919-1933, not 1934; Hindenburg died in 1934.
  • Reichstag Fire Decree (28 February 1933: rights suspended) vs Enabling Act (23 March 1933: laws without parliament).
  • SA vs SS: the SA (Brownshirts, Röhm) was purged by the SS (Himmler) in 1934.
  • Four-Year Plan (1936) = autarky and war readiness; the Autobahn and work-creation schemes started in 1933.
  • Memel was taken by ultimatum (1939), not conquest; the Saar returned by plebiscite (1935), legally.
  • Nuremberg Laws (1935) vs Nuremberg Trials (1945-46).
  • Khalkhin Gol (1939, Mongolian border) vs Lake Khasan (1938, near Vladivostok).
  • Japan left the League in 1933, Germany in 1933, Italy in 1937; the USSR joined in 1934 and was expelled in 1939.
  • Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact = Nazi-Soviet Pact = German-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact (23 August 1939).
  • Exam directive: "Fascism" (Italy) is the genus; "Nazism" adds biological racism - do not use them interchangeably.

30-Second Revision

Italy: mutilated victory + Biennio Rosso + weak governments → Fasci 1919 → PNF 1921 → March on Rome 1922 → Acerbo 1923 → 1924 election + Matteotti → one-party state 1926 → Lateran 1929 → Ethiopia 1935-36 → Axis 1936 → Pact of Steel 1939 → war June 1940.

Germany: defeat + Versailles + stab-in-the-back → Weimar (PR, Article 48) → NSDAP 1920, putsch 1923, Mein Kampf → Depression: 18% (1930), 37% (1932) → Chancellor Jan 1933 → fire decree, Enabling Act → Long Knives, Führer 1934 → Nuremberg Laws 1935, Kristallnacht 1938, Holocaust.

Road to war: Manchuria 1931 → League exits 1933 → rearmament 1935 → Ethiopia 1935 → Rhineland 1936 → China 1937 → Anschluss and Munich 1938 → Prague 1939 → Nazi-Soviet Pact → Poland, 1 September 1939.

Causes: Versailles, dictators, Depression, Japanese and Italian aggression, League failure, Hitler, appeasement, Nazi-Soviet Pact.

Corrections Made

  1. c01 - Italy's Ethiopian defeat was at Adwa, 1 March 1896 (war of 1895-96), not 1894-95.
  2. c02 - "Mutated victory" should be "mutilated victory" (vittoria mutilata), D'Annunzio's slogan.
  3. c03 - Mussolini took office in October 1922; June 1919 - October 1922 saw six cabinets under four PMs (often counted as five), not "1919-23".
  4. c04 - In 1919 Mussolini founded a movement, the Fasci di Combattimento; the National Fascist Party (PNF) dates from November 1921.
  5. c05 - The 6 June 1919 programme asked for a National Assembly to decide the form of state, nationalisation of arms and explosives factories only, and had no land-for-peasants clause.
  6. c06 - The National List won about 65% in April 1924 (25% was only the Acerbo threshold); Mussolini was already PM; parties were banned in November 1926.
  7. c07 - Protectionism began in 1925 (Battle for Grain), after the liberal De Stefani phase of 1922-25, not in 1922.
  8. c08 - "Attacked Greece" (1923) was the Corfu incident: bombardment and occupation of the island, not a war with Greece.
  9. c09 - Italy entered WWII on 10 June 1940, before the Tripartite Pact of 27 September 1940.
  10. c10 - The Anti-Comintern Pact was signed by Germany and Japan on 25 November 1936; Italy joined on 6 November 1937; the 1936 Axis was not an alliance treaty.
  11. c11 - League sanctions failed mainly because oil, coal and steel were excluded, Suez stayed open and non-members traded; Hoare-Laval was an unsigned plan, not a treaty.
  12. c12 - Wilhelm II resisted abdication, not surrender; abdication was announced on 9 November 1918 and the new government signed the armistice.
  13. c13 - The Weimar Republic is dated 1919-1933, with 12 Chancellors and 20 cabinets (21 only with the 1918-19 revolutionary council).
  14. c14 - Roma and "Gypsies" are the same people; Nazi ideology meant Slavs for subjugation or expulsion, but Jews for removal and extermination - not one "slave race".
  15. c15 - The Four-Year Plan (1936, Goering) aimed at autarky and war readiness; infrastructure schemes began in 1933.
  16. c16 - Memel was ceded by Lithuania under a German ultimatum in March 1939 ("2939" is a slip), not conquered.
