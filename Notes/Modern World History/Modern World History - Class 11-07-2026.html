5.5.1 Revolutions within a revolution

Need: the demand for cotton textiles increased (at home and in world markets), so cotton production had to be mechanised. This set off a chain of innovations - first in textiles, then in power, mining and iron, transport and communication.

Note: the cotton gin was an American invention (Eli Whitney, 1793; patent 1794, US National Archives). It did not spin or weave; it made raw cotton cheap and plentiful for Lancashire - and, [Additional], it entrenched slave-worked cotton plantations in the US South.

(b) Power - from water to steam

  • The steam engine came first from Thomas Newcomen (1712), but it was inefficient and used mainly to pump mines.
  • James Watt improved it with the separate condenser (1765; patent 1769). With Matthew Boulton (from 1775) he built engines that could turn machinery.
  • Earlier machines were water-powered (water frame mills had to stand beside fast rivers); the steam engine freed factories from rivers and let them grow in towns and on coalfields. Gradually steam power became the driver of industry and hence of the Industrial Revolution.

(c) Mining and iron

  • The need to manufacture machines boosted mining and iron. Problems of deep mining (flooding, explosive gas) were met by the steam pump and the safety lamp (Humphry Davy, 1815).
  • Coal output rose roughly fourfold between 1750 and 1830 (about 5-6 million tons to 22-24 million tons) and about tenfold by 1850 (49-56 million tons) (Pollard, Economic History Review). Easy availability of cheap coal reduced the cost of metal production.
  • Iron: Abraham Darby's coke smelting (1709) and Henry Cort's puddling and rolling (1783-84) made iron cheaper and better; after Cort, British iron output quadrupled in 20 years. Pig iron rose from about 61,000 t (1785) to about 250,000 t (1805) and 2.25 million t by 1850 (Evans and Ryden).

[Correction] The rough notes say the steam engine and safety lamp increased coal output four times. The fourfold rise is about right for 1750-1830, but output rose about tenfold by 1850, and it was not due to these two inventions alone: Pollard also credits canals, the demand from iron works and the railways. The steam pump (from 1712) and the safety lamp (1815) made deeper mining possible.

[Correction] The rough notes say Abraham Darby found a new method of iron refining - the blast furnace - which quadrupled iron output. The blast furnace already existed in Europe from the 15th century. Darby's achievement (Coalbrookdale, 1709) was to smelt iron ore with coke instead of charcoal in the blast furnace. The quadrupling of British iron output followed Henry Cort's grooved rollers (patent 1783) and puddling (patent 1784), which refined pig iron into wrought iron (Britannica).

(d) Transport and communication

  • Linking raw-material sources to industries and markets needed better logistics and communication.
  • Richard Trevithick developed the high-pressure steam engine (patent 1802) and ran the first railway steam locomotive at Penydarren, South Wales, on 21 February 1804.
  • George Stephenson made railways practical: Stockton and Darlington Railway (27 September 1825), the Rocket (Rainhill trials, 1829) and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (15 September 1830).
  • Road transport improved through John Loudon McAdam's macadamised roads (Bristol, from 1815).
  • Modern communication arrived with the electric telegraph (Cooke and Wheatstone, 1837) and Morse code (Samuel Morse; first long-distance message, Washington-Baltimore, 24 May 1844).

[Correction] The rough notes say Trevithick's high-pressure engine powered boats, ships and locomotives (railways) first constructed by George Stephenson. Trevithick's engine powered the first railway locomotive (1804), which came before Stephenson. Steamboats were developed separately - William Symington's Charlotte Dundas (1802) and Robert Fulton's boat in the USA (1807) (Britannica). Stephenson's contribution was the first public steam railway (1825) and the first inter-city line (1830), not the first locomotive.

[Correction] The rough notes list the telegraph and Morse code among British developments. The British telegraph was Cooke and Wheatstone's (patent 1837); Morse code was devised by the American Samuel Morse (code 1835, patent 1837, first long-distance message 1844).

(e) The cotton chain reaction

This whole process - textiles → power → mining and iron → transport → communication - is called the cotton chain reaction: it started with cotton. Each solution created a new bottleneck ("challenge and response"). Most inventions of the second half of the 18th century were prompted by the rising demand for cotton textiles. The jenny, water frame and mule greatly increased output but could not meet demand while mills depended on water power; Watt's improved steam engine revolutionised the cotton sector, and the power loom and cotton gin also contributed. Because the Industrial Revolution transformed several sectors - cotton textiles, mining, iron, power, transport and communication - it is called "revolutions within a revolution".

5.5.2 It was all about cotton

  • Why cotton? Clothing is a basic human necessity, so demand was huge and elastic. British capitalists also wanted to counter the dominance of Indian cotton textiles (calicoes and muslins). [Additional] Parliament had earlier restricted Indian calicoes by the Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721 †.
  • Capitalists therefore invested most of their accumulated capital in cotton and hired large numbers of workers to maximise output.
  • The result was the cotton chain reaction (Figure 6): the British cotton industry propelled the Industrial Revolution, spurred technical advances, boosted mining and iron and improved transport and communication.
  • These developments placed Britain at the forefront of innovation, industrialisation and foreign trade. Hence historians say industrialisation in Britain was "all about cotton".

5.5.3 Rise of industrialism

  • Industrialism is the practice of investing huge sums of capital to finance industrial production for profit.
  • British capitalists invested most of their capital - and reinvested their profits - to expand the productivity of British industries.
  • This created the modern factory system: advanced machines, inanimate power (water, then steam), division (specialisation) of labour and wage labour under one roof and one discipline. [Additional] Adam Smith had described the division of labour in a pin factory in The Wealth of Nations (1776) †.
  • Unskilled workers (including women and children) gained jobs, while skilled artisans such as handloom weavers lost their livelihood. Artisans, handicraftsmen, master-apprentice networks and the regulatory guilds declined. [Additional] Machine-breaking by the Luddites (1811-16) † expressed this distress.
  • Gradually industrialists replaced mercantilists (merchants) as the main players in the capitalist system - the rise of industrialism (merchant capitalism → industrial capitalism; see the 7 Nov economic orders).

5.5.4 A series of transformations

  1. Economy: Britain's agrarian economy became an industrial economy; Britain became the "workshop of the world" (English Heritage), displayed at the Great Exhibition (1 May 1851 †).
  2. Britain's lead: Britain was about 50 years ahead of other countries in industrialisation [Verification Required: a common textbook approximation, no source opened]. Its power produced the era of Pax Britannica (c. 1815-1914) - a century of British naval and commercial supremacy and relative peace among the great powers (c08).
  3. Society: two new social classes emerged - the industrial capitalist class and the industrial working class (5.5.5). [Additional] Rapid urbanisation: Manchester and other mill towns, with slums, disease and child labour.
  4. Ideologies: widespread exploitation of the working class by the capitalist class led to communism and socialism (c09; 5.5.6).
  5. A divided world: countries that industrialised became strong and modern while others fell behind. Industrialised countries adopted imperial-colonial rule and exploited weaker countries, producing the conditions of the underdeveloped "Third World". [Additional] The term "Third World" was coined later by Alfred Sauvy (1952) †.
  6. Politics: the Industrial Revolution, together with Enlightenment ideas, promoted a new order and the democratisation of politics in the West. [Additional] For example, the Reform Act of 1832 and the Chartist movement (1838-48) † in Britain.
  7. Europeanisation: as industrialisation accelerated in Britain and spread to Europe (5.7), European domination and Europeanisation of the world became possible.

[Correction] The rough notes date Pax Britannica to 1814-1914. It is usually dated 1815-1914 - from the defeat of Napoleon and the Congress of Vienna settlement (1815) to the outbreak of World War I (1914) (Britannica summary; no source for 1814 was found).

[Correction] The rough notes say exploitation led to "communcalism and socialism". The intended word is communism. Communalism - politics based on religious community identity - is a different concept and should not be confused with communism in an answer.

5.5.6 Marx, Marxism and the socialist response

  • The Communist Manifesto - Marx and Engels, published in London, February 1848, for the Communist League. It ends: "Working men of all countries, unite!".
  • Das Kapital (Capital), volume 1 - Marx, published in Hamburg (Otto Meissner) in 1867 (14 September 1867 [Verification Required: day from secondary sources]; Britannica's summary page says Berlin - Hamburg is correct). [Additional] Volumes 2 (1885) and 3 (1894) were edited by Engels after Marx's death †.
  • Their ideas - Marxism - became the basis of communism and (revolutionary) socialism. [Additional] Marx called his method historical (dialectical) materialism: history moves through class struggle driven by material conditions.

Figure 7 - Marx's programme as taught in class. Marx's own phrase was the "revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat" (Critique of the Gotha Programme, 1875).

  • Workers were to unite and revolt against capitalist governments and capture political power through a (violent) proletarian revolution, then establish a dictatorship of the proletariat.
  • They would abolish private property rights - "the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property" (Manifesto, ch. 2 †) - ending capitalism and, in the class's words, imperial-colonial policies; with private property gone, classes would end.
  • Ultimate goal: world (international) communism - a stateless, classless, moneyless, non-religious, exploitation-free world of equality, community control and social cooperation. [Additional] Engels wrote that the state would "wither away" †; Marx called religion "the opium of the people" (1843-44) †.

[Additional] Capitalism and imperialism: Marx wrote on British rule in India (1853), but the full theory that imperialism is a stage of capitalism came later from Lenin (Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism, 1916-17 †). Keep the two apart in answers.

5.7 Spread of Industrialisation

Origin: Britain, from the mid-18th century (c. 1760), under George III (reigned 1760-1820, died 29 January 1820). From Britain, industrialisation spread first to continental Europe and North America, and later to Russia, Japan and - in a limited, colonial form - India. Late-comers often industrialised through the state (railways, tariffs, banks) rather than through private inventors.

Mains Points

  • "Revolutions within a revolution": show the challenge-and-response chain from cotton to power, coal and iron, transport and communication (Figure 6); use dates as evidence.
  • "All about cotton": leading sector, mass demand, competition with Indian textiles, raw cotton from slave plantations and India; link to India's de-industrialisation (5.5.2, Map 4).
  • Social impact: factory system, decline of artisans and guilds, urbanisation, new classes, child labour, then reforms and democratisation (5.5.3, 5.5.4).
  • Ideological impact: class conflict produced Marxism; distinguish Marxian communism from democratic socialism (table; c10).
  • Global impact: industrial power → New Imperialism → a divided world (industrial core and colonial periphery) → "Third World" underdevelopment.
  • Spread: early (Belgium, France, USA) vs late, state-led (Germany, Russia, Japan) industrialisers (table); India's colonial, enclave industrialisation.

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • James Hargreaves - inventor of the spinning jenny; Richard Arkwright - water frame and the factory system; Samuel Crompton - spinning mule; Edmund Cartwright - power loom; Eli Whitney - American inventor of the cotton gin.
  • Thomas Newcomen - first practical steam engine (1712); James Watt - separate condenser; Matthew Boulton - Watt's business partner.
  • Humphry Davy - chemist, miners' safety lamp (1815); Abraham Darby I - coke smelting; Henry Cort - puddling and rolling.
  • Richard Trevithick - high-pressure engine, first railway locomotive (1804); George Stephenson - "father of railways" †; William Symington, Robert Fulton - steamboats; John Loudon McAdam - macadamised roads.
  • William Cooke, Charles Wheatstone - British telegraph; Samuel Morse - American telegraph and code.
  • Cotton chain reaction; revolutions within a revolution - the linked sector revolutions started by cotton.
  • Industrialism; factory system - capital invested in machine production for profit; production in mills with wage labour and division of labour.
  • Guilds; master-apprentice system - pre-industrial craft organisations that declined.
  • Pax Britannica - British-led century of relative peace, 1815-1914.
  • Bourgeoisie / proletariat - capitalist owners / wage labourers (Engels' definitions).
  • Karl Marx (1818-83 †) - German philosopher and economist; Friedrich Engels (1820-95 †) - co-author and Manchester mill-owner's son †.
  • Communist Manifesto; Das Kapital - 1848 programme; 1867 critique of capitalism.
  • Dictatorship of the proletariat; world communism - transitional workers' rule; stateless, classless final society.
  • Dialectical / historical materialism - Marx's theory of history through class struggle.
  • Welfare state; democratic socialism - state provision of social security through elections and reform.
  • Third World - term (Sauvy, 1952 †) for poorer, mostly ex-colonial countries.
  • George III - British king 1760-1820; Leopold I - first King of the Belgians 1831-65; John Cockerill - British-born industrialist of Liege and Seraing.
  • Napoleon I - First Consul 1799, Emperor 1804-14/15; Napoleon III - Emperor 1852-70.
  • Count Camillo di Cavour - Piedmontese prime minister, architect of Italian unification, died 1861; Giovanni Giolitti - Italian prime minister (five times, 1892-1921).
  • Otto von Bismarck - German Chancellor and unifier †; Zollverein - German customs union, 1834.
  • Alexander II - "Tsar Liberator" †, 1855-81; Sergei Witte - Russian finance minister 1892-1903; Trans-Siberian Railway - Moscow to Vladivostok.
  • Samuel Slater - "father of the American factory system" †; Lowell - Massachusetts mill town.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Darby did not invent the blast furnace - he used coke in it (1709); the quadrupling of iron output followed Cort (1783-84) (c04).
  • Watt did not invent the steam engine (Newcomen 1712).
  • First locomotive = Trevithick (1804); first public steam railway = Stephenson (1825) (c05).
  • Morse was American; the British telegraph was Cooke and Wheatstone's (c06).
  • Water frame 1769, power loom 1785 (patent dates) (c01, c02); the cotton gin is American and is not a spinning machine.
  • Communism ≠ communalism (c09); welfare state ≠ Marxian communism (c10).
  • Manifesto 1848 (Marx and Engels) vs Das Kapital 1867 (Marx alone, vol. 1).
  • Pax Britannica starts 1815, not 1814 (c08).
  • Cavour died in 1861 - he did not lead 1870s industrialisation (c12).
  • Russia's take-off = 1890s (Witte), not Alexander II's reign alone; first railway 1837 (c14).
  • Meiji = reign name, not a dynasty (c15).
  • Old Style vs New Style: Russian Emancipation 19 Feb 1861 (OS) = 3 Mar 1861 (NS).

30-Second Revision

The Industrial Revolution had four features. (1) Revolutions within a revolution: rising cotton demand brought the jenny (c. 1764), water frame (1769), mule (1779) and power loom (1785); water power gave way to Watt's steam engine (1769); engines needed coal and iron (Davy lamp 1815; Darby coke 1709; Cort 1783-84); goods needed transport (Trevithick 1804, Stephenson 1825/1830, McAdam 1815) and signals (telegraph 1837, Morse 1844). (2) All about cotton: the leading sector, which also turned India from cloth exporter to raw-cotton supplier. (3) Industrialism: the factory system replaced artisans and guilds; industrialists replaced merchants. (4) Transformations: industrial economy, Pax Britannica (1815-1914), bourgeoisie vs proletariat, Marxism (Manifesto 1848, Das Kapital 1867: revolution, dictatorship of the proletariat, stateless classless world), imperialism and a divided world, democratisation. It spread to Belgium (Cockerill; 1830s), France (Napoleon III), the USA (Slater 1790), Germany (Zollverein 1834; 1871), Italy (after 1861), Russia (Witte, 1890s), Japan (Meiji, 1868) and colonial India (railway 1853, mill 1854).

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020 (General Studies Paper I)

Which of the following statements correctly explains the impact of the Industrial revolution on India during the first half of the nineteenth century?

  1. Indian handicrafts were ruined.
  2. Machines were introduced in the Indian textile industry in large numbers.
  3. Railway lines were laid in many parts of the country.
  4. Heavy duties were imposed on the imports of British manufacturers.
Answer: (a). Cheap Lancashire cloth flooded India after 1813 and ruined handicrafts (5.5.2, cotton figures). (b) and (c) came only after 1850 (Bombay mill 1854, railway 1853 - spread table); (d) is the reverse of British policy. Source: PMF IAS (compilation).

Who among the following wrote "The Communist Manifesto" along with Karl Marx?

  2. Friedrich Engels
  3. Robert Owen
  4. Max Weber
Answer: (b) Friedrich Engels - London, February 1848 (5.5.6). Owen was a utopian socialist; Durkheim and Weber were sociologists. Source: UnlockIAS (compilation); one other compilation prints a wrong answer key, so ignore "(c)".

Karl Marx explained the process of class struggle with the help of which one of the following theories?

  4. Dialectical materialism
Answer: (d) Dialectical materialism - history moves through contradictions between classes rooted in material (economic) conditions (5.5.6, 5.5.5). Source: AcmeIAS (compilation).

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2013 (GS Paper 1, 10 marks)

"Latecomer" Industrial Revolution in Japan involved certain factors that were markedly different from what West had experienced. Analyze.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]: set Britain's model (private inventors, cotton, gradual - 5.5) against Japan's: Meiji Restoration (1868), state-built pilot factories such as Tomioka (1872) later sold to private firms (zaibatsu †), borrowed Western technology and experts, land tax as capital †, railways (1872), and military-imperial aims (early vs late industrialisers, c15). 6. UPSC CSE Mains 2023 (GS Paper 1, 10 marks, 150 words)

Bring out the socio-economic effects of the introduction of railways in different countries of the world.

Source: UnlockIAS (compilation).
