9.5 Unification of Germany (1815-1871) - part 2: Bismarck, 1862-1871

Part 1 (Germany before and under Napoleon, the German Confederation, the Zollverein, and the Frankfurt Parliament of 1848-49) is 9.5.1-9.5.5 of the 13 November note. The lesson of 1848-49 was that liberal speeches and parliaments could not unite Germany. Unity came "from above", through the Prussian state and its army.

9.5.6 King William I of Prussia (r. 1861-1888)

  • Who: Wilhelm I, younger brother of Frederick William IV (the king who refused the Frankfurt crown in 1849). He was regent from October 1858, when his brother was incapacitated, and King of Prussia from 2 January 1861.
  • He became German Emperor (Kaiser) at Versailles on 18 January 1871 and died on 9 March 1888.
  • Goal: a Germany united under Prussia, achieved through a strong army (militarism). His army reform programme (1860-62) led to a clash with the liberal Prussian parliament, and that clash brought Bismarck to power.

[Correction] The rough notes call him "Kaiser William 1 (1861-1888)". In 1861 he became King of Prussia, not Kaiser. He took the title German Emperor (Kaiser) only on 18 January 1871. So 1861-1888 is his reign as king; 1871-1888 is his reign as emperor. Before 1871, "the King" is the right title (for example at Ems in 1870).

9.5.8 Making Prussia strong (1860s)

  • The army: William I, his war minister Albrecht von Roon (from 1859) and Bismarck modernised the Prussian army. When the liberal majority in parliament refused the money (the constitutional conflict, 1862-66), Bismarck collected taxes and spent them without a parliamentary budget †.
  • The general staff: Helmuth von Moltke the Elder was chief of the Prussian General Staff from 1857 (confirmed 1858) to 1888. He planned the three wars.
  • Railways were planned for fast mobilisation and for carrying troops to the frontier. The telegraph network linked headquarters with armies spread over long fronts.
  • The economy: the Zollverein (9.5.4 of the 11-13 note), Ruhr coal and iron, the Krupp works and the railways gave Prussia an industrial base that Austria lacked. This is why some historians say Germany was united by "coal and iron" as much as by "blood and iron" (see PYQ 4).
  • The needle gun (Dreyse breech-loading rifle) let Prussian infantry fire lying down and several times faster than Austrian muzzle-loaders in 1866 † [Additional].

[Correction] The rough notes say Bismarck "consulted Helmuth von Moltke and formulated the BLITZKRIEG strategy". "Blitzkrieg" ("lightning war") is a twentieth-century word. It first appeared in print in the 1930s and became famous in 1939-40 for Germany's Second World War campaigns with tanks and aircraft. It is anachronistic for 1866 and 1870. Moltke's own methods are better described as:

  • rapid railway mobilisation and concentration;
  • a war of movement (Bewegungskrieg);
  • armies that "march divided, fight united" (a saying traditionally attributed to him) and encircle the enemy (Kesselschlacht; Sedan is the classic case);
  • general directives to commanders in place of rigid orders.

These ideas were ancestors of Blitzkrieg; the strategy was Moltke's, not Bismarck's.

[Additional] What Blitzkrieg means (asked in class): a fast, highly coordinated, mobile style of warfare. Concentrated armoured (tank) and motorised forces, with close air support, break through at a narrow point and drive deep into the enemy's rear. They cut communications and encircle enemy armies before they can react. The classic examples are Poland (1939) and France (1940). It will be treated in detail with World War II.

9.5.9 Bismarck's policies

  1. Realpolitik (practical politics): policy decided by power and national interest, not by ideology, morality or principle. Alliances were made and broken as interest required: with Austria against Denmark in 1864, then against Austria in 1866.
  2. "Blood and iron": on 30 September 1862 Bismarck told the budget committee of the Prussian Chamber of Deputies:
    "Not by speeches and majority resolutions are the great questions of the day decided - that was the great mistake of 1848 and 1849 - but by iron and blood."
    He actually said "Eisen und Blut" (iron and blood); "blood and iron" is the popular inverted form. It meant unity through military power rather than parliaments (a swipe at the Frankfurt Parliament, 1848-49).
  3. Policy of war and isolation:
    1. Identify the enemies of unification under Prussia.
    2. Divide and isolate them through secret diplomacy and treaties, so that each enemy fights alone.
    3. Defeat each one individually in a short war.
    The three enemies were Denmark (it controlled Schleswig and Holstein), Austria (the rival for leadership of Germany) and France (the protector of the Catholic states of southern Germany, which would not accept a stronger Germany on its border).

9.5.10 War 1: the Danish War (Second Schleswig War), 1864

  • The duchies: Schleswig (mixed Danish and German population) and Holstein (German) were ruled by the King of Denmark in personal union. Holstein was a member of the German Confederation; Schleswig was not. The small duchy of Lauenburg went with them.
  • The crisis: Denmark's November Constitution, signed by the new king Christian IX on 18 November 1863, bound Schleswig more closely to Denmark. German nationalists saw this as annexation.
  • The war: the German Confederation carried out a "federal execution" in Holstein (December 1863) †. Prussia and Austria then acted together and invaded Schleswig on 1 February 1864. The Danish lines at Dybbol (Dueppel) fell on 18 April 1864; fighting ended on 20 July.
  • Treaty of Vienna (30 October 1864): Denmark gave up Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg to Prussia and Austria jointly.
  • Gastein Convention (14 August 1865): Prussia administered Schleswig, Austria administered Holstein, and Prussia bought Lauenburg. This arrangement was a ready-made source of quarrels.

[Correction] The rough notes say Denmark tried to annex Schleswig and Holstein "in 1861" and that the people asked Austria and Prussia for help. The trigger was Denmark's November Constitution of 1863 (signed 18 November 1863), and it concerned Schleswig, not Holstein. German national feeling in the duchies and in the Confederation did demand action. But Prussia and Austria went to war on their own account in February 1864, by-passing the Confederation's own plans, not simply at the people's request. The war is called the Second Schleswig War or Danish War (1864); its decisive battle was Dybbol.

9.5.11 War 2: the Austro-Prussian (Seven Weeks') War, 1866

  • The quarrel over Holstein: Bismarck increased Prussian military strength in Schleswig. Austria's Holstein was separated from Austria by Prussian-controlled and other German lands, so Austria could not easily reinforce it.
  • In June 1866 Austria referred the duchies question to the Federal Diet at Frankfurt. Bismarck called this a breach of the Gastein agreement, and Prussian troops marched into Holstein (June 1866) †.
  • The Diet voted to mobilise federal forces against Prussia (14 June 1866) †. Prussia declared the German Confederation dissolved (Bismarck declared the Diet "dead") and war began in mid-June.
  • Isolating Austria:
    • Italy: a secret alliance on 8 April 1866. Italy would attack Austria and receive Venetia.
    • France: at Biarritz (October 1865) Bismarck secured Napoleon III's neutrality, leaving him with vague hopes of "compensation". Napoleon expected a long war that would exhaust both sides.
    • Russia was friendly because Prussia had helped it against the Polish revolt of 1863 (the Alvensleben Convention) †. Russia was also still hostile to Austria after the Crimean War.
    • Britain stayed out of continental affairs.
  • Sides: most of the German states, including the larger ones, sided with Austria: Saxony, Hanover, Bavaria, Wurttemberg, Baden, Hesse-Kassel, Hesse-Darmstadt, Nassau and others †.
  • Battle of Koniggratz (Sadowa), 3 July 1866, in Bohemia: a decisive Prussian victory. The war lasted about seven weeks.
  • Peace of Prague (23 August 1866):
    • Austria accepted the end of the German Confederation and its own exclusion from German affairs.
    • Schleswig-Holstein went to Prussia.
    • Venetia went to Italy, via France (Peace of Vienna, 3 October 1866).
    • Bismarck gave Austria lenient terms: no Austrian land for Prussia, and no victory parade in Vienna. He wanted Austria as a future friend, not an enemy seeking revenge.
  • Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, the free city of Frankfurt and Schleswig-Holstein. This joined the two halves of Prussia into one block (Map 2).

[Correction] The rough notes say Austria "could not send troops into Holstein as it is land locked" and "complained to the German Diet over Holstein which is not part of Diet". Two fixes:

  • Holstein has a coastline. The problem was that it was geographically cut off from Austria by Prussian and other German territory.
  • Holstein was a member of the German Confederation; Schleswig was not. Austria's reference of the duchies question to the Federal Diet (June 1866) was what Bismarck used as his excuse to occupy Holstein.

[Correction] The rough notes say Bismarck "promised Belgium to Napoleon 3". No such promise is recorded. At Biarritz (October 1865) Bismarck only let Napoleon III hope for unspecified "compensation" in return for neutrality. After the war, the French ambassador Benedetti drafted a scheme (August 1866) for French annexation of Luxembourg and Belgium, which Bismarck kept, never signed and published in July 1870 to turn Britain against France †. The Italian alliance (8 April 1866) in the notes is right.

[Correction] The rough notes say "a few North German states" (Saxony, Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Hesse-Darmstadt) opposed Prussia, and that Bismarck "conquered all the opposed territories". Opposition was much wider: most of the Confederation, including the southern kingdoms of Bavaria and Wurttemberg and Baden, sided with Austria. Bismarck annexed only some of them: Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau and Frankfurt, plus Schleswig-Holstein. Saxony was not annexed (it joined the North German Confederation). The southern states kept their independence but signed secret military alliances with Prussia (August 1866) †. Hesse-Darmstadt lost a little territory, and its northern part joined the new confederation †.

9.5.12 The North German Confederation (1867) and Austria-Hungary (1867)

  • North German Confederation (1867-1871): all German states north of the River Main, under Prussian leadership. The King of Prussia was its president and commander of its army, and Bismarck was its chancellor. It had a Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage and a Bundesrat of the states' governments. Its constitution became the model for the Empire of 1871. It was made up of 22 states †, and its constitution came into force on 1 July 1867 †.
  • It was a predominantly Protestant block. The Catholic south (Bavaria, Wurttemberg, Baden, southern Hesse-Darmstadt) remained outside, linked to it by the Zollverein and the military alliances.
  • Austria-Hungary: excluded from Germany and weakened, Austria made the Compromise (Ausgleich) with Hungary, concluded 8 February 1867. It created the Dual Monarchy, with one emperor-king (Franz Joseph), separate Austrian and Hungarian governments and parliaments, and common foreign affairs, army and finance for both.
  • It was a multi-ethnic empire (Germans, Magyars, Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, Ruthenians, Romanians, Croats, Serbs, Slovenes, Italians). So nationalism, especially South Slav (Serbian) nationalism, was an existential threat to it. This matters for 1908 and 1914 (10.9).

The rough notes say the dual monarchy "was existential threat". The meaning is that nationalism was an existential threat to multi-ethnic Austria-Hungary, not that Austria-Hungary was a threat to others.

9.5.13 War 3: the Franco-Prussian War (1870-1871)

The Spanish throne and the Ems Dispatch

  • The vacancy: Queen Isabella II of Spain was deposed in the revolution of September 1868 †, and the Spanish throne was offered around Europe.
  • The candidate: Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, a Catholic relative of the Prussian royal house. Bismarck secretly encouraged the candidature.
  • French opposition: France feared encirclement by Hohenzollerns on two sides and protested strongly. Leopold's candidature was withdrawn on 12 July 1870.
  • Ems, 13 July 1870: the French government wanted more. Its ambassador, Count Benedetti, approached King William at the spa town of Ems. He asked for a promise that the candidature would never be renewed. The King politely refused and declined a further audience.
  • The telegram: the King's account of the meeting was telegraphed to Bismarck. Bismarck shortened (edited) it so that it read as though the King had snubbed the ambassador and France had been rebuffed. He released it to the press: the Ems Dispatch, published 13-14 July 1870.
  • Result: French opinion was inflamed, and France declared war on Prussia on 19 July 1870. France appeared the aggressor.
Spanish throne vacant (1868) → Leopold's candidature → French protest; withdrawn 12 Jul 1870 → Benedetti at Ems, 13 Jul → Edited Ems Dispatch, 13-14 Jul → France declares war, 19 Jul 1870

[Correction] The rough notes say the French ambassador went "to Kaiser" and that Bismarck's telegram "insulted the French Ambassador". Two fixes:

  • In 1870 William I was King of Prussia, not Kaiser (c01).
  • Bismarck did not insult the ambassador himself. He edited the King's telegram from Ems so that the exchange looked like a mutual insult: the King appeared to have snubbed France's ambassador, and the French demand appeared insolent. Bismarck said it would act like "a red rag on the Gallic bull" †.

The war and its results

  • France isolated: Austria-Hungary, Russia, Italy and Britain stayed neutral. Britain was alarmed by the Benedetti draft on Belgium (c07). Russia used the war to repudiate the Black Sea clauses of 1856 (October 1870) †.
  • The south German states honoured their 1866 alliances and fought on Prussia's side. The common war united north and south.
  • Battle of Sedan (1-2 September 1870): a French army was encircled and surrendered. Napoleon III was taken prisoner and held at Wilhelmshoehe near Kassel †.
  • 4 September 1870: the Second Empire collapsed and the Third Republic was proclaimed in Paris (1870-1940). Paris was besieged and surrendered in January 1871. The Paris Commune of March-May 1871 followed †.
  • November 1870: treaties brought Bavaria, Wurttemberg, Baden and southern Hesse into the federation.
  • 18 January 1871: William I proclaimed German Emperor in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, a deliberate humiliation of France.
  • Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871):
    • France ceded Alsace and about half of Lorraine (including Metz).
    • France paid an indemnity of 5 billion francs, with German occupation of north-eastern France until it was paid.
  • France after 1871: a humiliating defeat and lost provinces left France wanting revenge (revanche) and the return of Alsace-Lorraine. This is why Bismarck considered France Germany's most dangerous enemy (10.7).

[Correction] The rough notes say "France gave Alsace and Lorraine to Prussia" and that "France got isolated, by which Catholic states joined NGC". Two fixes:

  • By the Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871), France ceded Alsace and only part (about half) of Lorraine, with Metz. They went to the new German Empire as an imperial territory (Reichsland Elsass-Lothringen), not to Prussia.
  • The southern states joined because of their 1866 alliances and the shared war against France. Their treaties of accession were signed in November 1870, and they entered the German Empire, not the North German Confederation as such.

9.5.14 The German Empire, 1871

  • Form: a federal constitutional monarchy of 25 states † under the King of Prussia as German Emperor. It had a Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage and a Bundesrat of the states. The Chancellor was responsible to the Emperor, not to the Reichstag. So it was constitutional in form but authoritarian and Prussian-dominated in practice. Prussia had about two-thirds of the territory and population †.
  • Bismarck unified Germany with an iron hand and was called the "Iron Chancellor". He was Chancellor of Germany from 1871 to 1890.
  • He relied heavily on secret diplomacy: secret alliances with Italy (1866) and the south German states (1866); the edited Ems Dispatch; the secret Reinsurance Treaty (1887, 10.7).

[Correction] The rough notes call Bismarck the "Father of secret diplomacy". This is not a standard title. No reference consulted uses it. He is called the "Iron Chancellor", the master of Realpolitik, and the "honest broker" (his description of himself at the Congress of Berlin, 1878). It is fair to say that his alliance system was built on secret treaties. These treaties were a major cause of mistrust before 1914, but secret diplomacy itself is far older than Bismarck.

9.7 Impact of the unifications of Italy and Germany

  • Nationalism: the creation of two new nation-states (Italy, 1861-70; Germany, 1871) showed that the Vienna settlement could be overturned. It gave a strong impulse to nationalism across Europe, especially among the subject peoples of the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires (Poles, Czechs, South Slavs and others).
  • Leaders as models: Mazzini, Garibaldi, Cavour and Bismarck inspired nationalists across the world. India link [Additional]: Mazzini's ideas and Garibaldi's example were admired by Indian nationalists. Lala Lajpat Rai wrote biographies of Mazzini and Garibaldi, and V. D. Savarkar translated Mazzini's autobiography into Marathi (1907) †.
  • Economy: Cavour's economic reforms in Piedmont and Bismarck's industrial and tariff policies (the Zollverein base, railways, protection from 1879) laid the foundations for industrial growth after unification. Germany grew fastest; by 1914 it rivalled Britain in steel, chemicals and electrical goods †.
  • Rivalry for colonies: two new industrial powers entered the competition for markets, raw materials and colonies. This sharpened imperial rivalry (Africa, the Pacific, China) and made the industrial powers hostile to one another (10.1).
  • Balance of power: a united Germany in the centre of Europe, the strongest military power on the continent, upset the balance of power of 1815. France was humiliated and wanted revenge, and Austria-Hungary turned towards the Balkans.
  • Militarism and secret diplomacy: the success of "blood and iron" glorified the army and war, and Bismarck's system of secret alliances divided Europe into blocs. These planted the seeds of World War I (11.2).
  • The Papacy: the loss of Rome (1870) ended the Pope's temporal power; the "Roman Question" lasted until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 † [Additional].

10. The World Before the World Wars (1871-1914)

10.1 The Industrial Revolution, Europe and the world

  • Spread: the Industrial Revolution began in Britain (c. 1760s onwards) and spread in the 19th century to Belgium, France, Germany, Italy and Russia, to the USA, and, after the Meiji Restoration (1868), to Japan. These countries became politically, economically and militarily strong.
  • Science and technology improved transport and communication:
    • railways and steam-powered, iron and steel shipping;
    • new waterways: the Suez Canal (opened 17 November 1869 †) and the Panama Canal (opened 15 August 1914 †);
    • the telegraph (with submarine cables), the telephone (Bell's patent, 7 March 1876) and wireless/radio (Marconi's transatlantic signal, 12 December 1901).
  • Industrial needs drove expansion: raw materials (cotton, rubber, oil, minerals), markets for manufactures, outlets for capital investment, and coaling stations and naval bases. So the industrial powers adopted "New Imperialism" (c. 1870-1914): an intense race for colonies, protectorates and spheres of influence.
  • Scale: the rough notes say that by the early 20th century the colonial powers controlled about 84% of the world's land. This figure is widely quoted for 1914 (Edward Said gives "roughly 85 percent"). It counts former colonies such as the Americas, and it could not be checked against a primary source for this note [Verification Required].
  • India link [Additional]: British machine-made textiles hit Indian handicrafts (deindustrialisation). Railways (from 1853) and the Suez Canal tied India more closely to the British economy as a supplier of raw cotton, jute and wheat and a market for British goods. The Suez route cut the sea journey between Britain and India by thousands of kilometres †.

10.2 Pax Britannica (1815-1914)

  • Britain was the most industrialised, prosperous and powerful country, with the strongest navy and the largest colonial empire: about a quarter of the world's land by the early 20th century (the rough notes say 23%) †.
  • Pax Britannica ("British peace") names the century from the end of the Napoleonic Wars (1815) to 1914. In this period British naval supremacy and financial power kept the world's sea lanes open. Europe saw no general war, though there were many limited wars (Crimea, the wars of unification) and colonial wars.
  • It was an era of relative peace, prosperity, scientific and technological advance and optimism about progress. The decades from the 1870s to 1914 are often called the Belle Epoque ("beautiful era") in Europe [Additional].

[Correction] The rough notes date Pax Britannica as "1814-1914". It is normally dated from 1815 (Waterloo and the Congress of Vienna) to 1914. The notes' "Golden age of Europe" in the 1870s is better put as the Belle Epoque (c. 1871-1914). It was golden only for the European middle and upper classes; for the colonised world it was the peak of imperial conquest.

10.3 The rise of Germany and the Anglo-German naval race

  • Rise: under Kaiser William I (1871-1888) with Bismarck, and then William II (1888-1918), Germany became a great industrial, economic and military power. By 1914 it out-produced Britain in steel †.
  • Weltpolitik ("world policy") [Additional]: after Bismarck's fall (1890), William II wanted Germany to have a "place in the sun". That meant global influence, colonies and a navy to match, a challenge to British hegemony.
  • German colonies were not extensive and were economically unprofitable:
    • Africa: Togoland, Kamerun, German South West Africa (from 1884) and German East Africa;
    • the Pacific: German New Guinea, the Bismarck Archipelago, other island groups and Samoa;
    • China: Kiautschou (Jiaozhou Bay) in Shandong, leased for 99 years on 6 March 1898, with Tsingtao (Qingdao) as its naval base.
    Compare the British Empire across Asia, Africa, the Pacific and the Americas.
  • Naval race (1898-1914):
    • Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz's Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 began building a battle fleet in the North Sea to challenge the Royal Navy.
    • Britain launched HMS Dreadnought (10 February 1906). It was an all-big-gun, turbine-driven battleship that made all earlier battleships obsolete. Its name means "fear nothing".
    • Germany answered with its own dreadnoughts. The first, Nassau, was launched in 1908.
    • Britain then built larger "super-dreadnoughts" with heavier (13.5-inch) guns, beginning with the Orion class (launched 1910).
  • Result: by 1914, according to the Imperial War Museums, Britain had 29 dreadnoughts and Germany 17. Britain kept its lead, but the race poisoned Anglo-German relations and pushed Britain into the Entente with France and Russia (10.8). Naval rivalry was part of the wider militarism behind World War I.

[Correction] The rough notes say Germany "equalled the British Navy" and that "both Britain and Germany became super dreadnoughts by 1914". Two fixes:

  • Germany never equalled the Royal Navy. In 1914 Britain had 29 dreadnought-type capital ships and Germany 17 (IWM figures; the counts vary with whether battle-cruisers are included).
  • A super-dreadnought is a type of ship: a larger dreadnought with bigger guns, from Britain's Orion class (1910). Britain and Germany were building such ships by 1914; the countries did not "become" super-dreadnoughts.

The naval race ran from Germany's first Navy Law (1898) to 1914; the notes' dates are right.

10.4 Problems before the world (1): the Eastern Question

The Eastern Question to 1878, with a map, is in 9.6.1 of the 11-13 note (Map 5 there). This section adds what matters for 1914.

  • The Ottoman Empire was based in Turkey (Anatolia). At its height it ruled the Middle East, North Africa and the Balkans. Its decline in the 19th century came from wars with Russia and the rise of nationalism in the Balkans. The question of what would replace Ottoman rule, and who would gain, is the Eastern Question.
  • Russia supported Balkan nationalism. It helped Greece, Serbia, Montenegro, Romania and Bulgaria to autonomy or independence:
    • Serbia, Montenegro and Romania were recognised as independent at the Congress of Berlin (1878);
    • Bulgaria was autonomous from 1878 and declared independence on 5 October 1908.
    Russia backed Pan-Slavism, the solidarity of the Slav peoples under Russian leadership, and was the main patron of Serbian nationalism.
  • Serbia's goal was to unite the South Slavs (later called Yugoslavia, "land of the South Slavs") or at least to form a Greater Serbia. That needed Bosnia-Herzegovina, which had a large Serb population.
  • Austria-Hungary occupied and administered Bosnia from 1878 and annexed it in 1908. It saw Serbian and South Slav nationalism as an existential threat, because its own South Slavs (Croats, Serbs, Slovenes) might break away. So Austria-Hungary and Russia became rivals in the Balkans.
  • Germany was also interested in the Balkans and the Ottoman Empire, through the Baghdad Railway:
    • The concession for the line from Konya/Ankara to Baghdad was granted to a German company (1899-1903).
    • Linked with lines through Austria-Hungary, Serbia and Bulgaria to Constantinople, it would form a "Berlin-Baghdad" route towards the Persian Gulf.
    • Britain and Russia saw it as a German drive into the Middle East and a threat to the route to India.

[Correction] The rough notes say Germany "planned to build the Baghdad Railway (Germany - Persian Gulf) passed via Balkan states (Serbia and Bulgaria)". Three fixes:

  • The Baghdad Railway proper ran inside the Ottoman Empire, from Konya (later Haydarpasa, opposite Constantinople) towards Baghdad and Basra. The Ottoman government gave the Ankara-Baghdad concession to a German company in 1902-03.
  • The "Berlin-Baghdad" idea joined it to existing railways through Austria-Hungary, Serbia and Bulgaria.
  • The line was not completed before 1914. By 1918 it had reached only Nusaybin, far short of Baghdad, and it was finished in 1940 †.

[Correction] The rough notes list Bulgaria with Romania, Montenegro and Serbia among Balkan states that became independent with Russian help, and say Serbia's goal was "Yugoslavia". Two fixes:

  • The Congress of Berlin (1878) recognised the independence of Serbia, Montenegro and Romania. Bulgaria was only an autonomous principality under the Sultan in 1878 and declared independence on 5 October 1908.
  • The name "Yugoslavia" was adopted only in 1929 †; the state was formed in 1918 as the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. Before 1914 the aim is better called South Slav (Yugoslav) unity or "Greater Serbia".

10.5 Problems before the world (2): the Far Eastern Question - "cutting the Chinese melon"

The Opium Wars, treaty ports and spheres of influence, with a map, are in 9.6.2 of the 11-13 note (Map 6 there).

  • China was ruled by the Qing (Manchu) dynasty from 1644 to 1911-12 †. In the 19th century Qing China became politically, economically, socially and militarily weak: the Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion and unequal treaties.
  • The European powers, and later Japan, lent money to China and used the debt and the treaties to set up spheres of influence. A sphere of influence is a region of another country in which a foreign power claims exclusive economic privileges (railways, mines, loans, trade) without formally ruling it. So China became a victim of colonialism without being formally colonised (semi-colonialism). Chinese writers of the 1890s called this the "carving up of the melon" (guafen).
  • Japan and Russia both wanted Korea and Manchuria:
    • First Sino-Japanese War (1894-95): Japan defeated China. Treaty of Shimonoseki (17 April 1895): China recognised Korea's independence and ceded Taiwan to Japan.
    • Russo-Japanese War (1904-05): Japan defeated Russia, the first defeat of a European great power by an Asian one in modern times. Treaty of Portsmouth (USA, September 1905).
    • Korea became a Japanese protectorate (17 November 1905) and was annexed by Japan by the treaty of 22 August 1910, which took effect on 29 August 1910.
  • India link [Additional]: Japan's victory over Russia in 1905 inspired Asian nationalists, including Indian leaders during the Swadeshi movement †. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) also ended Britain's isolation (10.8).

10.6 Problems before the world (3): the Scramble for Africa

Treated in detail, with a map of Africa in 1914, in 9.6.3 of the 11-13 note (Map 7 there).

  • Africa had long been a continent of tribal societies and kingdoms, some of them large states (Ethiopia, the Asante, the Zulu, the Sokoto Caliphate) †. Europeans knew its coasts but not its interior, and called it the "Dark Continent", a Eurocentric label.
  • In the 19th century, explorers, missionaries, quinine (against malaria), steamships, railways and machine guns made conquest of the interior possible.
  • Brussels Geographic Conference (12-14 September 1876): called by King Leopold II of Belgium. It promoted "exploration" and set up the International African Association, the cover for Leopold's personal empire in the Congo.
  • Berlin (West Africa) Conference (15 November 1884 - 26 February 1885): hosted by Bismarck. It laid down rules for claiming African territory, mainly notification to the other powers and "effective occupation". It also recognised Leopold's Congo Free State. No African was present.
  • From paper to physical conquest: partition agreed on maps in Europe's conference rooms was followed by military conquest on the ground, mostly between the 1880s and 1914. This rapid partition is the Scramble for Africa.
  • By 1914 about 90% of Africa was under European rule; only Ethiopia (which defeated Italy at Adwa, 1896) and Liberia stayed independent.

[Correction] The rough notes say the Berlin Conference "encouraged colonisation" and date the conquest "1800s-1910s". Two fixes:

  • The Berlin Conference (1884-85) did not start the Scramble; claims were already being made. It regulated the Scramble, by notification and "effective occupation", so that the European powers would not fight one another over Africa. The effect was to speed up partition.
  • The Scramble is normally dated c. 1881-1914 (from the French occupation of Tunisia, 1881, and the British occupation of Egypt, 1882). It does not span the whole 19th century.

10.7 Bismarck's foreign policy and the alliance system (1871-1890)

  • Aim: Bismarck, Chancellor from 1871 to 1890, called Germany a "satiated" power that wanted no more territory †. He regarded France as Germany's most dangerous enemy because of its wish for revenge over Alsace-Lorraine. The goal of his foreign policy was to keep France isolated, without allies, and to avoid a two-front war.
  • League of the Three Emperors (Dreikaiserbund), 1873: Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia, three conservative monarchies (agreements of 1873; Germany joined on 22 October 1873).
  • Congress of Berlin (1878): Bismarck acted as the "honest broker" after the Russo-Turkish War. Russia lost much of its gains of San Stefano, blamed Germany, and the League broke down over the Balkans.
  • Dual Alliance (7 October 1879): Germany and Austria-Hungary, a defensive alliance against Russia. It lasted until 1918, the core of the later Central Powers.
  • Three Emperors' Alliance (1881): Bismarck drew Russia back in. It was renewed in 1884 and lapsed in 1887 over Bulgaria †.
  • Tunisia (1881): France and Italy both wanted Tunisia. France made it a protectorate by the Treaty of Bardo (12 May 1881). Angry Italy turned to Germany and Austria-Hungary.
  • Triple Alliance (20 May 1882): Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy. It was a secret, defensive alliance, renewed regularly until 1915.
  • Reinsurance Treaty (18 June 1887): a secret treaty with Russia. Each would stay neutral if the other went to war with a third power, except if Germany attacked France or Russia attacked Austria-Hungary †. It kept Russia away from France.
  • Fall of Bismarck: William II became Kaiser in 1888 and dismissed Bismarck in March 1890. Germany did not renew the Reinsurance Treaty in 1890. William II's attitude isolated Russia, and Russia turned to France.

[Correction] The rough notes say "Russia quit [the League] in 1878 over Balkan issues; now this led to the Dual Alliance 1879". The League did break down after the Congress of Berlin (1878), and the Dual Alliance (1879) followed. But Bismarck revived the link with Russia in the Three Emperors' Alliance of 1881, which lasted until 1887. He then replaced it with the Reinsurance Treaty (1887). Russia was finally lost only in 1890, after Bismarck's fall.

[Correction] The rough notes say the Triple Alliance "became the reason for the Central Powers in WW1". The Central Powers of 1914-18 were Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire (from November 1914) and Bulgaria (from October 1915). Italy, though a member of the Triple Alliance, declared neutrality in August 1914, arguing that the alliance was defensive and Austria was the aggressor. It signed the secret Treaty of London (26 April 1915) and joined the Allies in May 1915. The Dual Alliance (Germany and Austria-Hungary) was the true core of the Central Powers.

10.8 The Triple Entente (1894-1907)

  • Franco-Russian Alliance (1891-1894): an entente in 1891, a military convention on 18 August 1892, and ratification in 1893-94. Each would mobilise if a Triple Alliance member mobilised, and each would attack Germany if the other was attacked by Germany. France's isolation was over, and Germany faced the risk of a two-front war.
  • Britain leaves "splendid isolation": it formed the Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902). Then, alarmed by the German naval race (10.3), it settled its colonial quarrels with France: the Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904), by which Britain had a free hand in Egypt and France in Morocco.
  • First Moroccan (Tangier) Crisis, 1905-06: Kaiser William II landed at Tangier (31 March 1905) and backed Moroccan independence to test the Entente. At the Algeciras Conference (January-April 1906) Britain supported France, and Germany was isolated. The Entente was strengthened.
  • Anglo-Russian Convention (31 August 1907) †: Britain and Russia settled their "Great Game" rivalry in Asia:
    • Persia divided into a Russian zone (north), a British zone (south-east) and a neutral zone between them;
    • Afghanistan recognised as within the British sphere;
    • both to stay out of Tibet.
  • Triple Entente (1907): Britain, France and Russia. It was a set of understandings, not a formal military alliance; Britain had no treaty obligation to fight.
  • Second Moroccan (Agadir) Crisis, 1911: the German gunboat Panther arrived at Agadir (1 July 1911). Britain backed France again (Lloyd George's Mansion House speech) †. Germany accepted part of the French Congo, and France made Morocco a protectorate in 1912. The Entente held, and Anglo-German hostility deepened.
  • The two blocs:
    • Triple Alliance (1882): Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy ("GAI").
    • Triple Entente (1907): Britain, France, Russia ("BFR"). It formed as the reply, to restore the balance of power, and became the core of the Allied Powers in World War I.
    The alliance system divided Europe into two armed camps, so a local quarrel could drag in all the great powers.
  • India link [Additional]: the 1907 Convention ended the Anglo-Russian "Great Game" over the approaches to India (Afghanistan, Persia, Tibet). British fear for India's security was a main reason for both the Convention and British hostility to the Baghdad Railway (10.4).

[Correction] The rough notes call the Franco-Russian link the "Franco-Russian Entente (1891-94)" and the Triple Entente "a diplomatic alliance". The reverse is closer to the truth:

  • The Franco-Russian agreement became a real military alliance: the military convention of August 1892, ratified 1893-94.
  • The Triple Entente was only a set of diplomatic understandings (ententes) settling colonial disputes. It carried no binding military commitment for Britain.

The notes' dates for the Entente Cordiale (1904), the Moroccan crises (1905-06, 1911) and the Anglo-Russian entente (1907) are right.

10.9 The Bosnian Crisis (1908-1909)

  • Background: Austria-Hungary had occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina since 1878 (Congress of Berlin), though it was still formally Ottoman. It considered Serbian nationalism an existential threat (10.4).
  • The crisis: the Young Turk Revolution in the Ottoman Empire (July 1908) gave Austria-Hungary a pretext. Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina (announced 6 October 1908 †), and Bulgaria declared full independence (5 October 1908).
  • Response: Serbia and Russia protested. Germany backed Austria-Hungary firmly, and Russia, still weak after its defeat by Japan (1905), had to back down (March 1909) †. Russia felt humiliated and resolved not to back down again, which mattered in July 1914.
  • Consequences: intense anti-Austrian feeling in Bosnia and Serbia, and the rise of revolutionary nationalist groups:
    • Narodna Odbrana ("National Defence", Serbia, founded 8 October 1908);
    • Young Bosnia (Mlada Bosna), a loose movement of young Bosnian nationalists, not a formal organisation;
    • the Black Hand ("Unification or Death", Serbia, May 1911), a secret society of Serbian army officers.

10.10 The Balkan Wars (1912-1913)

  • Balkan League (spring 1912): Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro, encouraged by Russia †. The Ottomans were weakened by the war with Italy over Libya (1911-12).
  • First Balkan War (October 1912 - May 1913): Montenegro declared war on 8 October 1912. The League defeated the Ottoman Empire and conquered almost all of its European lands, including Macedonia. The Treaty of London (30 May 1913) ended it. Albania was made independent (proclaimed 28 November 1912 †), largely at Austria-Hungary's insistence, to keep Serbia from the Adriatic.
  • Second Balkan War (June-August 1913): the victors quarrelled over sharing Macedonia. Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece (night of 29-30 June 1913). Romania and the Ottoman Empire joined in against Bulgaria, which was quickly defeated. Under the Treaty of Bucharest (10 August 1913), most of Macedonia went to Serbia and Greece, and southern Dobruja to Romania. The Ottomans recovered Adrianople (Edirne) †.
  • Serbia, twice victorious, almost doubled in size: from about 48,000 to about 87,000 square kilometres, and from about 2.9 to about 4.4 million people [Verification Required]. The rise of Serbia was a serious threat to Austria-Hungary.
  • Russia encouraged anti-Austrian feeling in the Balkans. Austria-Hungary was looking for an opportunity to crush Serbia militarily.
  • "Powder keg of Europe": because of the alliance system, a minor Balkan conflict could ignite a general European war. Russia stood behind Serbia, Germany behind Austria-Hungary, and France and Britain were linked to Russia.
[Additional] The rough notes say the Balkan League "defeated Turkey" in the first war and Bulgaria was defeated in the second. Note that in the Second Balkan War Bulgaria fought Romania and the Ottoman Empire too, not only Serbia and Greece. Bulgaria's resentment made it join the Central Powers in 1915.

11. World War I (1914-1918)

11.1 Introduction: from the "unstable peace" to the Great War

  • At the turn of the 20th century the great powers of Europe had been at peace with one another since 1871. Many Europeans believed progress had made war between civilised nations a thing of the past.
  • Below the surface of peace and goodwill, dark forces were at work: nationalism, imperial rivalry, militarism and the alliance system. Within a few years several gradual developments pushed the continent into war.
  • Names: contemporaries called it the "Great War" because the world had never seen a war of such scale and proportion. It was also called "the war to end war"; the name "First World War" / "World War I" became usual only after 1939 †.
  • Dates and sides: a global war between the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, Bulgaria) and the Allied (Entente) Powers (Britain and its empire, France, Russia, Serbia, Belgium, Japan, Italy from 1915, the USA from 1917, and others). It ran from 28 July 1914 (Austria-Hungary's declaration of war on Serbia) to the armistice of 11 November 1918.
  • Outcome: an Allied victory, with far-reaching consequences for humanity. The conditions created by the war made the Russian Revolution of 1917 possible.
  • India link [Additional]: over a million Indians served overseas, in France, Mesopotamia, East Africa and Gallipoli †. The war's economic burden and broken political promises shaped the Home Rule movement, the Montagu Declaration (1917) and the Rowlatt Act (1919). This will be covered with the impact of the war.

[Correction] The rough notes say the great powers had been at peace with each other "for nearly last 30 yrs". From the Franco-Prussian War (1870-71) to 1914 is about 43 years; Britannica calls 1871-1914 an "unstable peace". Some wars did involve great powers in this period: the Russo-Turkish War (1877-78), the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05) and the Italo-Turkish War (1911-12). Two great powers of Europe did not fight each other again until 1914.

11.2 Causes of World War I (part 1)

Cause 1: The rise of nationalism and national rivalries

  • Nationalism (love of one's nation) can turn into aggressive rivalry when nations try to dominate or suppress one another. By the early 20th century intense national rivalries had emerged in Europe:
    • French revanche over Alsace-Lorraine;
    • Pan-Germanism and Germany's demand for a "place in the sun";
    • Pan-Slavism (Russia) and Serbian nationalism against Austria-Hungary;
    • Anglo-German economic and naval rivalry;
    • the nationalism of subject peoples in Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire (10.9, 10.10).

Cause 2: Secret diplomacy, entangled alliances and the balance of power

  • The efforts of Bismarck and the German Kaisers to secure Germany's position, and later its global hegemony, produced several peacetime alliances and secret treaties (10.7).
  • These proved harmful in the long run. They disturbed the balance of power and provoked a series of counter-alliances (10.8). Thus the alliance system divided Europe into two armed camps, so that a dispute between two powers could drag in all the others ...

[Additional] Historiography: historians differ on responsibility:
  • Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919) placed the blame on Germany and its allies.
  • Between the wars, many historians spread the blame among all the powers (Lloyd George: the nations "slithered" into war).
  • Fritz Fischer (1961) argued that Germany sought war to become a world power.
  • Recent work such as Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012) stresses shared responsibility and miscalculation †.

Mains Points

  • Bismarck's method: Realpolitik plus "iron and blood". Diplomacy isolated each enemy, and Moltke's army won short wars. Unity came "from above", conservative and Prussian, not liberal "from below" as attempted in 1848.
  • "Coal and iron" against "blood and iron": the Zollverein, railways and Ruhr industry gave Prussia the means; diplomacy and war decided the timing. A balanced answer gives weight to both (9.5.8).
  • Bismarck's restraint: lenient terms for Austria in 1866 made it an ally in 1879. The harsh Treaty of Frankfurt (1871) made France a permanent enemy and shaped his whole foreign policy.
  • The 1871 Empire: federal and constitutional in form, with universal male suffrage for the Reichstag, but the chancellor answered to the Kaiser. Militarist and Prussian-dominated, it was a key to understanding 1914.
  • Italy and Germany compared: similar structure (Austria the obstacle, a leading state, a realist statesman, Napoleon III exploited, a monarchy as the result). Different methods: popular volunteers and dependence on France for Italy; Prussian army and economic strength for Germany (table).
  • Impact: nationalism spread, the balance of power was upset, the colonial race intensified, and militarism and secret alliances grew, so the seeds of 1914 were sown.
  • New Imperialism: industrial needs (raw materials, markets, capital), technology (steamships, telegraph, quinine, machine guns), nationalism and strategic competition. Results: Africa partitioned, China carved into spheres, Japan's rise.
  • From Bismarck to William II: Bismarck kept France isolated and Russia tied (Reinsurance). William II's Weltpolitik, the naval race and the failure to renew Reinsurance encircled Germany with the Franco-Russian Alliance and the Triple Entente.
  • Balkans as powder keg: Ottoman decline, Serbian and South Slav nationalism, and Austro-Russian rivalry, combined with alliances, meant that a local conflict would become general (10.10).
  • India links: the Suez route; the Anglo-Russian Convention ended the "Great Game"; British fear of the Baghdad Railway; Japan's 1905 victory inspired Indian nationalists; Indian troops served in World War I.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • King against Kaiser: William I was King of Prussia from 1861 but Emperor only from 18 January 1871. At Ems in 1870 he was "the King".
  • Minister-President against Chancellor: Bismarck was Prussian Minister-President in 1862, Chancellor only from 1867 (North German Confederation) and 1871 (Empire).
  • Blitzkrieg belongs to World War II (1939-41), not to Moltke's wars.
  • Schleswig against Holstein: Holstein was in the German Confederation, Schleswig was not. After Gastein, Prussia had Schleswig and Austria had Holstein.
  • Treaty of Vienna (1864) ended the Danish War. Do not confuse it with the Congress of Vienna (1815) or the Peace of Vienna (1866, Venetia).
  • Treaty of Prague (1866) ended the war with Austria; the Treaty of Frankfurt (1871) ended the war with France. Frankfurt was also the seat of the Federal Diet and the 1848 Parliament.
  • Alsace-Lorraine went to the German Empire (as Reichsland), not to Prussia, and only part of Lorraine was taken.
  • Proclaimed at Versailles in 1871 is the German Empire; signed at Versailles in 1919 is the peace treaty with Germany (a deliberate reversal).
  • Triple Alliance (1882) against Triple Entente (1907). Italy was in the Alliance but fought for the Allies from 1915. The Central Powers were Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottomans and Bulgaria.
  • Franco-Russian Alliance was a military alliance; the Triple Entente was only a set of understandings.
  • Dual Alliance (1879) is Germany and Austria-Hungary; the Dual Monarchy (1867) is Austria-Hungary itself.
  • Two Moroccan crises: Tangier/Algeciras (1905-06) and Agadir (1911). Two Balkan Wars: 1912-13 against Turkey, and 1913 against Bulgaria.
  • Bosnia: occupied in 1878, annexed in 1908. The 1878 occupation was not an annexation.
  • Brussels (1876) was Leopold's geographic conference; Berlin (1884-85) was Bismarck's partition-rules conference. The Congress of Berlin (1878) was about the Balkans.
  • Germany did not equal the Royal Navy in 1914 (17 against 29 dreadnoughts).

30-Second Revision

  • Bismarck (1862): "iron and blood", Realpolitik, isolate then defeat. Three wars: Denmark 1864 (with Austria), Austria 1866 (Koniggratz; North German Confederation 1867), France 1870-71 (Ems Dispatch, Sedan). The Empire was proclaimed at Versailles on 18 January 1871, and the Treaty of Frankfurt took Alsace-Lorraine.
  • Impact: nationalism, an upset balance of power, a colonial race, militarism and secret alliances.
  • 1871-1914: industrial powers and New Imperialism; Pax Britannica challenged by Germany (Navy Laws, Dreadnought); Eastern, Far Eastern and African questions.
  • Alliances: Bismarck isolated France (Dual 1879, Triple 1882, Reinsurance 1887). After 1890: Franco-Russian 1894, Entente Cordiale 1904, Anglo-Russian 1907, giving two blocs.
  • Balkans: Bosnia annexed 1908; Balkan Wars 1912-13 enlarged Serbia, making the Balkans the "powder keg". WWI: 28 July 1914 - 11 November 1918.

1. UPSC CSE Mains 2020 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(a) (20 marks)

"From Bismarck's 'Blood and Iron' speech and his forceful actions to achieve German unification, Bismarck came to be known as the Iron Chancellor." Critically examine.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • The speech of 30 September 1862 and its context: the constitutional conflict and the lesson of 1848 (9.5.9).
  • Force: taxes collected without a budget, army reform, and three wars (Figure 1; Map 2).
  • Critically: the iron was matched by diplomacy (isolation, Biarritz, the Italian alliance, lenient Prague terms), by the economic base (the Zollverein) and by Moltke's planning. After 1871 he was a peace-keeper (alliances, "honest broker").
  • Conclusion: the title fits his methods and his authoritarian rule, but his genius lay in calculation, not force alone.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2018 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(a) (20 marks)

Examine the role of Bismarck in state building in Germany.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Unification: the three wars, the North German Confederation constitution (1867) and the Empire (1871) (9.5.12, 9.5.14).
  • Institutions: Reichstag (universal male suffrage), Bundesrat, a chancellor responsible to the Kaiser, and Prussian dominance.
  • After 1871 (beyond today's class; treat briefly): Kulturkampf, protection (1879), social insurance laws, anti-socialist laws †.
  • Assessment: a strong, industrial, but authoritarian and militarist state.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2018 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(d) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: At the end of the Battle of Sedan (1870), "Europe lost a mistress and gained a master".

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • "Mistress" means France's leading role under Napoleon III; "master" means Germany as the dominant continental power after Sedan and 1871 (9.5.13).
  • Bismarck's system (1871-90) made Berlin the centre of European diplomacy (10.7).
  • Critically: Britain still ruled the seas (Pax Britannica, 10.2); Russia and Austria-Hungary remained great powers; Germany's dominance produced counter-alliances (10.8).

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(b) (20 marks)

What determinant factors, along with diplomatic, shaped the process of German Unification?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Economic: the Zollverein, railways, coal and iron ("coal and iron") (9.5.8).
  • Military: Roon's reforms, Moltke, the needle gun, the railways.
  • Ideological and cultural: nationalism since Napoleon and 1848; the failure of liberalism.
  • International: Austria weakened by 1859; Russia friendly; Napoleon III's miscalculation.
  • Diplomatic: isolation in each war (Figure 1).

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(d) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: "18 January, 1871 had been a day of triumph for the strength and pride of Germany and 28 June, 1919 was the day of chastisement."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • 18 January 1871: the Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors after the defeat of France (9.5.13).
  • 28 June 1919: the Treaty of Versailles was signed in the same hall, five years to the day after Sarajevo; France reversed the humiliation (to be covered with World War I).
  • Critically: the "triumph" of 1871 created French revanche, one cause of 1914 (11.2). The "chastisement" of 1919 in turn bred German revanchism.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 - GS Paper I, Q7 (12.5 marks, 200 words)

To what extent can Germany be held responsible for causing the two World Wars? Discuss critically.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • World War I: Weltpolitik, the naval race, the "blank cheque" to Austria-Hungary (next class), the Schlieffen Plan. But also Austro-Serbian conflict, Russian mobilisation, the alliance system and French revanche (10.3, 10.8, 11.2).
  • Historiography: Versailles Article 231; Fischer against Clark (11.2).
  • World War II (later class): Hitler's aggression was primary, though appeasement and Versailles share some blame.
  • Conclusion: heavy but not sole responsibility for 1914; primary responsibility for 1939.

7. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(a) (20 marks)

How far is it correct to say that the First World War was fought essentially for the preservation of the balance of power?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • For: the rise of Germany upset the 1815/1871 balance. The Entente formed to restore it, and Britain entered to stop German hegemony and protect Belgium (9.7, 10.8).
  • Against: nationalism (Serbia, Austria-Hungary), imperial rivalry, militarism and war plans, and domestic pressures (Figure 3).
  • Conclusion: balance of power was a central strategic logic, but not the only cause.

8. UPSC CSE Mains 2025 - History Optional, Paper II, Q6(c) (10 marks)

Did new imperialism represent a continuation of old colonial practices or did it mark a fundamental shift in global power structure? Discuss critically.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Continuity: the search for trade and resources; settler and plantation colonies; Britain and France remained the leaders.
  • Shift: industrial capitalism (raw materials, markets, capital export); new powers (Germany, Italy, the USA, Japan); the partition of Africa and spheres in China; technology; about 84-85% of the globe under European control by 1914 [Verification Required] (10.1, 10.5, 10.6).
  • Conclusion: it intensified older practices but created a global, competitive system that fed into World War I.

Corrections Made

  1. c01 - William I was King of Prussia from 1861; Kaiser only from 18 January 1871.
  2. c02 - Bismarck was born at Schoenhausen in the Altmark (Province of Saxony), not "Brandenburg".
  3. c03 - In 1862 Bismarck became Prussian Minister-President; Chancellor only from 1867 and 1871.
  4. c04 - "Blitzkrieg" is a 1930s-40s term; Moltke's methods were railway mobilisation and a war of movement.
  5. c05 - The Danish crisis came from the November Constitution of 1863 (not 1861) and concerned Schleswig.
  6. c06 - Holstein was cut off from Austria, not landlocked; Holstein was a Confederation member.
  7. c07 - Bismarck did not promise Belgium to Napoleon III; only vague compensation was hinted at Biarritz.
  8. c08 - Most German states, including Bavaria and Wurttemberg, sided with Austria; only some were annexed, and Saxony was not.
  9. c09 - Benedetti met the King (not the Kaiser) at Ems; Bismarck edited the telegram to look like a mutual insult.
  10. c10 - Alsace and part of Lorraine went to the German Empire, not Prussia; the south joined through its 1866 alliances and the November 1870 treaties.
  11. c11 - "Father of secret diplomacy" is not a standard title; he is the "Iron Chancellor".
  12. c12 - Pax Britannica dates from 1815; the "golden age" is the Belle Epoque (c. 1871-1914).
  13. c13 - Germany never equalled the Royal Navy (17 against 29 dreadnoughts in 1914); a super-dreadnought is a type of ship.
  14. c14 - The Baghdad Railway ran inside the Ottoman Empire, was linked to Berlin through the Balkans, and was unfinished in 1914.
  15. c15 - Bulgaria was only autonomous in 1878 (independent 1908); the name "Yugoslavia" dates from 1929.
  16. c16 - The Berlin Conference regulated the Scramble; the Scramble is dated c. 1881-1914.
  17. c17 - The link with Russia was revived in 1881 and through Reinsurance (1887); Russia was lost only in 1890.
  18. c18 - Italy did not join the Central Powers; it stayed neutral in 1914 and joined the Allies in 1915.
  19. c19 - The Franco-Russian link was a military alliance; the Triple Entente was only a set of understandings.
  20. c20 - The great powers had been at peace with one another for about 43 years (since 1871), not 30.
