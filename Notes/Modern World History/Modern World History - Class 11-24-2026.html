17. The Cold War (1945-1991) - concluded: the fall of the USSR and the impact of the Cold War

17.7 Causes of the fall of the USSR

  • The fall of the USSR was dramatic and swift: about 70 years of communist rule (1917-1991) collapsed within a few years (1989-1991).
  • Many historians argue it was an "accident waiting to happen", because of the political and economic limitations of the Soviet system. Others stress the immediate role of Gorbachev's reforms. Both views are needed: the long-term weaknesses explain why the USSR was vulnerable; the reforms of 1985-1991 explain when and how fast it fell.

17.7.1 Long-term cause 1: political limitations

  • Communist Party (CPSU) rule was controlled by the Politburo, a small group at the top - in practice an oligarchy. This led to corruption, nepotism, lack of transparency and no political accountability, which bred popular discontent.
  • Ordinary people lacked basic rights: no freedom of speech, no press freedom; single-party, single-candidate elections were unfair.
  • The government was unresponsive to popular pressure and left no space for criticism, causing political frustration.
  • Constant use of military force and the secret police (KGB) to control the satellite states (eastern Europe) and the constituent republics (the 15 Soviet Socialist Republics), together with Russian domination of Union affairs, fed nationalist sentiment that threatened the existence of the USSR.
  • [Additional] Britannica notes that by the early 1980s the Politburo "was dominated by old men, and they were overwhelmingly Russian", and non-Russian representation at the top had declined. Source: Britannica - Russia: the Gorbachev era.

17.7.2 Long-term cause 2: economic limitations

  • The highly centralised planned economy (the command system) was neither effective nor efficient and did not satisfy people's aspirations; socialist policies did not make people prosperous.
  • Priority to heavy and defence industry caused severe shortages of consumer goods and economic discontent.
  • While much of the world was moving towards trade and investment liberalisation (the class note's "LPG" - liberalisation, privatisation, globalisation - is used loosely here; the term is Indian and belongs to 1991), the Soviet economy stayed relatively isolated from the world market, causing industrial and technological backwardness and economic stagnation.
  • Heavy spending on the Cold War - the arms, nuclear and space races, proxy conflicts, and the cost of keeping military control over the republics and satellites - weakened the economy.

[Correction] "The cost of 2 cold war" should read the cost of the "Second Cold War" (renewed hostility of 1979-1985, see the 23 November note, 17.6): the Soviet war in Afghanistan (Dec 1979 - Feb 1989) and the new arms race with Reagan's America (including the SDI challenge, 1983) strained an already fragile economy. [Additional] Britannica notes that Andropov's advisers (the Novosibirsk Report, late 1983) already argued that the whole system of central planning had become obsolete. Source: Britannica (Students) - glasnost and perestroika.

17.7.3 Immediate causes: Gorbachev's reforms (1985-1991)

The long-term limitations explain the USSR's susceptibility to collapse but not the timing and speed of the collapse. The sudden fall is attributed to a series of reforms introduced by Mikhail Gorbachev, General Secretary of the CPSU from March 1985 † and the first (and only) President of the USSR from March 1990.

[Correction] Glasnost did not begin in 1985. Gorbachev introduced the concept of perestroika in April 1985; glasnost was brought forward as a policy in late 1986 to overcome bureaucratic resistance to perestroika (Britannica). The 1985 date is correct for perestroika and for Gorbachev's accession, not for glasnost. Source: Britannica (Students) - glasnost and perestroika.

[Correction] Perestroika was not a market reform from the start. At first (1985-86) Gorbachev believed the system was basically sound and pursued faster growth through investment (the uskoreniye, "acceleration", phase). Only after two years did he conclude that deeper change was needed, and in 1987-88 he pushed through reforms that went "less than halfway to the creation of a semi-free market system"; the contradictions "brought economic chaos". Source: Britannica - Russia: the Gorbachev era.

17.7.4 The August coup, 1991

  • Hard-line communists realised that the situation was getting out of control and attempted a coup against Gorbachev (19-21 August 1991); it failed.

[Correction] The name is Gennady Yanayev (Vice-President of the USSR), who headed the eight-member State Committee on the State of Emergency (GKChP); others included KGB chief Vladimir Kryuchkov (Britannica: the coup was "guided by" Kryuchkov), Defence Minister Dmitry Yazov, Premier Valentin Pavlov and Interior Minister Boris Pugo. Gorbachev was held at his dacha at Foros, Crimea; the plotters claimed he was ill. The coup was timed to stop the signing of a new Union Treaty on 20 August that would have strengthened the republics. Yeltsin rallied resistance at the Russian parliament building (the "White House"), troops refused to fire, and the coup collapsed on 21 August. Sources: Britannica - 1991 Soviet coup attempt; Britannica - the attempted coup; Seventeen Moments in Soviet History (Michigan State University) - The August Coup.

  • [Additional] Consequence: "The attempted coup destroyed Gorbachev politically. The republics rushed to be free of Moscow's control before another coup succeeded" (Britannica). Gorbachev resigned as General Secretary and accepted the suspension of the Communist Party; the Baltic states' independence was recognised (September 1991 †); Ukraine voted for independence on 1 December 1991. The USSR started falling apart.

17.8 Disintegration of the USSR (December 1991)

  • Belavezha Accords, 8 December 1991: the leaders of Russia (Boris Yeltsin), Ukraine (Leonid Kravchuk) and Belarus (Stanislav Shushkevich) met at the Viskuli government lodge in the Belavezha Forest, Belarus, without Gorbachev, and declared that "the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, as a subject of international law and a geopolitical reality, is ceasing its existence". By the same agreement they founded the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS) to cushion the fall, so that former members could cooperate economically, militarily and in foreign policy.
  • Alma-Ata Protocol, 21 December 1991: CIS membership expanded to 11 republics, which collectively confirmed the end of the USSR.
  • 25 December 1991: Gorbachev resigned as President of the USSR; the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin and replaced by the Russian tricolour.
  • 26 December 1991: the USSR formally ceased to exist.

[Correction] The Alma-Ata Protocol did not legally "force" Gorbachev to resign; it left him with no state to lead. His resignation speech said he was discontinuing his activities "due to the situation which has evolved as a result of the formation of the Commonwealth of Independent States". Note also that the three Baltic states and Georgia did not sign at Alma-Ata. Britannica places the 8 December declaration "in Minsk, Belarus"; other accounts give the Viskuli lodge in the Belavezha Forest - both refer to the same meeting in Belarus. Sources: EBSCO - Gorbachev resigns (text of the speech); National Security Archive - The End of the Soviet Union 1991; Britannica.

17.9 Impact of the Cold War (1945-1991) - class handout

The Cold War was a period of intense geopolitical and ideological conflict between the United States and the Soviet Union. Its effects reached global politics, economics, society and technology.

17.9.1 Political impact

  • Bipolar world order: the world was divided into the capitalist Western bloc led by the USA and the communist Eastern bloc led by the USSR, formalised in military alliances - NATO (4 April 1949) and the Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955) (see the 23 November note, Table 17.6).
  • Proxy wars: the Korean War (1950-1953), the Vietnam War (1955-1975) and the Soviet-Afghan War (1979-1989), in which the superpowers backed opposing sides; these raised global tension and devastated the regions where they were fought.
  • Decolonisation and new alliances: many nations won independence in this period; both superpowers sought influence in them, often contributing to political instability.
  • [Additional] India link: India answered bipolarity with non-alignment (Nehru; Belgrade 1961 †), while signing the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation (9 August 1971) † during the Bangladesh crisis - an example of how newly independent states used superpower rivalry to protect their own interests.

Dating note: the start of the Vietnam War is variously given as 1955 (the handout's date, following the creation of South Vietnam), 1959 or 1964 (Gulf of Tonkin); it ended with the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 (23 November note).

17.9.2 Economic impact

  • Arms race and military spending: both superpowers raced in nuclear weapons, diverting resources from social and economic development - above all in the USSR, where military spending contributed to stagnation (17.7.2).
  • Space race: the USSR launched Sputnik 1 (4 October 1957); the USA landed the first humans on the Moon (Apollo 11, July 1969 †). These innovations had lasting effects on science and technology.
  • Economic systems: the contest entrenched capitalism (led by the USA) and socialism (led by the USSR) and influenced economic policy, trade, industrialisation and planning across the world. [Additional] India's mixed economy and Five-Year Plans drew on both models.

17.9.3 Social and cultural impact

  • Fear and paranoia: the threat of nuclear war created widespread fear, especially during the Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962). In the USA this fed the Red Scare and McCarthyism (early 1950s), the persecution of suspected communists.
  • Cultural rivalry: events such as the Olympics became arenas of ideological competition (the US-led boycott of Moscow 1980 and the Soviet-led boycott of Los Angeles 1984 †); art, film and sport were used as soft power.
  • Civil rights movements: the USA's wish to present itself as the defender of freedom added pressure for racial equality at home.

[Correction] The handout says independence movements in Africa, Asia and Latin America "often aligned with socialist ideals". This needs qualification: some did (for example Vietnam, Angola, Mozambique, Cuba), but many leading movements and new states - India, Indonesia, Egypt, Ghana - chose non-alignment and mixed economies rather than alignment with either bloc. Treat the statement as a generalisation, not a rule (see the 23 November note on decolonisation).

17.9.4 Technological and scientific advances

  • Nuclear technology: advances in both weapons and civilian uses such as nuclear power; the danger prompted treaties such as the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT, 1968) - opened for signature 1 July 1968, in force 1970 †; India did not sign.
  • Information technology: competition spurred computers, satellite technology and the early internet (ARPANET, a US defence-funded network, 1969 †), driven by needs of communication, espionage and intelligence.

17.9.5 End of the Cold War and its legacy

  • The Cold War ended with the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 (17.7, 17.8), driven by economic difficulty, Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika, and external pressure. New independent states emerged and the bipolar order ended.
  • It left a unipolar world dominated by the USA; its legacy continues in regional conflicts, nuclear proliferation, and the lasting role of NATO and of tension with Russia.

[Correction] "External pressures from the US" is an interpretation, not settled fact. The "Reagan victory" school stresses US pressure (arms race, SDI, support for the Afghan resistance). Against it, declassified records published by the National Security Archive show that "keeping the Union together, and backing Gorbachev personally, remained at the core of U.S. policy all the way through 1991", for fear of a violent break-up; the Office of the Historian likewise calls US policy in 1989-91 "reactive". Most historians give the main weight to internal causes. Sources: National Security Archive - The End of the Soviet Union 1991; US Office of the Historian.

17.10 Impact of the fall of the USSR

  • Reorganisation of eastern Europe along democratic lines (and, later, entry of many states into NATO and the EU).
  • Fall of the "Second World" and world-wide decline of communism.
  • End of the Cold War - of the arms, space and nuclear races and of the ideological conflict.
  • Dominance of capitalist ideas and institutions - the IMF, World Bank and the WTO (1995) †.
  • End of the bipolar order and rise of a unipolar world - US hegemony.
  • Decline in economic, technical and military help to Third World countries, because the superpower competition that motivated it had ended.
  • Gradual change in world power equations towards a multipolar order, e.g. the European Union, G8, G20 and BRICS.
  • [Additional] New conflicts in the post-Soviet space (Chechnya, Transnistria, Nagorno-Karabakh) and economic chaos (hyperinflation, unemployment) in the successor states.
  • [Additional] India link: loss of a major trading partner and of rupee-trade arrangements contributed to the pressures of India's 1991 balance-of-payments crisis and the turn to economic reform †; India then built a strategic partnership with the USA while keeping close defence ties with Russia.

18. The Chinese Revolution (1911-1949)

18.1 Introduction and outline

  • In the 19th century China faced serious political, socio-economic and military problems because of Western and Japanese imperialism and the decline of the Qing dynasty.
  • Chinese revolutionaries mobilised millions against imperialist and capitalist exploitation and against inefficient Qing rulers. The Xinhai Revolution of 1911 led to the Republic of China (ROC) in 1912.
  • The world wars, the spread of socialism under the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), and the civil war between the CCP and the Kuomintang (KMT) culminated in the People's Republic of China (PRC) under Mao Zedong, proclaimed on 1 October 1949.
  • Names: the 1911 rising is called the Xinhai Revolution (after the year Xinhai in the Chinese calendar) or the Revolution of 1911; 10 October ("Double Tenth") is still marked as National Day in Taiwan. The 1949 victory is the Chinese Communist Revolution. Chinese writers call 1839-1949 the "century of humiliation".

18.2 China under the Qing monarchy

Political conditions

  • Chinese monarchy is traditionally dated from about 2000 BCE, and China was ruled by a succession of dynasties.
  • The Qing (Manchu) dynasty ruled from the mid-17th century: an absolute monarchy with a merit-based bureaucracy recruited through the imperial (civil-service) examinations (abolished in 1905 †).
  • Empress Dowager Cixi dominated politics from 1861 to 1908; the last emperor, Puyi, reigned 1908-1912.

[Correction] The Qing came to power in 1644 - the mid-17th century, not the "mid 7th century". Britannica: the Qing "ruled China from 1644 to 1911/12". Source: Britannica - Opium Wars.

[Correction] Two points on the monarchy. (a) The imperial system proper - rule by an emperor over a unified state - dates from the Qin unification of 221 BCE † and lasted about 2,000 years (Britannica: the 1912 abdication ended "China's 2,000-year-old imperial system"); the date "around 2000 BCE" refers to the traditional (partly legendary) Xia dynasty. [Verification Required: "13 different dynasties" - the count depends on which dynasties and periods are included; standard lists give more.] (b) Cixi was not a reigning "queen": she was the Empress Dowager (mother of the Tongzhi Emperor and aunt of the Guangxu Emperor) who controlled the court as regent or from behind the throne. Source: Britannica - abdication of the Qing emperor.

Society

  • Multi-ethnic, multi-religious: Confucianism, Taoism, Buddhism, Christianity (and Islam †).
  • A Sino-centric world view - China as the centre of civilisation, especially in language and culture.
  • A historically hard-working people with famous inventions: the compass, printing, paper and gunpowder.
  • Ordinary people lacked rights and political power.

[Correction] "People could not own land" is an overstatement. Private landownership, with land bought and sold, existed in Qing China; the problem was that much land was concentrated in the hands of landlords and gentry and many peasants were tenants paying heavy rents †. [Verification Required: the share of tenant farmers varied by region and cannot be given as one figure here.] The CCP's later appeal - "land to the tiller" - rested on this inequality (18.12).

Economy

  • An agrarian economy with many traditional industries - tea, silk, porcelain, mining.
  • Largely self-sufficient; little demand for foreign goods.
  • An export-oriented trade with a favourable (positive) balance of trade: Europeans paid for tea, silk and porcelain in silver.

18.3 The advent of the Europeans and the Opium Wars

  • The Portuguese reached China in the early 16th century (1513 †) and were trading by the mid-16th century (Macau, 1557 †).
  • In the 18th century Britain, France and the USA also traded with China. Western traders were confined to one port - Canton (Guangzhou), and to licensed Chinese merchants (the Canton system, from about 1757-60 to 1842). Missionaries were also active.
  • The Western powers had a negative balance of trade with China. To correct it, British traders turned to opium, illegal in China, shipped from India; the trade grew from the late 18th century and "dramatically from about 1820" (Britannica).
  • Opium addiction created serious economic and social problems: silver flowed out, officials were corrupted, and addiction spread.

[Additional] India link: opium was grown in British-ruled India (Bengal under an East India Company monopoly, and Malwa in western India) and auctioned to private traders who carried it to China. Britain used the profits to buy Chinese tea, silk and porcelain; the trade also supported the revenues of British India. See Map 2. Source: Britannica - Opium Wars; Treaty of Nanking (Wikipedia, background).

[Verification Required: "12 million opium addicts" - figures for the number of opium smokers in 19th-century China vary widely between sources and dates; no reliable single figure was confirmed.]

18.3.1 The Opium Wars

  • Immediate cause of the First Opium War: in spring 1839 Imperial Commissioner Lin Zexu confiscated and destroyed more than 20,000 chests of opium (about 1,400 tons) held by British merchants at Canton.
  • First Opium War (1839-1842): Britain defeated China and imposed the Treaty of Nanking (Nanjing), 29 August 1842, signed aboard HMS Cornwallis: Hong Kong Island ceded; five treaty ports opened - Canton, Amoy (Xiamen), Foochow (Fuzhou), Ningpo (Ningbo) and Shanghai; an indemnity of $21 million; a "fair and regular" tariff. The Treaty of the Bogue (8 October 1843) added extraterritoriality and most-favoured-nation status.
  • Second Opium War (Arrow War, 1856-1860): Britain and France defeated China. The Treaties of Tianjin (Tientsin), June 1858: foreign envoys in Peking, 10 new ports, Yangtze open to foreign ships, travel in the interior, freedom for missionaries, and legalised opium imports.

[Correction] The Second Opium War lasted 1856-1860, not 1856-58. China signed the Treaties of Tianjin in 1858 but refused to ratify them; fighting resumed in 1859; in October 1860 British and French troops captured Peking and burned the emperor's Summer Palace, and China signed the Convention of Peking (24-25 October 1860), accepting the Tianjin terms and ceding southern Kowloon to Britain; Russia gained the lands east of the Ussuri, including Vladivostok (14 November 1860). Sources: Britannica - Timeline of the Second Opium War; Britannica - Unequal treaty.

[Correction] "Hong Kong (1842-1997)" needs qualifying: Hong Kong Island was ceded in 1842, southern Kowloon in 1860, and the New Territories were leased for 99 years in 1898 †; the whole colony returned to China on 1 July 1997 †. 18.4 China in the mid-19th century and the Taiping Rebellion (1850-1864)

  • Conditions: opium addiction and its economic and social costs; defeat in the Opium Wars; spread of Christianity; decline of Qing authority; rising corruption and inefficient administration; rapid population growth with falling food output (Yellow River floods) - famine-like conditions. Qing rule became unpopular.
  • Taiping Rebellion (1850-1864): led by Hong Xiuquan, a failed civil-service candidate who, influenced by Christian teaching, believed himself the younger brother of Jesus. On 1 January 1851 he proclaimed the Taiping Tianguo ("Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace"); the Taipings took Nanjing on 10 March 1853 and made it their capital. Their programme - common property, equality of men and women, bans on opium, foot-binding and slavery - attracted famine-stricken peasants.
  • It "ravaged 17 provinces" and is one of the deadliest civil wars in history. Nanjing fell in July 1864.

[Correction] Two refinements. (a) Deaths: Britannica gives "an estimated 20 million lives"; modern estimates range from 20 to 30 million. The note's upper figure of 40 million is above most estimates. (b) The Qing did not defeat the Taipings mainly with European help. The decisive force was the gentry-led Xiang (Hunan) Army of Zeng Guofan (with Li Hongzhang's Huai Army), which surrounded Nanjing by 1862. Western help was limited: the Western-officered "Ever-Victorious Army" under the American Frederick Townsend Ward and then the British officer Charles George Gordon defended Shanghai (1860) and the lower Yangtze. Sources: Britannica - Taiping Rebellion; Taiping Rebellion (Wikipedia).

18.5 The rise of Japan and the First Sino-Japanese War (1894-1895)

  • By the mid-19th century China's political, socio-economic and military weakness had been exposed. Japan, modernising rapidly after the Meiji Restoration (1868) † and industrialising, wanted Korea and Manchuria.
  • Japan defeated China in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894-1895); war was declared on 1 August 1894. The Treaty of Shimonoseki (17 April 1895): China recognised the independence of Korea; ceded Taiwan (Formosa), the Pescadores (Penghu) and the Liaodong Peninsula; paid an indemnity; opened four more ports to Japan.
  • China borrowed from European powers to pay and fell into debt; the powers then carved out spheres of influence - the "scramble for concessions" (1897-98), part of the Far Eastern Question.

[Correction] The indemnity was 200 million (Kuping) taels of silver, not "150 million dollars". After the Triple Intervention of Russia, France and Germany, Japan returned the Liaodong Peninsula in exchange for a further 30 million taels. Sources: Britannica - Treaty of Shimonoseki; Treaty of Shimonoseki (Wikipedia, treaty articles).

  • [Additional] The scramble for concessions (1898): Germany leased Jiaozhou Bay (Qingdao) in Shandong; Russia leased Port Arthur and Dalian on the Liaodong Peninsula (the territory Japan had been forced to return); Britain leased Weihaiwei and the New Territories of Hong Kong; France leased Kwangchowan †. Spheres of influence followed: Russia in Manchuria, Germany in Shandong, Britain in the Yangtze valley, France in the south-west, Japan in Fujian †. The USA, a late-comer, issued the Open Door notes (1899-1900) calling for equal trade access and China's territorial integrity. See Map 3.

18.6 The Boxer Uprising (1899-1901)

  • By the late 19th century China was a victim of European and Japanese imperialism; foreign domination and the rapid spread of Christianity caused popular discontent.
  • Peasants, led by martial-arts groups, formed the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists (Yihequan) - called "Boxers" by Westerners - in rural Shandong in the late 1890s, amid Yellow River floods (1898) and drought.
  • Boxer Uprising (1899-1901): attacks on missionaries and Chinese Christians; the siege of the foreign legations in Peking (from 20 June 1900, 55 days), ended when an eight-nation relief force reached the city on 14 August 1900.
  • Empress Dowager Cixi backed the Boxers and declared war on the powers; the rising was crushed - but it marked the rise of Chinese nationalism.
  • Boxer Protocol (7 September 1901): signed by China and 11 powers.

[Correction] The fine was imposed on China (the Qing state), not on the Empress Dowager personally: an indemnity of 450 million Haikwan taels, at 4% interest over 39 years (1902-1940) - nearly one billion taels with interest - secured on the Maritime Customs and the salt tax. Other terms: punishment of officials, a fortified legation quarter with foreign guards, the Taku forts razed, foreign troops at 12 points between Peking and the sea, and a two-year ban on arms imports. [Verification Required: "18,000 tons of silver" - 450 million taels at about 37.5 g each comes to roughly 17,000 tonnes, so the figure is of the right order but depends on the tael weight used.] Sources: Boxer Protocol (Wikipedia); Encyclopedia of Economic Statecraft - Boxer Indemnity.

[Additional] In 1908 the USA remitted the excess of its share and used it to educate Chinese students, founding the school that became Tsinghua University; Britain and others followed in the 1920s.

18.7 China in the early 20th century: reform too late

  • Conditions: political, socio-economic and military weakness; decline of Qing rule; foreign (European and Japanese) imperialism; nationalism stirred by the Boxer Uprising.
  • Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War (1904-1905) - fought largely on Chinese soil in Manchuria - showed that an Asian state could defeat a European power; Japan made Korea a protectorate (1905) and annexed it (1910).
  • Chinese people demanded rights, democratic reforms and modernisation. New revolutionary groups rose against the Qing; the most prominent was Sun Yat-sen's Tongmenghui ("Chinese Revolutionary Alliance" or United League), founded in Tokyo in 1905 †.
  • Late Qing reforms: Cixi sent officials abroad (1905-06) to study foreign constitutions; on their recommendation an edict of 1906 committed the court to "constitutional preparation".
  • Cixi died in November 1908 (the Guangxu Emperor died the day before †); the new emperor Puyi (r. 1908-1912) was a two-year-old child, with his father Prince Chun as regent.

[Correction] The promise was not "within 10 years in 1906". The 1906 edict only committed the court to prepare; the Outline of the Imperial Constitution (27 August 1908), modelled on Japan's Meiji constitution, came with a nine-year programme before a parliament would meet. Provincial assemblies opened in 1909 and a National Advisory Council in 1910; under pressure the parliament was brought forward to 1913, but the dynasty fell first. The outline kept major powers with the emperor, which disappointed reformers. Sources: Digitheque MJP (University of Perpignan) - Chinese imperial constitutional plan, 27 August 1908; Principles of the Constitution, 1908 (Wikipedia).

18.8 The Xinhai Revolution (1911) and the Republic of China (1912)

  • Trigger: the Qing plan to nationalise railways with foreign loans (agreement of 5 April 1911) angered local investors; open revolt broke out in Sichuan in September 1911.
  • 10 October 1911 - Wuchang mutiny: after a revolutionary plot was uncovered in Hankou, troops at Wuchang (now part of Wuhan) mutinied; this is regarded as the formal beginning of the revolution. City after city and province after province declared against the Qing - 14 provinces by the end of the year.
  • A provisional republican government was set up at Nanjing, and Sun Yat-sen, who returned from abroad in December, was elected provisional president (took office 1 January 1912 †).
  • The provisional government faced the Qing monarchy in civil-war-like conditions. The court recalled Yuan Shikai (1859-1916), creator of the modern Beiyang Army, who became premier in November 1911 but played a "double game".

[Correction] The revolution was not begun by the Tongmenghui conquering territory. Britannica: it "was triggered not by the United League itself but by the army troops in Hubei who were urged on by the local revolutionary bodies not incorporated in the league"; with no national leader present, the rebels made a colonel, Li Yuanhong, their figurehead. Sun Yat-sen learned of the rising from a newspaper in Denver, USA. Sources: Britannica - China: the Chinese Revolution (1911-12); Britannica - Sun Yat-sen.

  • The settlement: Sun compromised with Yuan Shikai. On 12 February 1912 the boy emperor Puyi abdicated, ending the 268-year Qing dynasty and the imperial system; Sun resigned the next day, and on 14 February Yuan was elected his successor as President of the ROC. The government moved to Beijing in April 1912.

[Correction] The sequence in the notes is reversed. The Republic of China was proclaimed with Sun's provisional government at Nanjing (1 January 1912 †); it was Yuan Shikai, not Sun, who forced Puyi's abdication, in return for the presidency. The abdication edict also kept Puyi's title and a pension for life. Sources: Britannica - Chinese Revolution 1911-1912; Britannica - Sun Yat-sen.

18.9 The Kuomintang, Yuan Shikai and the warlord era (1912-1928)

  • The Tongmenghui was reorganised as the Kuomintang (KMT, Guomindang - "Nationalist Party") in 1912.
  • Elections (December 1912 - February 1913): the KMT won a commanding majority in the new parliament; provincial assemblies were also elected.
  • In March 1913 the KMT organiser Song Jiaoren was assassinated, the evidence pointing to Yuan's circle; a "Second Revolution" against Yuan (summer 1913) was crushed and Sun fled to Japan. Yuan was inaugurated as formal President on 10 October 1913 and dissolved parliament on 10 January 1914 - the presidency had become a dictatorship.
  • Japan seized the German leasehold in Shandong in 1914 and presented the Twenty-One Demands (18 January 1915), which Yuan largely accepted under an ultimatum (May 1915).
  • Yuan declared himself emperor (December 1915 †). Provincial governors and military commanders rose against him in the National Protection War (1915-16); he abandoned the monarchy and died in June 1916 †.
  • Warlord era (1916-1928): regional military commanders ("warlords") controlled provinces and installed puppet governments in Beijing; China was unified on paper but divided in practice.

[Correction] The KMT was not formed simply by Sun renaming the Tongmenghui. Song Jiaoren reorganised the Alliance Society, merging it with smaller parties, into the Nationalist Party (August 1912 †); Sun was its leader in name. And Yuan did not "become president" through the 1912-13 elections, which the KMT won: Yuan was already president (since February 1912) and later coerced parliament into electing him formally (October 1913). Sources: Britannica - Sun Yat-sen; Britannica - History of China: the early republican period.

18.10 World War I, the May Fourth Movement and the founding of the CCP

  • World War I (Allied vs Central Powers) began during the warlord era. China joined the Allies in 1917 (declared war on Germany in August 1917 †) and sent a labour force to France †. China hoped to recover Shandong.
  • The Paris Peace Conference (1919) and the Treaty of Versailles humiliated Germany but also disappointed China.
  • May Fourth Movement (4 May 1919): more than 3,000 students protested at Tiananmen in Beijing against the Versailles decision; the protest spread to workers and merchants, and China's delegation refused to sign the Treaty of Versailles. It encouraged nationalism and anti-imperialism and demanded modernisation and social reform (it grew out of the New Culture Movement of 1915 onwards, around Chen Duxiu's journal New Youth).

[Correction] Versailles did not give Shandong to China: it transferred Germany's former rights in Shandong to Japan (which had seized them in 1914) - that is what provoked the May Fourth protests. (Japan returned the Shandong leasehold to China in 1922 †.) Sources: Britannica - History of China: the early republican period; PolSci Institute - Origins of the CPC.

  • Intellectuals such as Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao, joined by younger activists such as Mao Zedong, were attracted by socialism after the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, and founded the Chinese Communist Party (CCP or CPC) in 1921 in Shanghai, with help from Comintern agents (Voitinsky, Maring).
  • Goals: spread socialism in China; national unity; an end to foreign domination and to the warlord era.

[Correction] Both founders were at Peking University, not "Shanghai University": Chen Duxiu was dean there and editor of New Youth; Li Dazhao was the university's head librarian (and professor), who wrote China's first major analyses of Marxism. The First National Congress opened on 23 July 1921 in the French Concession of Shanghai, with 13 delegates (Mao among them) representing about 57 members; it ended on a boat on South Lake, Jiaxing. Neither Chen nor Li attended, though Chen was elected leader. The CCP marks 1 July as its founding day. Sources: Britannica - Chinese Communist Party; Chronicles of Modern China - Founding of the CCP.

18.11 The First United Front, the Northern Expedition and the Shanghai massacre (1923-1927)

  • First United Front (1923/24-1927): the KMT under Sun Yat-sen and the CCP, both backed by the USSR, cooperated against the warlords. CCP members joined the KMT as individuals; the CCP grew fast through peasant and labour organisation, and Shanghai became a centre of its work.
  • Sun died in March 1925. Chiang Kai-shek, commandant of the Whampoa Military Academy, emerged as leader; he was a conservative nationalist, supported by landlords and business people.
  • With CCP cooperation Chiang launched the Northern Expedition from Guangzhou on 9 July 1926.
  • Shanghai massacre (12 April 1927): after communist-led unions captured Shanghai for the Nationalists, Chiang - with Shanghai's Green Gang and business support - turned on them; thousands of communists and workers were killed, and similar purges followed in other cities. Chiang set up a rival Nationalist government at Nanjing; in July 1927 the left-KMT government at Wuhan also expelled the communists. The First United Front was over.

[Correction] The CCP did not provide the KMT's "military power". Military help came from the USSR and the Comintern: adviser Mikhail Borodin (from October 1923) reorganised the KMT on Leninist lines, Soviet advisers and arms supported the Whampoa Military Academy (1924) and the National Revolutionary Army (NRA), and Soviet advisers accompanied the Northern Expedition. The CCP's contribution was mass mobilisation - peasant associations, unions and propaganda that undermined warlord armies. Sources: Britannica - Sun Yat-sen; Britannica - China: the Northern Expedition; Chronicles of Modern China - Northern Expedition.

[Correction] Most warlords were not defeated "by 1927". By the end of 1926 the NRA held Hunan, Hubei, Jiangxi and Fujian; it took Nanjing and Shanghai in March 1927. After a pause caused by the KMT split, the second phase (1928) took Beijing in June 1928 (renamed Beiping), and in December 1928 Zhang Xueliang brought Manchuria under the Nanjing government - China's nominal reunification, opening the "Nanjing decade" (1928-37). Sources: Britannica - Northern Expedition; Northern Expedition (Wikipedia).

18.12 Rural bases, the Jiangxi Soviet and the Long March (1927-1935)

  • After 1927 the communists moved to the countryside. The Nanchang Uprising (1 August 1927) † and Mao's Autumn Harvest Uprising failed in their aims but founded the communist army; leaders such as Mao Zedong and Zhu De built bases in the hills of Jiangxi.
  • They spread socialist ideas among the peasants, confiscated landlords' land and distributed it to peasants, and encouraged agriculture. In November 1931 the Chinese Soviet Republic (the "Jiangxi Soviet") was proclaimed at Ruijin, with a population of some nine million.
  • Chiang launched five encirclement campaigns (1930-34) against it. The first four were defeated by Mao's mobile guerrilla tactics; in the fifth Chiang used about 700,000 troops and lines of blockhouses, and the leadership (which had sidelined Mao) fought positional battles and was nearly crushed.
  • The Long March (October 1934 - October 1935): about 86,000 troops broke out of Jiangxi in October 1934 and marched about 6,000 miles (10,000 km), crossing 18 mountain ranges and 24 rivers, to Shaanxi (the note's "Shensi") in the north-west; only about 8,000 arrived. At the Zunyi Conference (January 1935) Mao gained enough support to establish his dominance of the party. In December 1936 the communists moved to Yan'an, their base until 1945 †.
  • Along the way the marchers spread socialist ideas and gave land to peasants; Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai became extremely popular.

[Correction] The People's Liberation Army (PLA) was not established under that name in 1927. The Chinese Red Army dates its founding to the Nanchang Uprising of 1 August 1927 (still "Army Day" †); during the war with Japan its forces became the Eighth Route Army and New Fourth Army, and the name PLA was adopted in the civil war (1947 †). Also, Mao did not lead the Long March at the start - Zhu De commanded the army and Zhou Enlai was political commissar; Mao's rise came at Zunyi. Sources: Britannica - Long March; Britannica - Chinese Communist Party.

Distance and survivors: Britannica gives "6,000-mile (10,000-km)" and about 8,000 survivors with Mao in October 1935; other accounts give 12,500 km or fewer survivors, because "the Long March" covers several columns and routes. The note's "approx 10,000 km" matches Britannica.

18.13 Japanese aggression and the Second Sino-Japanese War (1931-1945)

  • Manchuria: after the Mukden Incident (18-19 September 1931) Japan seized Manchuria and in 1932 set up the puppet state of Manchukuo, with the deposed Qing emperor Puyi as its titular head.
  • Xi'an Incident (December 1936): Chiang, who preferred to fight the communists first, was seized by his own generals and compelled to form a Second United Front with the CCP against Japan.
  • Full-scale war: the Marco Polo Bridge Incident (7 July 1937) near Beiping (Beijing) began the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937-1945). Japan took Shanghai and Nanjing (the Nanjing Massacre, December 1937 - January 1938) and Wuhan and Guangzhou (1938); the Nationalist government moved to Chongqing.
  • The war exhausted the KMT's National Revolutionary Army (the "Nationalist army"), which fought conventional battles to defend cities; the communists fought a guerrilla war behind Japanese lines and mobilised the countryside. By 1945 the CCP controlled base areas of about 100 million people.
  • In the last days of World War II the USSR invaded Manchuria (and northern Korea) in August 1945 †.

[Correction] The notes compress two stages: Japan occupied Manchuria in 1931 and created Manchukuo in 1932, but the full-scale invasion of China came only in July 1937. The Second United Front followed the Xi'an Incident of December 1936; it was formalised in 1937 and broke down in practice after the New Fourth Army Incident of January 1941 †. Sources: Britannica - Second Sino-Japanese War; Britannica - Marco Polo Bridge Incident.

[Verification Required: "USA helped with $2 billion" and "the KMT lost 4-5 million troops" - figures for wartime aid and Chinese military losses vary widely by source and definition and could not be confirmed. For scale: US Lend-Lease aid to the Chinese government totalled about $1.63 billion, of which about $849 million before 2 September 1945 and $778 million after (State Department figures, 1948); in 1938-40 the USSR extended about $250 million in military credits and the USA, Britain and France some $263.5 million for civilian purposes (Britannica). Sources: FRUS 1948, vol. VIII, doc. 585; Britannica - History of China: the late republican period.]

18.14 The Civil War (1946-1949) and the People's Republic of China

  • After Japan's surrender, US-mediated talks failed and full civil war resumed in 1946-47: the communist PLA against the KMT's Nationalist army.
  • The USSR handed captured Japanese weapons in Manchuria to the communists †; the USA aided the KMT (Lend-Lease after V-J Day, the China Aid Act of 1948). [Verification Required: the note's "USA provided 1.5 billion dollars" - see the figures in 18.13.]
  • The CCP's land-reform programme won peasant support, while "the Nationalists' ineptitude and demoralization" (Britannica) - inflation, corruption, war-weariness - cost them support. The PLA won the decisive campaigns (1948-49) and took control of the mainland.
  • On 1 October 1949 Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China in Beijing. The KMT leaders retreated to Taiwan (December 1949 †), where the Republic of China continued and was recognised by the USA and many of its allies as the government of China (Britain, however, recognised the PRC in January 1950 †).

[Correction] The Republic of China on Taiwan did not "exist till 1970-71" - it still exists. What changed in 1971 was China's seat in the UN: General Assembly Resolution 2758 (25 October 1971), adopted 76-35 with 17 abstentions, recognised the PRC's representatives as "the only legitimate representatives of China to the United Nations" and expelled "the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek". Most states that still recognised Taipei switched to Beijing in the 1970s (the USA on 1 January 1979 †). Sources: UNGA Resolution 2758 (text, Wikisource); UN Mandate Registry - A/RES/2758 (XXVI).

[Additional] India link: India recognised the PRC on 30 December 1949, one of the first non-communist states to do so †, and supported the PRC's claim to China's UN seat.

Mains Points

  • Why the USSR fell - structure versus agency: long-term political and economic weaknesses (Table 17.11) made the system brittle; Gorbachev's glasnost, perestroika, democratisation and "Sinatra Doctrine" explain the timing; nationalism in the republics and the failed coup sealed it (17.7).
  • Reform paradox: reforms meant to save socialism dissolved it - glasnost removed fear, perestroika broke the command system without building a market, democratisation created rival centres such as Yeltsin's Russia (Table 17.12).
  • The role of the USA is contested: "Reagan victory" versus documentary evidence that the USA backed Gorbachev and the Union until 1991 (c11).
  • Impact of the Cold War across political, economic, social and technological dimensions, with India's non-alignment and the 1991 turn (Table 17.15).
  • From bipolar to unipolar to multipolar: EU, G20, BRICS and the rise of China and India (17.10).
  • Imperialism and the decline of the Qing: unequal treaties, indemnities and spheres of influence; internal crises (population, famine, corruption, Taiping) (Table 18.2, 18.4).
  • Why 1911 produced a republic but not a modern state: a revolution triggered by army units and provincial elites; Yuan Shikai's dictatorship; warlordism (18.8, 18.9).
  • May Fourth as a turning point from liberal reform to Marxism and mass nationalism (18.10).
  • Why the CCP won (1949): peasant mobilisation and land reform, guerrilla strategy, patriotic legitimacy in the war against Japan, KMT corruption and exhaustion (Table 18.5, Figure 2).
  • Mao versus Lenin: a peasant-based revolution from rural bases, fusing communism with nationalism, rather than an urban workers' insurrection (18.12).
  • India links: opium from India financed Britain's China trade; India recognised the PRC early; the end of the USSR fed into India's 1991 reforms.

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Mikhail Gorbachev: last Soviet leader (General Secretary 1985-91, President 1990-91); glasnost, perestroika, New Thinking.
  • Boris Yeltsin: Russian leader; elected RSFSR President Jun 1991; resisted the August coup; signed the Belavezha Accords.
  • Gennady Yanayev: Soviet Vice-President; nominal head of the August 1991 coup committee.
  • Vladimir Kryuchkov: KGB chairman; chief organiser of the coup.
  • Eduard Shevardnadze / Gennadi Gerasimov: Soviet Foreign Minister / Foreign Ministry spokesman who named the "Sinatra Doctrine".
  • Leonid Kravchuk / Stanislav Shushkevich: leaders of Ukraine / Belarus at Belavezha.
  • Politburo: top policy body of the CPSU. GKChP: State Committee on the State of Emergency (coup committee). CIS: Commonwealth of Independent States (1991).
  • Glasnost / Perestroika / Uskoreniye / Demokratizatsiya: openness / restructuring / acceleration / democratisation.
  • Brezhnev Doctrine vs Sinatra Doctrine: right of Soviet intervention to defend socialism (1968) vs freedom of each bloc state to choose its path (1989).
  • Empress Dowager Cixi: power behind the Qing throne 1861-1908. Puyi: last Qing emperor (1908-12); head of Manchukuo.
  • Lin Zexu: Qing commissioner who destroyed British opium at Canton, 1839.
  • Hong Xiuquan: leader of the Taiping Rebellion, "Heavenly King".
  • Zeng Guofan / Li Hongzhang: Qing officials whose regional armies crushed the Taipings; Li signed Shimonoseki (1895) and the Boxer Protocol (1901).
  • Frederick Townsend Ward / Charles Gordon: commanders of the Ever-Victorious Army.
  • Boxers (Yihequan): anti-foreign, anti-Christian peasant movement, Shandong.
  • Sun Yat-sen: founder of the Tongmenghui and the KMT; provisional president 1912; Three Principles of the People.
  • Tongmenghui: United League / Chinese Revolutionary Alliance (Tokyo, 1905).
  • Yuan Shikai: Beiyang Army commander; President 1912-16; tried to make himself emperor.
  • Song Jiaoren: organiser of the KMT's 1913 election victory; assassinated Mar 1913.
  • Kuomintang (KMT): Nationalist Party (1912). National Revolutionary Army (NRA): its army - the note's "Nationalist army".
  • Chen Duxiu: Peking University dean, editor of New Youth, first CCP leader. Li Dazhao: Peking University librarian, early Marxist, CCP co-founder.
  • Mao Zedong: CCP leader from the Long March (Zunyi, 1935); proclaimed the PRC. Zhou Enlai: CCP leader, later PRC Premier. Zhu De: commander of the Red Army.
  • Chiang Kai-shek: Whampoa commandant; KMT leader and head of the Nationalist government 1928-49; then on Taiwan.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Perestroika (Apr 1985, economic restructuring) vs glasnost (late 1986, openness) - not both "1985".
  • Belavezha Accords (8 Dec, 3 Slavic republics, CIS founded) vs Alma-Ata Protocol (21 Dec, 11 republics).
  • Yanayev led the coup committee; Yeltsin defeated it; Gorbachev was its target, held in Crimea.
  • Yeltsin became President of Russia (RSFSR) in Jun 1991 - Gorbachev was President of the USSR.
  • The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 Nov 1989; Germany was reunified on 3 Oct 1990.
  • BRIC (2009) became BRICS only after South Africa joined (2010-11).
  • Qing from 1644 (mid-17th century); Cixi was Empress Dowager, not queen regnant.
  • Second Opium War is 1856-60; Tianjin (1858) vs Convention of Peking (1860).
  • Nanking (1842): Hong Kong Island; Peking (1860): Kowloon; 1898: New Territories lease.
  • Shimonoseki indemnity 200 m taels; Boxer indemnity 450 m taels - on China, not on Cixi.
  • First Sino-Japanese War 1894-95 vs Second 1937-45; Russo-Japanese War 1904-05.
  • Xinhai Revolution was begun by an army mutiny at Wuchang; Sun Yat-sen was in the USA.
  • Versailles gave Shandong to Japan - hence May Fourth.
  • Li Dazhao and Chen Duxiu - both Peking University.
  • Shanghai massacre (Apr 1927) ended the First United Front; the Xi'an Incident (Dec 1936) produced the Second.
  • Mukden Incident (1931) - Manchuria; Marco Polo Bridge (1937) - full-scale war.
  • "Shensi" = Shaanxi (Yan'an); do not confuse with Shanxi.
  • The ROC on Taiwan still exists; 1971 is when it lost China's UN seat (Res. 2758).

30-Second Revision

The USSR was brittle - Politburo oligarchy, no freedoms, a stagnant command economy, crushing Cold War costs - but Gorbachev's reforms decided the timing: perestroika (1985; market steps 1987-88) broke the planned economy, glasnost (1986) ended fear, multi-candidate elections (1989) and the "Sinatra Doctrine" (Oct 1989) let eastern Europe go (Wall opened 9 Nov 1989; Germany reunited 1990). Lithuania (Mar 1990) led the republics out; the August 1991 coup (Yanayev) failed and Yeltsin rose; Belavezha (8 Dec), Alma-Ata (21 Dec), Gorbachev resigned (25 Dec), USSR gone (26 Dec 1991). Result: unipolar US moment, then a multipolar world (EU, G20, BRICS). China: Qing (1644) weakened by opium from India, the Opium Wars and unequal treaties (Nanking 1842, Tianjin 1858, Peking 1860), the Taiping (1850-64), Japan (Shimonoseki 1895) and the Boxers (1901). The Wuchang mutiny (10 Oct 1911) ended the empire (Puyi out 12 Feb 1912); Yuan Shikai and the warlords followed. May Fourth (1919) bred the CCP (1921). The First United Front (1924-27) ended in the Shanghai massacre; the CCP survived via Jiangxi and the Long March (1934-35, Mao at Zunyi), joined the KMT against Japan (1937-45) and won the civil war - PRC, 1 Oct 1949.
