11. World War I (1914-1918) - continued

11.2 Causes of World War I (part 2: causes 3-7)

Causes 1 (nationalism) and 2 (secret diplomacy and the alliance system) are in the 11-14 note, with Figure 3 there showing how all the causes fit together.

Cause 3: Economic (business) imperialism and colonial competition

  • The spread of industrialisation created business competition and a race for raw materials, markets and colonies among the industrial powers of Europe.
  • Britain was the most powerful country in the world, with the largest colonial empire.
  • Germany had equalled (in steel and chemicals, overtaken) Britain industrially and economically, and had the strongest army in Europe. But its colonial empire was neither as large nor as profitable as Britain's.
  • Comparison in the notes, "23% : 0.6":

    [Correction] The figures make sense as shares of the world's population. In 1913 the British Empire ruled about 412 million people, about 23% of the world population (Maddison's estimate). Germany's colonies held only about 12 million people †, roughly 0.6-0.7% of a world population of about 1.8 billion. [Verification Required] the German colonial population figure was not checked against a page opened for this note. By area, the British Empire covered about a quarter of the world's land (about 24% by 1920).

  • To achieve global hegemony (Weltpolitik, a "place in the sun"), many in Germany believed it necessary to challenge Britain, if necessary militarily. This caused enmity between the two countries.
  • Flashpoints: Morocco (1905, 1911), the Berlin-Baghdad Railway (a threat to the route to India), and the scramble for the Ottoman and Chinese markets (11-14 note, Map 3).

Cause 4: Militarism and the Anglo-German naval race

  • Germany decided to challenge Britain at sea and began a naval race with the Navy Law of 1898 (and a larger one in 1900), under Admiral Tirpitz.
  • In 1906 Britain launched HMS Dreadnought, an all-big-gun battleship that made every older battleship obsolete. This restarted the race on equal terms, because both sides now had to build dreadnoughts from scratch.
  • By 1914 both had built dreadnoughts and "super-dreadnoughts". The race poisoned Anglo-German relations and pushed Britain towards France and Russia.
  • On land, every power expanded its army, adopted conscription (except Britain) and drew up rigid war plans (Germany's Schlieffen Plan, France's Plan XVII). Once mobilisation began, the plans' timetables left little room for diplomacy.

[Correction] The notes say Germany "matched Britain's naval capability by 1906". It never did. In 1914 Britain had about 29 dreadnoughts against Germany's 17 (Imperial War Museums figure, see the 11-14 note). The point is that the Dreadnought (1906) cancelled Britain's lead in older ships and so renewed the race.

Cause 5: A series of crises among the European powers

  • The Moroccan crises (Tangier 1905-06; Agadir 1911), the Bosnian crisis (1908-09) and the Balkan Wars (1912-13) deepened enmity and created mutual distrust and suspicion (11-14 note, 10.9-10.10).
  • Each crisis ended with one side humiliated (Russia in 1909, Germany in 1911), so each was less willing to back down next time.
  • The Balkans became the "powder keg of Europe": a minor Balkan conflict involving Serbia could become a continent-wide war through the alliance system.

Cause 6: The press, government attitudes and the absence of international control

  • Newspapers in every country encouraged aggressive nationalism and spread hostility (for example, against "perfidious Albion", the "Hun" or the "Slav peril").
  • Many European governments treated war as a "necessary evil" that could unite the people and distract attention from domestic problems (strikes, socialism, Irish Home Rule, the nationalities of Austria-Hungary).
  • There was no powerful international organisation to restrain them, so the powers prepared for a serious war.
[Additional] The Hague Peace Conferences (1899, 1907) set up the Permanent Court of Arbitration and rules of war, but arbitration was voluntary and disarmament failed. This gap is what the League of Nations was meant to fill in 1919 (11.11).

Cause 7: The immediate cause - the assassination at Sarajevo (28 June 1914)

  • Who and where: Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and his wife Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg, were shot in Sarajevo (capital of Bosnia, annexed by Austria-Hungary in 1908) on 28 June 1914 during an official visit.
  • By whom: Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb student of the Young Bosnia (Mlada Bosna) movement. Princip was arrested; the inquiry showed that the conspirators had been armed and helped across the border by the Serbian secret society Black Hand ("Unification or Death"), led by Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijevic (Apis), head of Serbian military intelligence †.
  • Why: South Slav (Yugoslav) nationalists wanted to free Bosnia from Austria-Hungary and unite it with Serbia.
  • Consequence: Austria-Hungary treated the assassination as an act of war, blamed Serbia, and used it to crush Serbian nationalism.

[Correction] Franz Ferdinand was the heir presumptive (nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph), usually called the "Archduke", not the "crown prince". His wife was Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg. Note also: the Austrian inquiry linked the plot to Serbian officers and the Black Hand, but it did not prove that the Serbian government had ordered it †. Austria-Hungary still held Serbia responsible.

11.3 The July Crisis: from Sarajevo to a European war (28 June - 4 August 1914)

  • Austria-Hungary waited before acting, because it first needed to be sure of German support.
  • On 5-6 July Kaiser William II and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg promised Austria-Hungary unconditional support: the "blank cheque".
  • On 23 July Austria-Hungary sent Serbia a harsh ultimatum with a 48-hour deadline. Serbia's reply of 25 July accepted most points but refused to let Austro-Hungarian officials take part in the inquiry inside Serbia.
  • Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914; Belgrade was bombarded from across the Danube the next day. This is taken as the start of the war.
  • Russia, Serbia's protector, ordered general mobilisation on 30 July.
  • Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August. France, Russia's ally, began mobilising the same day, and Germany declared war on France on 3 August.
  • Germany now faced a war on two fronts, which it had wanted to avoid. Its answer was the Schlieffen Plan (memorandum of December 1905, by Count Alfred von Schlieffen, chief of the General Staff 1891-1906): defeat France quickly, within about six weeks, then turn on Russia, which was expected to mobilise slowly.
  • The plan required invading France through Belgium, avoiding the French border forts. But Belgium was neutral, its neutrality guaranteed by the Treaty of London, 1839, which Prussia itself had signed. Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August, calling the treaty "a scrap of paper" (Bethmann Hollweg †).
  • Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914, citing Belgian neutrality (and its entente with France).
  • Thus Austria-Hungary's attack on Serbia set off a chain reaction within the alliance system. A local conflict became a European war, and with the empires, Japan, the Ottoman Empire and later the USA, a global war.

[Correction] The notes put the attack on Belgrade on 28 July. Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July; the bombardment of Belgrade began on 29 July †. Also, the "blank cheque" came early in July, before the ultimatum, not after the declaration of war.

11.5 Course of the war (1914-1918)

11.5.1 The war at sea: blockade against U-boats

  • British naval blockade of Germany (1914-1919): a "distant blockade" closing the North Sea (Scotland-Norway line) and the Strait of Dover, to cut Germany's foreign trade and weaken it economically and militarily. From 1915 the Allies treated almost all goods, including food, as contraband.
  • Effects on Germany: serious shortages of food, coal, fertilisers (nitrates), oil, rubber and medicines; falling industrial and farm output; rationing, inflation and unemployment; disease and a famine-like "turnip winter" (1916-17).
  • Deaths: in December 1918 the German Board of Public Health claimed 763,000 civilian deaths from the blockade; a 1928 study put it at 424,000, and later scholars give similar or lower figures. The notes' "5-8 lakh" fits this range, but the numbers are disputed because of the 1918 influenza. The blockade continued after the armistice until July 1919, to force Germany to sign the peace.
  • [Verification Required] The notes' figure of a "50% fall" in Germany's foreign trade was not checked; estimates of the fall in imports vary by year and source.
  • Germany's reply - unrestricted submarine warfare (U-boats): from February 1915 Germany declared the waters round Britain a war zone where merchant ships, including neutral ones, could be sunk without warning.
    • On 7 May 1915 the U-20 sank the British liner RMS Lusitania off the south coast of Ireland: 1,198 killed, including 128 Americans.
    • US President Woodrow Wilson warned Germany not to attack passenger and neutral merchant ships.
  • Battle of Jutland (31 May - 1 June 1916), North Sea, off Denmark: the only great clash of the British Grand Fleet (Jellicoe) and the German High Seas Fleet (Scheer). Britain lost more ships and men, but the German fleet returned to port and stayed there. Tactically inconclusive; strategically, Britain kept control of the sea and the blockade.

[Correction] Germany did not simply "stop" unrestricted submarine warfare in 1915. After the Lusitania it restricted attacks on large passenger liners (the Arabic pledge, September 1915), and after the sinking of the Sussex it promised not to sink merchant ships without warning (the Sussex pledge, May 1916) †. It resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February 1917, as the Zimmermann Telegram itself states.

11.5.2 The Western Front: the Schlieffen Plan fails and trench warfare begins

  • Germany invaded Belgium and France under the Schlieffen Plan (as modified by Moltke the Younger).
  • Battles of the Frontiers (August 1914): the siege of the Liege forts (5-16 August) and the Battle of Mons (23 August, against the British Expeditionary Force) were German successes. In the French offensive into Lorraine and the Ardennes (Plan XVII) the French suffered huge losses. Germany advanced to within about 40 km of Paris, and the French government moved to Bordeaux.
  • First Battle of the Marne (6-12 September 1914): French and British forces (Joffre; the "taxis of the Marne") counter-attacked into a gap between the German First and Second Armies. The Germans were defeated and fell back to the River Aisne.
  • The Schlieffen Plan had failed: there was no quick victory, and Germany now had to fight on two fronts.
  • After the First Battle of the Aisne (from 13 September) and the "Race to the Sea" (September-October), each side tried to outflank the other until the lines reached the coast. Both sides dug networks of trenches. This line from the Belgian coast to the Swiss border was the Western Front; its trench systems ran about 765 km (475 miles) (National WWI Museum), close to the "800 km" in the notes.
  • Trench warfare: machine guns, artillery and barbed wire favoured defence; attacks gained a few kilometres at enormous cost. New weapons were tried to break the deadlock: poison gas (Ypres, April 1915), tanks (Somme, September 1916), aircraft †.
  • Battle of Verdun (21 February - 18 December 1916): Germany (Falkenhayn) tried to "bleed France white"; France held (Petain: "They shall not pass").
  • Battle of the Somme (1 July - 18 November 1916): a British-French offensive, partly to relieve Verdun; on the first day alone the British suffered about 57,000 casualties †.
  • The war became a deadlock (stalemate) and a war of attrition.

[Correction] The notes list the Aisne among Germany's victories in its advance. The First Battle of the Aisne came after the Marne, when the Allies attacked the retreating Germans; it was indecisive and began the trench lines †. The notes also say "1.4 million people got killed" in these battles. This figure could not be matched to a source: [Verification Required]. Losses are normally given as casualties (killed, wounded, missing and captured), not deaths. The National WWI Museum states that by the end of 1914 the dead and wounded on all fronts already exceeded four million.

[Correction] After the First Marne the Germans retreated about 60-70 km to the Aisne river (north of Reims and Soissons), not to the "French-Belgian border". Most of north-eastern France and almost all of Belgium stayed under German occupation until 1918.

[Correction] The notes say that about 1 million soldiers were "killed" at Verdun and the Somme. These are casualty figures, not deaths: about 700,000 French and German casualties at Verdun and over 1 million casualties at the Somme alone. Deaths were a smaller share of these (estimates vary) †.

11.5.3 The Eastern Front: Russia's weakness

  • Russia mobilised faster than Germany expected and invaded East Prussia, which forced Germany to move troops east: the Eastern Front.
  • Germany and Austria-Hungary fought Russia. Russia's strength was its huge population (the "Russian steamroller"), but it lacked arms, shells, railways and good leadership.
  • Battle of Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914), East Prussia: Hindenburg and Ludendorff destroyed the Russian Second Army (Samsonov), a humiliating defeat; it was followed by the Masurian Lakes (September 1914) †.
  • In 1915 the Central Powers drove Russia out of Poland and Lithuania. Russia's Brusilov Offensive (June-September 1916) against Austria-Hungary was its last great success [Additional] †.
  • Supplies could not reach Russia: the Baltic Sea was controlled by Germany and the Black Sea exit through the Dardanelles and Bosporus was closed by the Ottoman Empire. Britain and France tried to force the Straits in the Gallipoli campaign (April 1915 - January 1916), which failed [Additional].
  • Russian losses were huge, but the Tsar continued the war and even took personal command (September 1915). This made him deeply unpopular and helped cause the Russian Revolutions of 1917 (12).

[Correction] "Russia lost 5 million soldiers in 2 years" should read as casualties (killed, wounded and especially prisoners), not deaths. Estimates of Russian military deaths for the whole war are roughly 1.7-2 million †. [Verification Required] the exact two-year casualty total.

11.5.4 The war beyond Europe

  • Japan declared war on Germany on 23 August 1914 and captured Germany's possessions in Asia and the Pacific: the naval base of Tsingtao (Qingdao) in Kiautschou, Shandong (November 1914), and the German islands north of the equator (October 1914) †.
  • Britain and France (with South Africa, Belgium and others) attacked Germany's colonies in Africa: Togoland (August 1914), Kamerun (1914-16), German South West Africa (taken by South Africa, July 1915) and German East Africa, where General Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck held out until after the armistice (25 November 1918) †. Australia and New Zealand took German New Guinea and Samoa (1914).
  • The Ottoman fronts: Gallipoli, Mesopotamia (Basra 1914; the British-Indian surrender at Kut, 29 April 1916; Baghdad taken March 1917), Sinai-Palestine (Jerusalem, December 1917) and the Arab Revolt (1916) [Additional] †.
  • India link: Indian troops fought in France (from autumn 1914; Neuve Chapelle, March 1915), Mesopotamia (the largest Indian commitment), East Africa, Egypt, Gallipoli and Palestine. See the figures in 11.7.

11.5.5 The position of the USA and its entry into the war (April 1917)

  • Tradition: the USA had long followed a policy of isolation and neutrality towards European wars (Washington's Farewell Address, 1796; the Monroe Doctrine, 1823 †). In 1914 Wilson declared neutrality.
  • In the early years the USA traded with both sides, but because Britain controlled the seas, trade and credit flowed mainly to the Allies. Gradually the USA's neutrality leaned towards Britain and France.
  • Loans: the USA supplied the Allies with goods and credit. The notes' "about $10 billion to Britain, France and Italy" is the approximate total of US government war loans.

    [Correction] Most of the roughly $10 billion of US government loans to the Allies were made after the USA entered the war (1917-1920) †. Before April 1917 the credit came mainly from private American banks (above all J.P. Morgan), about $2-2.5 billion †. Either way, American industry and finance had a large stake in an Allied victory, a point often made by "revisionist" historians in the 1930s.

  • The Lusitania (1915) and Wilson's warning turned American opinion against Germany (11.5.1).
  • 1917 - the decisive provocations:
    • Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February 1917, gambling that it could starve Britain before the USA could make a difference. The USA broke off relations on 3 February.
    • U-boats sank American merchant ships in February-March 1917 (for example the Laconia, 26 February, with Americans aboard; three American ships on 18 March †). The notes' "3 commercial vessels" refers to these.
    • The Zimmermann Telegram: German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann sent a coded telegram to the German minister in Mexico (relayed via Washington on 19 January 1917). It proposed that if the USA entered the war, Mexico should ally with Germany and "reconquer the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona", and that Japan be invited to join. British naval intelligence ("Room 40") decoded it; Britain gave it to the US ambassador, who telegraphed it to Washington on 24 February; it was published on 1 March 1917.
  • Congress declared a state of war with Germany on 6 April 1917. The American Expeditionary Forces under General John J. Pershing arrived in France in growing numbers from 1918.
  • Effect: on the Western Front the US entry gave the Allies fresh men, money, food and ships, just as Russia was collapsing.

11.5.6 After the US entry: Russia leaves, Germany's last gamble and defeat (1917-1918)

  • Eastern Front - Russia leaves the war: the February Revolution (March NS) 1917 ended the Tsar's rule; the Provisional Government continued the war. The Bolsheviks seized power in the October Revolution (25 October OS / 7 November NS 1917) with the slogan "Peace, Land and Bread". They agreed an armistice in December 1917 and, after a renewed German advance, signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918 with the Central Powers.
  • Terms of Brest-Litovsk: Russia gave up Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, Belarus and Ukraine, and Kars and Batum in the Caucasus (to the Ottoman Empire), and confirmed the independence of Finland. These lands held about 34% of the empire's population, 54% of its industry, 89% of its coalfields and 26% of its railways; Britannica speaks of over a quarter of its population and a third of its grain land. A protocol of August 1918 added reparations of 6 billion marks. Lenin accepted it as a "breathing space" for the revolution.
  • The notes' "25 lakh km2" of territory lost: [Verification Required] figures for the area vary widely by source (roughly 1-2.6 million km2 depending on what is counted) †. The "one-third of the population" in the notes is supported.
  • Germany was now free to move about a million men west and became, briefly, very strong. The treaty was annulled by the armistice of 11 November 1918.
  • Western Front - Germany's last gamble: Ludendorff's Spring Offensive (from 21 March 1918) drove the Allies back and again reached the Marne, but exhausted Germany's last reserves.
  • Second Battle of the Marne (15 July - 6 August 1918): the German attack was stopped and turned back by French forces with American, British and Italian help. Then came the Allied Hundred Days Offensive from Amiens (8 August 1918, Ludendorff's "black day of the German army") through the Hindenburg Line.
  • Wilson's Fourteen Points (8 January 1918), a speech to a joint session of the US Congress, set out a programme for peace and order: open diplomacy (no secret treaties), freedom of the seas, free trade, arms reduction, adjustment of colonial claims, self-determination for the peoples of Europe (Poland, Austria-Hungary, the Balkans, the Ottoman Empire) and, in Point 14, a "general association of nations" (the League of Nations).
  • Germany's allies collapsed: Bulgaria signed an armistice at Salonica (29 September 1918), the Ottoman Empire at Mudros (30 October) and Austria-Hungary at Villa Giusti (3 November) after defeat at Vittorio Veneto.
  • Germany was left alone, exhausted by war and blockade and fighting singly.

[Correction] Germany was not defeated at the Second Marne "due to the USA's power" alone. The battle was mainly a French victory (Foch was Allied supreme commander from spring 1918), with American divisions playing an important supporting part. US strength mattered most in the later offensives (St Mihiel and Meuse-Argonne, September-November 1918) and as a promise of unlimited reinforcements †.

[Correction] The Fourteen Points were given on 8 January 1918, before the Spring Offensive and the Second Marne, not "at the same time" as them. They were delivered to a joint session of Congress (the USA has a Congress, not a parliament). The surrenders of 1918 were armistices (cease-fires); formal peace came with the treaties of 1919-1923.

11.6 The end of the war in Germany (November 1918)

  • Kaiser William II was not ready to surrender or abdicate. In late October the navy was ordered out for a last battle; the sailors mutinied at Kiel (3 November 1918), and workers' and soldiers' councils spread across Germany: the German Revolution.
  • The Chancellor, Prince Max von Baden (appointed October 1918 to seek peace on the Fourteen Points), announced the Kaiser's abdication on 9 November 1918 and handed the chancellorship to the Social Democrat Friedrich Ebert.
  • The same day Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed Germany a republic from the Reichstag. A provisional government, the Council of People's Deputies, headed by Ebert, took over on 10 November.
  • The Kaiser fled to the Netherlands (10 November) and signed his formal abdication on 28 November 1918.
  • Armistice of 11 November 1918: signed in a railway carriage in the Forest of Compiegne by a German delegation led by Matthias Erzberger; fighting stopped at 11 a.m.
  • A National Assembly met at Weimar and adopted the Weimar Constitution (passed 31 July, in force 14 August 1919) †. The Weimar Republic lasted until the Nazi seizure of power in 1933. Ebert became its first president.
  • It was the new republic that had to sign the peace treaty: the Treaty of Versailles, 28 June 1919 (11.10).

[Correction] Max von Baden did not "convince" the Kaiser to abdicate: he announced the abdication on 9 November without the Kaiser's consent, to head off revolution; William signed only on 28 November in exile. Ebert headed the Council of People's Deputies (from 10 November), and the republic was proclaimed on 9 November. Germany signed an armistice, not an unconditional surrender.

[Correction] The Weimar Republic lasted from 1918/1919 to 1933, not 1934: Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933 and the Enabling Act (23 March 1933) ended parliamentary rule †.

What is the "stab-in-the-back" myth (Dolchstosslegende)? (asked in class)
  • The claim: the German army was "undefeated in the field" in 1918 and lost only because it was betrayed at home by strikers, socialists, revolutionaries, republican politicians and, in its antisemitic form, Jews. The civilians who signed the armistice were branded the "November criminals".
  • Origin: it was spread by the High Command, which had hidden the true military situation from the public. On 18 November 1919, before a parliamentary committee of inquiry, Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg (with Ludendorff) quoted an alleged English officer: "the German army was stabbed in the back" (Bundesarchiv; USHMM).
  • Why it was believed: no Allied army had entered Germany; German troops still stood in France and Belgium in November 1918; Germany had beaten Russia; and the news of defeat came suddenly.
  • The truth: historians reject the myth. Ludendorff himself had demanded an armistice on 29 September 1918, because Germany had no reserves left, its allies had collapsed and the Allies, reinforced by the USA, were advancing.
  • Consequences: it discredited the Weimar Republic and the Treaty of Versailles, and was used by the Nazis and other right-wing groups to attack socialists, communists and Jews.

11.7 Causes of the Allied victory

  1. Vast resources of the Allies in men, money, food and raw materials, especially Britain's colonial resources and the Dominions.
    India's contribution [Additional] (Government of India, India's Contribution to the Great War, 1923, as quoted by India1914 and 1914-1918-online):
    • Men: 877,068 combatants and 563,369 non-combatants recruited to 31 December 1919, a total of 1,440,437 (the notes' 8.8 lakh and 5.6 lakh). More than a million served overseas: France and Belgium, Mesopotamia, Egypt, Gallipoli, Palestine, East Africa.
    • Money: contributions from Indian revenues of about £146.2 million by 1919-20, including an outright "gift" of £100 million in 1917.
    • Supplies and animals: about 3.7 million tonnes of supplies and stores, and about 1.7-1.8 lakh animals sent overseas.
    • Losses: official figures of 1922 give 53,486 Indians dead, 64,350 wounded and 2,937 missing by the end of 1919; other estimates are higher (about 74,000 dead).
    • Recognition: 11 Victoria Crosses; Sepoy Khudadad Khan (Ypres, October 1914) was the first Indian-born VC. The India Gate in Delhi commemorates the dead.

    [Correction] The notes give "1.8 M animals". The figure is about 184,350 animals sent overseas (horses, ponies, mules, camels, bullocks and cattle), i.e. about 1.8 lakh, not 1.8 million (India1914, from the 1923 official volume; the Hindustan Times gives "over 170,000"). 11.8 Redrawing national boundaries: the Paris Peace Conference (1919-1920)

  • When and where: opened on 18 January 1919 in Paris (the anniversary of the proclamation of the German Empire in 1871); it worked until January 1920, and the treaties were signed in palaces around Paris.
  • Who: delegates of 32 countries (and nationalities) took part, but the conference was dominated by the "Big Three": Woodrow Wilson (USA), David Lloyd George (Britain) and Georges Clemenceau (France, the conference president). With Vittorio Orlando (Italy) they formed the "Big Four".
  • Not invited: the defeated (enemy) countries, which were simply handed the terms, and Soviet Russia: the Bolshevik government was not recognised by the capitalist Western powers, and Russia was in civil war (the Allies were even intervening against the Bolsheviks). Neutral countries took no part in the negotiations.
  • Italy's limited influence: Italy's war effort was significant but not as great as that of the main Allies; its claims (Fiume, Dalmatia) clashed with Wilson's self-determination, and Orlando walked out in April 1919.
  • Conflicting aims: Wilson wanted a peace based on the Fourteen Points and a League of Nations; Clemenceau wanted security and to weaken Germany permanently; Lloyd George wanted to punish Germany but not ruin it as a trading partner, and to secure Britain's naval and imperial interests.
  • Result: five peace treaties, one with each defeated state, which redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East (table, Map 5).
  • India link: India was represented within the British Empire delegation and signed the Treaty of Versailles (Edwin Montagu and the Maharaja of Bikaner, Ganga Singh †), so it became a founder member of the League of Nations although it was not self-governing.

11.10 The Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) explained (asked in class)

Who, what, when, where: the peace treaty between the Allied and Associated Powers and Germany, signed in the Hall of Mirrors of the Palace of Versailles on 28 June 1919, five years to the day after Sarajevo, and in the same hall where the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871. The terms were presented to the Germans on 7 May 1919 with no real negotiation; Germany signed under threat of renewed war (and the continuing blockade). It came into force on 10 January 1920.

Outcomes and assessment of Versailles (asked in class)

  • German resentment: all German parties protested. Germans called it a "Diktat" (dictated peace), argued that it broke the Fourteen Points (self-determination was denied to Germans in Austria, the Sudetenland, Danzig and the Corridor) and hated the "honour clauses" (Articles 227-231).
  • Weakening of the Weimar Republic: the republic that signed was blamed for it (stab-in-the-back myth). Reparations led to the French-Belgian occupation of the Ruhr (January 1923) and the hyperinflation of 1923; payments were rescheduled by the Dawes Plan (1924) and Young Plan (1929) and in effect ended at Lausanne in 1932 †.
  • Rise of Hitler: the Nazis exploited the grievances. Hitler broke the treaty step by step: rearmament and conscription (1935), the remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936), the Anschluss with Austria (1938), the Sudetenland (1938), and finally Danzig and the Corridor, which led to the invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939.
  • Too harsh or too weak? Critics such as J.M. Keynes (The Economic Consequences of the Peace, 1919) called it a vindictive "Carthaginian peace" that would wreck Europe's economy. Others argue that it was too harsh to conciliate Germany but too weak to keep it down: Germany remained united and potentially the strongest power in Europe. Britannica notes that many historians blame the combination of a harsh treaty and its lax enforcement; by 1938 only its territorial articles still stood.
  • Weak foundations of the peace: the US Senate rejected the treaty (votes of November 1919 and March 1920), so the USA never joined the League; Soviet Russia was excluded; Italy and Japan were dissatisfied; and new states contained large minorities.
  • Contemporary verdict (attributed to Marshal Foch): "This is not peace. It is an armistice for twenty years" †.
[Additional] Historiography: the "Versailles caused WWII" thesis is a common exam framing, but it is an interpretation. Historians such as Margaret MacMillan (Peacemakers, 2001) argue that the peacemakers faced impossible conditions and that later choices (the Depression, appeasement, Hitler's own aims) mattered more †. Write it as "contained the seeds of", then weigh other causes.

11.11 The League of Nations and the mandate system

  • Origin: proposed in the 14th of Wilson's Fourteen Points; Wilson chaired the commission that drafted the Covenant (26 articles), adopted on 28 April 1919 and placed as Part I of every peace treaty. British and South African drafts (Lord Robert Cecil, Jan Smuts) also shaped it †.
  • Founded: came into existence on 10 January 1920; headquarters in Geneva. Main organs: the Assembly, the Council, the Secretariat; linked bodies: the Permanent Court of International Justice (The Hague) and the ILO.
  • Aim: "to promote international cooperation and to achieve international peace and security" through collective security (an aggressor against one member is an aggressor against all), the peaceful settlement of disputes, disarmament and an end to secret diplomacy (UN Geneva).
  • Membership: 42 founding members. In all 63 states were members at some time between 1920 and 1946, but never all together; the largest membership at one time was 58 (September 1934 - February 1935).
  • The USA, whose president had proposed it, never joined: the Senate vote on the treaty (49-35) fell short of the two-thirds majority required.
  • Lack of legitimacy and strength from the beginning: it was seen as a club of the victors (enforcing the peace treaties); the defeated states were excluded at first (Germany joined only in 1926, the USSR in 1934); the USA was absent; and it had no army of its own and decided by unanimity.
  • India link: India was a founder member (as a signatory of Versailles) and a founder member of the ILO.

[Correction] The notes say the League "had 42 and later 63 members" and that "neutral and enemy countries were not allowed". (1) 63 is the total number of states that were ever members; the maximum at one time was 58 (the UN Office at Geneva gives "at most 60"). (2) Neutral states were in fact invited: the Covenant invited 13 neutral states, and all 13 joined within two months. It was the Paris Peace Conference, not the League, that excluded neutrals from its negotiations. (3) The defeated states were excluded at first but could be admitted later by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly: Austria and Bulgaria in 1920, Hungary in 1922, Germany in 1926 †.

The mandate system (Article 22 of the Covenant) [Additional - the notes left this heading blank]

  • What: the former German colonies and the Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire were not annexed outright but placed under the "tutelage" of "advanced nations" (the mandatory powers) on behalf of the League, until they could "stand alone". The mandatories reported every year to the League's Permanent Mandates Commission.
  • Why: a compromise between Wilson's opposition to annexation and the Allies' wish to keep their conquests.
  • In practice: critics called it colonialism under another name; only Iraq became independent in the inter-war period (1932).

11.12 Impact of World War I [Additional - the class heading had no notes]

A short framework only; expect detail in a later class.
  • Human cost: nearly 10 million soldiers died, and as many as 13 million civilians died directly or indirectly (USHMM); millions more were wounded or displaced. The influenza pandemic of 1918-19 followed.
  • Political: the fall of four empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, Ottoman) and four dynasties; new nation-states in Central and Eastern Europe; the Russian Revolution and the first communist state; the League of Nations.
  • Economic: huge debts and inflation; Europe's decline and the rise of the USA as the world's creditor; the economic background to the Depression.
  • Social: women's work in war industries and women's suffrage (Britain 1918, Germany 1918-19, USA 1920); disillusion ("the lost generation").
  • Colonies and India: Indian hopes of self-government (Home Rule Leagues 1916; Lucknow Pact 1916; the Montagu Declaration, 20 August 1917; Government of India Act 1919) turned to anger after the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919); war-time price rises and the Khilafat issue fed Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement. Indian industry grew during the war †. (For detail, see the Modern Indian History notes.)

12. The Russian Revolutions (1905-1922)

12.0 Introduction

  • The early 20th century was a period of drastic social, political and economic transformation in the Russian Empire.
  • The Russian people revolted against the unpopular autocracy of the Tsars. After the Revolution of 1905, Russia gained an elected parliament, the State Duma.
  • Eventually the monarchy was abolished and a socialist (Soviet) republic was established, through two successive revolutions in 1917 (February and October) and the Russian Civil War (1917/18-1922).
  • The Bolshevik Revolution of 1917 and the founding of the communist Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) on 30 December 1922 inspired later communist revolutions across the world (China 1949, Vietnam, Cuba) and anti-colonial movements.
  • The rise of a powerful communist USSR under Joseph Stalin (in power from the late 1920s to 1953) paved the way for the Cold War with the capitalist West after 1945. [Note: this is a common textbook link; the Cold War's causes are debated, and Western and Soviet policies both played a part.]

[Correction] The notes say Russia "became a constitutional monarchy due to the revolution of 1905". This needs qualifying. The October Manifesto (17 October OS / 30 October NS 1905) promised civil liberties and an elected Duma, but the Fundamental Laws of April 1906 kept the Tsar's title of "autocrat", his control of ministers, the army and foreign policy, and his power to dissolve the Duma (he dissolved the first two, 1906 and 1907) †. Historians therefore call the result a semi-constitutional or limited constitutional monarchy, not a true one.

Mains Points

  • Causes of WWI as a system: long-term (imperial and economic rivalry, the naval race and militarism, nationalism, alliances, the press, no international control) + crises (Morocco, Bosnia, Balkans) + the trigger (Sarajevo) + the July Crisis, where rigid war plans and the alliance chain turned a local war general (11.2, 11.3).
  • Why the war became a stalemate: the failure of the Schlieffen Plan, the superiority of defence (machine gun, artillery, wire), and two evenly matched coalitions; hence attrition and "total war" mobilising whole economies and societies.
  • Why the Allies won: resources (empire, the USA), sea power and blockade, Germany's strategic errors (Belgium, U-boats, Zimmermann), the collapse of its allies, and home-front collapse. The "stab in the back" is a myth (11.6).
  • Versailles: terms; German objections (Diktat, Fourteen Points betrayed, war guilt); a balanced verdict: harsh but not destructive, poorly enforced, without US and Soviet support; "contained the seeds" of WWII alongside the Depression and appeasement (11.10).
  • Redrawing boundaries: self-determination applied unevenly; new states with minorities (Sudeten Germans, Hungarians); the Middle East divided into mandates (roots of the Palestine and Iraq questions).
  • League of Nations: a pioneer of collective security and international cooperation (ILO, health, mandates) but weak from birth: no USA, defeated states and USSR excluded at first, no force, unanimity.
  • India link: 1.44 million men and £146 million for the war; founder member of the League and ILO; disappointment after 1919 (Rowlatt, Jallianwala) and the Khilafat issue fed mass nationalism.
  • Russia: war defeats destroyed Tsarism; Brest-Litovsk shows the Bolsheviks' priority of survival over territory.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Franz Ferdinand was heir to the throne, not the Emperor (Franz Joseph) or a "crown prince".
  • War began 28 July 1914 (A-H v Serbia); Britain entered on 4 August. Do not give 28 June (the assassination) as the start of the war.
  • Two Treaties of London: 1839 (Belgian neutrality) and 1915 (Italy joins the Allies). There was also a Treaty of London in 1913 (First Balkan War).
  • Italy was in the Triple Alliance but fought against the Central Powers from 1915.
  • First Marne (1914) stopped the Schlieffen Plan; Second Marne (1918) stopped Germany's last offensive.
  • Casualties are not deaths: casualties include wounded, missing and prisoners.
  • Armistice is not a peace treaty: armistice 11 Nov 1918; Versailles 28 Jun 1919; in force 10 Jan 1920.
  • Versailles 1871 and 1919: German Empire proclaimed (1871); peace treaty signed (1919), in the same Hall of Mirrors.
  • Saint-Germain = Austria; Trianon = Hungary; Neuilly = Bulgaria; Sevres = Ottoman Empire; Lausanne = Turkey. The Caliphate was ended by Turkey in 1924, not by a treaty.
  • Brest-Litovsk (1918) was a separate peace between Soviet Russia and the Central Powers; it was annulled by the armistice. It is not one of the Paris treaties.
  • League membership: 42 founders; 63 ever; 58 maximum at once. Neutrals were invited; the USA never joined though Wilson proposed it.
  • Fourteen Points (January 1918) came before the end of the war, not after the armistice.
  • Old Style / New Style: the "October" Revolution was on 7 November NS; the "February" Revolution in March NS.
  • 1905 gave Russia a Duma, but the Tsar remained "autocrat" under the 1906 Fundamental Laws.

  • July Crisis: blank cheque, ultimatum, war 28 July; alliance chain; Schlieffen Plan through Belgium; Britain in on 4 August.
  • Course: blockade v U-boats (Lusitania 1915, Jutland 1916); 1st Marne and trench deadlock (Verdun, Somme 1916); Russia beaten (Tannenberg), revolutions, Brest-Litovsk 1918; USA in April 1917 (U-boats + Zimmermann); 2nd Marne and Hundred Days; armistice 11 November 1918.
  • Germany: Kiel mutiny, Kaiser out, Weimar Republic; stab-in-the-back myth.
  • Peace: Paris 1919, Big Three; five treaties (Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Trianon, Sevres/Lausanne); Versailles = territory, colonies, disarmament, Art. 231 and reparations, League; resentment, the road to 1939.
  • League (1920): collective security, mandates; USA absent; India a founder member. Russia: 1905 -> 1917 (Feb, Oct) -> civil war -> USSR 1922.

1. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 - GS Paper I (12.5 marks)

To what extent can Germany be held responsible for causing the two World Wars? Discuss critically.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme):
  • WWI, against Germany: Weltpolitik and the naval race (cause 4), the blank cheque, the Schlieffen Plan and the violation of Belgium (11.3), unrestricted U-boat war; Article 231; Fischer.
  • WWI, shared: Austria-Hungary's ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, the alliance system, French revanche, Serbian nationalism; Clark's "sleepwalkers".
  • WWII: Hitler's aims are central, but Versailles' grievances, the Depression and appeasement set the stage (11.10).
  • Conclusion: shared responsibility in 1914; primary German responsibility in 1939.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2023 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(c) (10 marks)

The Treaty of Versailles contained in itself the seeds of the Second World War. Examine.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • The terms (table, Figure 4): territory, colonies, disarmament, war guilt, reparations.
  • The seeds: Diktat and humiliation, the stab-in-the-back myth, the weakened Weimar Republic, hyperinflation, Danzig and the Corridor, Sudeten Germans, Italy's "mutilated victory".
  • Limits of the thesis: revision in the 1920s (Dawes, Locarno, entry to the League), the Depression, appeasement and Nazi ideology; MacMillan's view.
  • Conclusion: a necessary background, not a sufficient cause.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2016 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(b) (20 marks)

Do you agree with the view that the Treaty of Versailles was a bad compromise between a treaty based upon force and a treaty based on ideas?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Ideas: Wilson's Fourteen Points, self-determination, the League and mandates (11.11).
  • Force: Clemenceau's demand for security, war guilt, reparations, disarmament, no negotiation (11.8).
  • Bad compromise: too harsh to reconcile Germany, too weak to restrain it; self-determination denied to Germans; the League without the USA.
  • Balance: the constraints facing the peacemakers; later revision.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2016 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(c) (10 marks, 150 words)

"League of Nations is a League of Notions." Comment.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Ideals: collective security, disarmament, open diplomacy, mandates (11.11).
  • Weaknesses: no USA, exclusion of the defeated and the USSR, no armed force, unanimity, a "victors' club".
  • Achievements: humanitarian and technical work (refugees, health, ILO), some 1920s disputes settled †.
  • Conclusion: fair for great-power aggression in the 1930s; unfair to its social and technical work, which passed to the UN.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(d) (10 marks, 150 words)

Critically examine: "18 January, 1871 had been a day of triumph for the strength and pride of Germany and 28 June, 1919 was the day of chastisement."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • 18 January 1871: the German Empire proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors after defeating France (11-14 note).
  • 28 June 1919: Germany signed Versailles in the same hall, a deliberate reversal by France (11.10).
  • "Chastisement": territorial losses including Alsace-Lorraine, war guilt and reparations; critically, Germany stayed united and potentially strong.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - History Optional, Paper II, Q6(b) (20 marks)

Why was the first World War termed as the first 'total' war in modern history?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Mass conscript armies and global fronts (Map 1, Map 3); colonies including India mobilised (11.7).
  • Economies mobilised; civilians targeted by blockade and U-boats (11.5.1); propaganda and the press.
  • New weapons; attrition (Verdun, Somme); political results (four empires fell).

7. UPSC CSE Mains 2026 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(c) (10 marks)

"The League of Nations failed to achieve its objectives of maintaining international peace and security." Analyse the major factors responsible for its failure during the period 1919-1946.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Birth defects: tied to the peace treaties; US absence; exclusion of Germany and the USSR at first; unanimity; no armed force (11.11).
  • 1930s failures (to be covered later): Manchuria 1931, Abyssinia 1935, German rearmament; withdrawals of Japan, Germany, Italy.
  • Conclusion: great-power self-interest, not the idea of collective security, caused the failure; lessons built into the UN.

8. UPSC CSE Mains 2011 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(b) (20 marks, 200 words)

Critically evaluate: "The peace of Versailles lacked moral validity from the start."

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • For: a dictated peace with no negotiation; the blockade kept on to force signature; war guilt imposed; Fourteen Points selectively applied; self-determination denied to Germans; secret treaties (London 1915) honoured in part (11.10).
  • Against: Germany had imposed Brest-Litovsk (11.5.6); the Allies had suffered invasion; the League and mandates had idealistic aims.
  • Conclusion: legally valid but politically fragile, since the defeated never accepted it.

Corrections Made

  1. c01 - "23% : 0.6" read as shares of world population: British Empire about 23% (412 million, 1913); German colonies well under 1%.
  2. c02 - Germany never matched Britain at sea; the Dreadnought (1906) renewed the race; 29 v 17 dreadnoughts in 1914.
  3. c03 - Franz Ferdinand was heir presumptive (not "crown prince"), wife Sophie; Serbian government complicity was not proved.
  4. c04 - War declared 28 July; Belgrade bombarded 29 July; the blank cheque came early in July.
  5. c05 - Treaty of London (1915) promised specific lands (Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, Istria, part of Dalmatia...), not "southern Europe".
  6. c06 - Germany proposed an alliance to Mexico (Zimmermann Telegram); Mexico did not provoke the USA.
  7. c07 - Germany limited U-boat attacks (Arabic 1915, Sussex 1916 pledges) and resumed unrestricted warfare on 1 Feb 1917.
  8. c08 - The Aisne followed the Marne and was not a German victory; "1.4 million killed" is unverified and losses are casualties.
  9. c09 - After the First Marne the Germans fell back to the Aisne, not to the French-Belgian border.
  10. c10 - Verdun (~700,000) and the Somme (over 1 million) figures are casualties, not deaths.
  11. c11 - Russia's "5 million" were casualties including prisoners; total military deaths about 1.7-2 million.
  12. c12 - Most of the ~$10 billion US government loans came after April 1917; earlier credit was private.
  13. c13 - The Second Marne was mainly a French victory with US support, not won by the USA alone.
  14. c14 - Fourteen Points: 8 January 1918, to Congress, before the Spring Offensive; 1918 surrenders were armistices.
  15. c15 - Max von Baden announced the abdication without consent (9 Nov); Kaiser signed 28 Nov; armistice, not surrender.
  16. c16 - The Weimar Republic lasted to 1933, not 1934.
  17. c17 - Animals sent overseas: about 1.84 lakh, not 1.8 million.
  18. c18 - Saint-Germain was with Austria; the Ottoman treaty was Sevres/Lausanne; the Caliphate was abolished by Turkey on 3 March 1924.
  19. c19 - League: 63 members in all but at most 58 at once; neutrals were invited; the defeated were admitted later.
  20. c20 - 1905 produced a Duma but only a semi-constitutional monarchy (Fundamental Laws, 1906).
