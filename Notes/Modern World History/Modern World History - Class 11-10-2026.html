7. The American Revolution (1763-1775-1783)

7.0 What it was - overview

  • What: a long political, economic, ideological and military conflict between Great Britain and its 13 colonies on the Atlantic coast of North America.
  • Trigger: after 1763 the British government taxed the colonies without colonial representation in Parliament. This set off a cycle of colonial defiance and punitive British legislation (Figure 1).
  • Outcome: the colonists revolted against the British Crown, won the War of Independence and founded the United States of America - independence declared on 4 July 1776 and recognised by Britain in the Treaty of Paris, 3 September 1783 (c01).
  • Why it mattered: the USA was a republic based on popular sovereignty in an age dominated by monarchies - a challenge to the world order. It was a major inspiration for the French Revolution (1789).

[Correction] The rough notes say the colonists "established USA in 1783". Independence was declared on 4 July 1776 (Declaration of Independence); the Treaty of Paris of 3 September 1783 ended the war and gave British recognition of that independence (Office of the Historian). The present federal Constitution came later (drafted 1787, in force 1789 †).

Nuance [Additional]: republics already existed (the Dutch Republic, Venice, the Swiss cantons), so the USA was not the first republic. Its novelty was a large republic founded on a written declaration of natural rights and popular sovereignty, created by revolt against a monarchy.

7.1 Prelude - the Age of Discovery and the colonisation of the Americas

  • Christopher Columbus (Genoese, sailing for Spain) reached the Caribbean in 1492: first landfall on 12 October 1492 at an island the Lucayans called Guanahani, which he renamed San Salvador (Bahamas). Which modern island it was is still debated. He believed he had reached the Indies, so he called the native peoples "Indians"; later European usage added "Red Indians".
  • [Additional] "Red Indian" is today regarded as derogatory; use Native Americans, American Indians or Indigenous peoples (in Canada, First Nations) in answers.
  • Amerigo Vespucci (Florentine) made voyages to the New World and described them in letters; the pamphlet Mundus Novus ("New World", c. 1503) popularised the idea of a "New World". The cartographer Martin Waldseemuller named the land "America" after him on his 1507 world map (Library of Congress) (c02).

[Correction] The rough notes say Vespucci "visited frequently (1497-1504)" and published Mundus Novus in 1503. Britannica accepts as authentic only the voyages of 1499-1500 (under Alonso de Ojeda, for Spain) and 1501-02 (for Portugal); the claimed 1497 voyage is doubted and unresolved, and a 1503-04 voyage is also disputed †. Mundus Novus was printed in Latin at Paris c. 1503, with Augsburg and Rome editions in 1504; "c. 1503" is the safe date. Scholars also debate whether Vespucci himself wrote it in its printed form †. The name "America" comes from Waldseemuller's 1507 map, which drew on Vespucci's 1501-02 voyage.

Plantations, mines and the search for labour

  • European settlers set up plantations (sugar, tobacco, later cotton) and mines (silver in Spanish America †). At first they enslaved the natives and forced them to work.
  • This failed because:
    1. natives knew the local terrain and could escape easily;
    2. natives had no immunity to diseases the Europeans brought (smallpox, measles, influenza †) because they had never been exposed to them. Mortality was catastrophic - figures of up to 90% are often quoted for many regions [Verification Required]: estimates of the pre-contact population and of the decline vary widely between scholars, and disease, war, enslavement and famine acted together.
  • Planters therefore preferred enslaved Black Africans, who:
    1. did not know the Americas, so found it harder to escape;
    2. were visibly distinct (skin colour), so runaways were easily identified;
    3. had more resistance to Old World diseases, through long contact with Europeans and Eurasian diseases.

7.2 The Thirteen British Colonies in North America

  • James I (reigned 1603-1625) chartered the Virginia Company on 10 April 1606 [Additional]. Its settlers founded Jamestown on 14 May 1607 (they moored on the night of 13 May and landed on 14 May) - the first permanent English settlement in North America (Britannica).
  • Economic activity (tobacco from c. 1612 †) grew around it, and it became the first British colony, Virginia.
  • Over about 125 years, 13 colonies were set up along the Atlantic coast. The 13th was Georgia: chartered 1732 (the Avalon text of the charter is dated 9 June 1732; other sources give other days of 1732), with Savannah settled on 12 February 1733.
  • Immigration: in c. 1600-1680, about 90% of immigrants were from Britain [Verification Required] (class figure; no source opened). After the 1680s a mixed European population arrived (Germans, Scots-Irish, Dutch, French Huguenots, Swedes †) of different races, religions, professions and nationalities, so colonial society became cosmopolitan.
  • Dutch commercial dominance: until the early 1650s the Dutch dominated the carrying trade with North America, helped by their Caribbean bases. England's first Navigation Act (1651) was aimed at them (7.3).

7.4 Policy change, 1721 - "Salutary Neglect"

  • What: an informal, unwritten policy, also called "beneficial neglect", associated with Robert Walpole, Britain's first prime minister (chief minister 1721-1742). It had two parts:
    1. lax enforcement of the trade regulations;
    2. loose imperial control over colonial administration.
  • Name [Additional]: the phrase comes later, from Edmund Burke's speech on conciliation with America (22 March 1775), praising Britain's "wise and salutary neglect" (Britannica).

Reasons for salutary neglect:

  1. Enforcing every law was costly and impractical given the distance from Britain.
  2. The navy was better used to make Britain a global power than to police colonial ports.
  3. Leniency would keep the colonists loyal.
  4. Prosperous colonies would be a large market for British goods.

Implication: colonists enjoyed de facto freedom of trade (including smuggling) and political autonomy.

7.5 Conditions in the colonies till the mid-18th century

  1. Political autonomy - a result of salutary neglect; assemblies grew used to governing themselves.
  2. Prosperity - from legal trade with Britain and from smuggling under lax enforcement (c06).
  3. Urbanisation - towns such as Boston, New York, Philadelphia (Pennsylvania; centrally located, well connected, modern infrastructure - the largest colonial city by the 1770s †), Charles Town (Charleston) and Savannah (Map 2).
  4. Education - colleges spread modern education and rationalism (table below).
  5. Press - newspapers spread ideas and shaped colonial opinion against Britain.
  6. Learned societies - Benjamin Franklin founded the American Philosophical Society at Philadelphia in 1743, which became a centre of science and knowledge (the oldest learned society in the USA).

7.6 Causes of the American Revolution

The causes run from the long-term (mercantilism, autonomy, Enlightenment, the 4 Ds above) to the immediate (the British reaction to the Boston Tea Party). The turning point is 1763.

7.6.1 The French and Indian War (1754-1763)

  • Where: the Ohio River valley ("Ohio Country"), west of the Appalachians, claimed both by New France (it linked Canada with Louisiana) and by British colonies such as Virginia (c08).
  • Who: France and its Native American allies attacked British colonial frontiers - hence the name "French and Indian War". The British also had Native allies, notably among the Iroquois (Mohawk) †, and won over some Ohio valley tribes.
  • Start [Additional]: the French built Fort Duquesne at the Forks of the Ohio (modern Pittsburgh) in 1754; a young George Washington clashed with them and surrendered Fort Necessity on 3 July 1754 †.
  • Albany Congress (19 June - 10 July 1754), Albany, New York: delegates of seven colonies † met to strengthen ties with the Iroquois and plan common defence. Benjamin Franklin proposed the Albany Plan of Union - a President-General appointed by the Crown and a Grand Council chosen by colonial assemblies (Office of the Historian). Neither the colonial assemblies nor the Crown accepted it (c09).
  • Franklin's cartoon "Join, or Die" (a snake cut into pieces, each a colony) appeared in the Pennsylvania Gazette on 9 May 1754 (Library of Congress) - before the Congress met. It is often called the first American political cartoon † and was revived during the Stamp Act crisis.

[Correction] The rough notes say the River Ohio "separated these two colonies" (French and British). The Ohio was not a boundary: the whole Ohio valley was contested. French claims ran from the St Lawrence through the Great Lakes and down the Ohio and Mississippi to Louisiana, while British colonies lay east of the Appalachians and claimed lands westward. The valley itself was home to Native nations (Shawnee, Delaware, Mingo †).

[Correction] The class calls Franklin's proposal "a plan for American Union". Its name is the Albany Plan of Union (1754); it was rejected by the colonies (unwilling to give up power) and never approved in London, but it is remembered as the first serious proposal for colonial union - a forerunner of the Continental Congress and the Articles of Confederation †.

7.6.2 The Seven Years' War (1756-1763) - the first global war

  • In Europe a major war broke out in 1756. At its core was Austria against Prussia over Silesia, but it became a war of two coalitions (c10):
    • Britain, Prussia and Hanover (Portugal joined in 1762);
    • France, Austria, Russia (to 1762), Sweden and Saxony, with Spain joining France in 1762 (Britannica).
  • It was fought in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India and the Philippines - so it is often called the first global conflict (Churchill called it the first "world war" †). The French and Indian War is its North American theatre, which began two years earlier.
  • Names: "Seven Years' War" (European usage, 1756-63); "French and Indian War" (US usage, 1754-63); the Third Carnatic War (1756/57-1763) is its Indian counterpart.
  • End: Treaty of Paris, 10 February 1763 (Britain, France, Spain; Portugal acceded) and Treaty of Hubertusburg, 15 February 1763 (Prussia, Austria, Saxony).

[Correction] The rough notes say the war was "Austria vs Prussia" and that "British and Portuguese won". Austria-Prussia was only one axis. The winners were Britain and Prussia (with Hanover and Portugal): Britain gained the most overseas, while Prussia kept Silesia at Hubertusburg. France was the main loser overseas (Britannica).

7.6.5 George Grenville's measures (PM 1763-1765)

  • Sugar Act, 5 April 1764 [Additional]: cut the molasses duty from 6d to 3d per gallon but enforced it strictly; with the Currency Act (1764), which forbade colonies to issue paper money (Office of the Historian).
  • Quartering Act, 1765: colonies were to provide quarters (shelter) for British troops - in barracks, inns or empty buildings † - and supply drink, fuel, bedding and candles †, and transport, at colonial expense.
  • Stamp Act, passed 22 March 1765, in force 1 November 1765: the first direct (internal) tax by Parliament on the colonists, to raise revenue for the cost of the troops defending the colonies. Newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, licences and playing cards had to be printed on stamped paper bought from British distributors (NPS).

Colonial response:

  • Stamp Act Congress, New York, 7-25 October 1765 - delegates of 9 colonies (Office of the Historian) questioned taxation without representation and adopted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances †.
  • Young activists formed revolutionary groups - the Sons of Liberty (summer 1765, Britannica) and the Daughters of Liberty (spinning homespun cloth for boycotts †) - which questioned Parliament's authority to tax the colonies.
  • Protests turned into riots; stamp distributors were forced to resign, and a boycott (non-importation) of British goods hurt British merchants †. The Stamp Act failed.

7.6.6 Marquess of Rockingham (PM July 1765 - July 1766)

  1. Repealed the Stamp Act on 18 March 1766.
  2. On the same day passed the Declaratory Act: Parliament had full power to make laws binding the colonies "in all cases whatsoever" (Avalon Project).
  3. The colonies did not protest, because no new tax came with it - they celebrated the repeal and largely ignored the claim.

7.6.7 Pitt the Elder's ministry and the Townshend Acts (1767)

  • New York Restraining (Suspending) Act, 1767: suspended the New York Assembly for not complying with the Quartering Act of 1765.
  • Revenue Act, 1767 - the core of the Townshend Acts (four acts, 15 June - 2 July 1767): passed to make up for the failure of the Stamp Act. It imposed import duties on paper, paint, lead, glass and tea, collected at colonial ports (an "external" tax) and used partly to pay governors and judges, making them independent of the assemblies † (Britannica).

[Correction] The rough notes say the next PM was "William Pitt" and attribute the 1767 Acts to him. The ministry (1766-1768) was nominally led by William Pitt the Elder (by then Earl of Chatham), but he was largely absent through illness †; the acts were framed by his Chancellor of the Exchequer, Charles Townshend - hence "Townshend Acts" (Britannica). The Duke of Grafton led the government from 1768 to January 1770 †.

Colonial response (1767-1770): renewed protests and boycotts for three years, voiced in John Dickinson's Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania (1767-68) †.

7.6.8 Boston Massacre (5 March 1770) and Lord North

  • Where/when: Boston, Massachusetts, 5 March 1770. British soldiers, harassed by a crowd, fired into it: three died at once, including Crispus Attucks, and two more died later - five dead (Britannica) (c12).
  • Trial: defended by John Adams, Captain Thomas Preston was acquitted, as were all but two soldiers, who were convicted of manslaughter.
  • Repeal: on the same day (5 March 1770) the new PM, Lord North (PM 1770-1782), moved repeal of the Townshend duties; repeal received royal assent on 12 April 1770. All were cancelled except the duty on tea, kept to assert Parliament's right to tax.

[Correction] The rough notes say the British "accidentally killed 5 colonists". The soldiers deliberately fired into a hostile crowd; whether Preston ordered the firing was disputed and he was acquitted, so the event is best described as a confrontation in which soldiers fired without a proven order, not an accident. The name "Massacre" is the patriots' term, spread by Paul Revere's engraving †; British accounts called it an incident or riot †.

7.6.9 The Tea Act (10 May 1773)

  • Why: to rescue the financially troubled British East India Company, which held a large surplus of tea in its London warehouses (about 17 million lb †).
  • What (Britannica):
    1. the EIC could ship tea directly to the colonies, without paying British duties on re-export (a drawback, or refund);
    2. it gained a monopoly of tea exported to the colonies - carried in ships it licensed and sold only through its own appointed agents (consignees), cutting out colonial merchants;
    3. the Townshend duty of 3d per pound was kept and still paid in the colonies.
  • Effect: EIC tea became cheaper than smuggled Dutch tea, and the EIC gained a monopoly.
  • Colonists saw the act as a trap: buying cheap taxed tea would mean accepting Parliament's right to tax, and a monopoly could be extended to other goods. Despite protests the EIC's tea ships reached Boston.

[Correction] The rough notes say the Townshend duty on tea was "decreased by 50%" and that the EIC could export "duty-free" tea to the colonies. The 3d Townshend duty was not reduced - it was retained (Britannica). What the Tea Act removed was the British duty on tea re-exported from Britain (through a full drawback), which let the EIC pay the colonial duty and still undersell smugglers. "Duty-free" applies only to the British leg.

India link [Additional]: the EIC's crisis followed the Bengal famine of 1770 and falling revenues †; in the same year Parliament passed the Regulating Act, 1773 to control the Company in India † (details: Indian Modern History notes).

7.6.10 Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773)

  • Who: American patriots of the Sons of Liberty, disguised as Mohawk Indians, after a mass meeting at which Samuel Adams was a leading organiser (c14).
  • What/where: they boarded three ships - the Dartmouth, Eleanor and Beaver - at Griffin's Wharf, Boston, and threw 342 chests of tea into the harbour (Britannica).
  • Similar actions followed elsewhere (c15). The Boston Tea Party became a direct challenge to British authority.

[Correction] The rough notes say the raiders were "disguised as portus" and were "led by Samuel Adams". They were disguised as Mohawk Indians. Samuel Adams organised and spoke at the protest meetings (at the Old South Meeting House †), but whether he took part in the raid itself is not established †; say "organised by Sons of Liberty leaders such as Samuel Adams".

[Correction] The rough notes say the Tea Party inspired similar episodes in "Maryland, South Carolina and Georgia". Confirmed episodes: Charleston, South Carolina (tea seized and stored, December 1773), New York (22 April 1774), Annapolis, Maryland (the Peggy Stewart burned, 19 October 1774, NPS) and Greenwich, New Jersey (tea burned, 22 December 1774). Philadelphia turned its tea ship back †. No tea protest in Georgia was confirmed [Verification Required].

7.6.11 The Coercive ("Intolerable") Acts, 1774

Parliament's aim was to punish Massachusetts and isolate it, frightening the other colonies into obedience.

The Coercive Acts of 1774 and related measures

  • Result: instead of dividing the colonists, the Intolerable Acts united them. Colonies sent food to Boston, and the First Continental Congress met at Philadelphia, 5 September - 26 October 1774 (12 colonies; Georgia absent †), adopting a boycott of British goods (the Continental Association) †.

[Correction] The rough notes say "criminal cases in the colonies were transferred to Britain". The Administration of Justice Act did not transfer all criminal cases: it allowed the governor to move the trial of royal officials (including soldiers) accused of capital crimes in enforcing the law to another colony or to Britain, if he thought a fair trial impossible locally. Note also that the notes list four measures with one unnumbered; the standard list of four Coercive Acts is the table above.

Date caution: some web pages give the Boston Port Act's royal assent as 20 March or 20 May 1774; the standard date is 31 March 1774 †, with the port closed from 1 June.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Columbus: first landfall 12 Oct 1492, Guanahani/San Salvador (Bahamas). Tordesillas 7 Jun 1494. Cabral - Brazil 22 Apr 1500.
  • Vespucci: authentic voyages 1499-1500 and 1501-02; Mundus Novus c. 1503; "America" on Waldseemuller's 1507 map.
  • Trans-Atlantic slave trade: about 12.5 million embarked, 1501-1866 (SlaveVoyages); the passage to the Americas = Middle Passage.
  • Jamestown 14 May 1607 (James I) - Virginia, first colony; Georgia 1732 - 13th colony.
  • Blocks: New England (NH, MA, RI, CT); Middle (NY, NJ, PA, DE); Southern (MD, VA, NC, SC, GA).
  • Corporate (charter) colonies with elected governors: Rhode Island, Connecticut. Proprietary: Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland.
  • Navigation Acts 1651-1696 (enumerated goods 1660; Staple Act 1663); Board of Trade 1696; Wool Act 1699; Hat Act 1732; Molasses Act 1733; Iron Act 1750.
  • Salutary neglect - Walpole (1721-42); term from Edmund Burke (1775).
  • Harvard 1636; Yale 1701; Princeton 1746; Brown 1764; Boston News-Letter 1704; American Philosophical Society 1743 (Franklin, Philadelphia).
  • Albany Congress 1754 - Albany Plan of Union (Franklin), rejected; "Join, or Die" 9 May 1754.
  • Seven Years' War 1756-63; Treaty of Paris 10 Feb 1763; Hubertusburg 15 Feb 1763; Plassey 23 Jun 1757; Wandiwash 22 Jan 1760.
  • Proclamation 7 Oct 1763; Sugar Act 5 Apr 1764; Stamp Act 22 Mar 1765 (in force 1 Nov); Stamp Act Congress Oct 1765 (New York, 9 colonies); repeal + Declaratory Act 18 Mar 1766.
  • Townshend Acts 1767 - duties on paper, paint, lead, glass, tea; Boston Massacre 5 Mar 1770 (5 dead; Crispus Attucks).
  • Tea Act 10 May 1773 (North; EIC monopoly; 3d duty kept); Boston Tea Party 16 Dec 1773 (342 chests, disguised as Mohawks).
  • Coercive Acts 1774 (Port, Government, Justice, Quartering) + Quebec Act; Gage governor; First Continental Congress 5 Sep - 26 Oct 1774, Philadelphia.
  • Lexington and Concord 19 Apr 1775; Declaration 4 Jul 1776; Treaty of Paris 3 Sep 1783.

Mains Points

  • Economic causes: mercantilism (Navigation Acts, Wool/Hat/Iron Acts) cast colonies as suppliers and markets; colonial capitalism - merchants, shipbuilders, planters, land speculators - outgrew it. The 1763 shift to enforcement and taxation hit their interests (useful for the 2013 GS-I and 2012 Optional questions).
  • Political causes: "no taxation without representation", a century of self-government under salutary neglect, parliamentary sovereignty asserted by the Declaratory and Coercive Acts (7.6.4).
  • Intellectual causes: colleges, press and learned societies spread Locke's natural rights; a distinct American identity (the 4 Ds).
  • Imperial war as catalyst: the Seven Years' War removed the French threat, doubled Britain's debt and led to the Proclamation, troops and new taxes - "victory produced the revolution".
  • British statesmanship: shifting ministries and a cycle of tax-repeal-reassertion (Figure 1) turned protest into union (2022 Optional theme).
  • Global and India link: the same war and the same 1763 treaty decided Canada and India; the EIC's tea surplus linked the Bengal crisis to the Boston Tea Party.
  • Interpretations: economic interpretations (Charles Beard †; "capitalism vs mercantilism") vs constitutional/ideological ones (Bernard Bailyn †, rights of Englishmen and republican ideology). Present both; neither is settled.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Two Treaties of Paris: 1763 ended the Seven Years' War; 1783 ended the American War of Independence. (A third, 1856, ended the Crimean War †.)
  • French and Indian War (1754-63) vs Seven Years' War (1756-63): same conflict, different theatres and start dates.
  • 1776 vs 1783: independence declared vs independence recognised.
  • Tea Act did not create a new tax - it kept the 1767 Townshend duty and made tea cheaper.
  • Stamp Act = direct/internal tax; Townshend = external import duties; Sugar Act = lowered duty, stricter enforcement.
  • Declaratory Act (1766) vs Declaration of Independence (1776).
  • Boston Massacre (1770) and Boston Tea Party (1773) - both in Boston; three years apart.
  • Quartering Acts: one in 1765 and another in 1774.
  • Quebec Act was not one of the four Coercive Acts, though colonists counted it as "Intolerable".
  • Disguise: Mohawk, not "portus" or other tribes.
  • Albany Plan was rejected - do not say it created a union.
  • Vespucci named the "New World"; he did not discover America before Columbus.
  • Proprietary vs royal vs corporate colonies - governors were not all Crown appointees.
  • Cornwallis, who surrendered at Yorktown (1781 †), later became Governor-General of India (1786 †) - a frequent India-link question for the next class.

30-Second Revision

Columbus (1492) and Vespucci opened the "New World"; Spain, Portugal, France, Britain and the Dutch colonised it, and natives were replaced by 12.5 million enslaved Africans in the triangular trade. Britain's 13 colonies (Jamestown 1607 to Georgia 1732; New England, Middle, Southern) lived under mercantilism but, thanks to salutary neglect (1721-63), grew autonomous, prosperous, educated and "American" (4 Ds). The Seven Years' War (1756-63; Plassey and Wandiwash in India) ended French power but doubled Britain's debt. From 1763 Britain enforced and taxed: Proclamation, Sugar, Stamp (1765), Declaratory (1766), Townshend (1767) Acts → Boston Massacre (1770) → Tea Act and Tea Party (1773) → Coercive Acts (1774) → First Continental Congress → war (1775) → independence (1776, recognised 1783).
