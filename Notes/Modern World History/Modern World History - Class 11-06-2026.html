1. 7.B Old Imperialism and 7.C Colonialism

1.1 Old Imperialism (15th-18th centuries)

  • Meaning: the overseas expansion of European dominance between the 15th and 18th centuries to secure trade, acquire wealth and spread Christianity.
  • Generally summed up in three words: God, Gold and Glory; the goal was empire building.
    • God - missionary zeal (Catholic Spain and Portugal; later Protestant powers).
    • Gold - bullion, spices, sugar, trade monopolies: the logic of mercantilism (7.A).
    • Glory - prestige of the crown and rivalry between monarchies.
  • [Additional] Main actors in order of rise: Portugal and Spain (15th-16th c.), then the Dutch, English and French (17th-18th c.), working largely through chartered joint-stock companies (English East India Company 1600, Dutch VOC 1602 †).

[Additional] Usage note. The slogan "God, gold and glory" is used in revision guides mainly for the old (Age of Discovery) imperialism, but some historians (for example, a Yale lecture series on European civilisation crediting Baumgart) apply it to the New Imperialism of 1870-1914 as well. Use it for both, but explain the motives of each period.

1.2 Colonialism

  • Meaning: the direct occupation and administration of foreign territories by an outside power, the primary purpose being to exploit the resources and trade of those territories for the economic benefit of the colonising country.

[Correction] The rough notes say colonialism was carried out "by British between 15th to 18th centuries". Colonialism was practised by many European powers - Portugal and Spain first (Brazil, Mexico, Peru, Goa), then the Dutch (Batavia (Jakarta), Ceylon (Sri Lanka)), the French and the British - and it did not end in the 18th century: colonial rule over most of Asia and Africa lasted until decolonisation after 1945. Britain became the largest colonial power only from the later 18th century (after the Seven Years' War, 1756-63 †, and Plassey, 1757).

2. Development 8: The Enlightenment (Age of Reason)

  • Also called the Age of Reason; the rough notes date it 17th to 19th centuries (see c02).
  • It was made possible by a series of thinkers and intellectuals who tried to explain the world and humanity through reason and scientific methods, and thus popularised the ideals of the Enlightenment.
  • The ideals of the Enlightenment propelled Europe, and later the world, into modernity.

[Correction] Dating. Britannica calls the Enlightenment a European intellectual movement of the 17th and 18th centuries; its high point is the 18th century (roughly from Locke and Newton in the 1680s to the French Revolution of 1789). Its influence continued into the 19th century (liberalism, constitutions, reform), which is probably what "to 19th century" in the notes means. Write: late 17th to late 18th century; influence into the 19th.

2.4 Effects and India link [Additional]

  • Revolutions: Enlightenment ideas fed the American Revolution (Declaration of Independence, 4 July 1776 †) and the French Revolution (1789: liberty, equality, fraternity) - the subject of later classes.
  • India link: Western liberal and rational ideas reached Indian reformers through English education - for example, Raja Ram Mohan Roy †. 3. Development 9: The Agricultural Revolution (Britain)

  • Started in the early 18th century in Britain (see c05): the modernisation of British agriculture.
  • It happened because of many factors; two were stressed in class:

[Correction] "Started in early 18th century" is one convention, not a settled date. Historians differ: some trace the key changes to the 16th-17th centuries (Britannica notes the Norfolk four-course rotation was established in Norfolk before the end of the 17th century), while others place the decisive rise in output in c. 1750-1850, alongside the peak of parliamentary enclosure. Write: a gradual change in British farming from the late 17th century, gathering pace after c. 1750.

3.1 Factor 1: Reallocation of land ownership - the Enclosure Movement

  • Influential British landowners consolidated control over agricultural land and created compact agricultural estates, protected by enclosures (fences, hedges, walls).
  • [Additional] What enclosure did: it turned open fields (scattered strips farmed in common) and common land (shared grazing, woodland) into private, compact, fenced farms. Poor villagers lost customary rights to graze, collect fuel and glean.
  • [Additional] Parliamentary enclosure: from the 1750s enclosure by private Act of Parliament became the norm; between 1604 and 1914 over 5,200 enclosure Bills were enacted, covering just over a fifth of England (about 6.8 million acres) - UK Parliament. General enclosure Acts followed from 1801, and the Inclosure Act of 8 August 1845 set up permanent Enclosure Commissioners (The National Archives; legislation.gov.uk).

[Correction] The notes imply enclosure was an 18th-century event. Enclosure was a long process: there was enclosure by agreement for centuries before (notably sheep-farming enclosures in the 15th-16th centuries †); what was new after c. 1750 was enclosure by Act of Parliament on a large scale.

[Correction] The chain "peasants lost jobs → migrated to cities → labour for industry" is an interpretation, not a settled fact. Marxist and older accounts (Marx; the Hammonds, The Village Labourer, 1911 †) stressed it. J. D. 4. Development 10: Modern / Industrial Capitalism (Industrialisation)

  • Capitalism is an economic system in which the factors of production (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) are owned or controlled by private individuals for profit.
  • Modern / industrial capitalism is capitalism centred on the industrial sector: private owners invest capital in factories and machines, hire wage labour and sell in a market for profit. It took shape from the mid-18th century.

5. The Industrial Revolution - Rise of Industrialism (c. 1760-1840)

5.1 Meaning and the name

  • The Industrial Revolution was a period of great innovations that transformed the primarily agrarian and handicraft economy of Britain into one dominated by industry and machine (mechanised) production.
  • It originated in the mid-18th century in Britain (see c09) and later spread across the world. It permanently transformed the political and socio-economic life of humanity and is one of the major turning points in human history, shaping the world we see today.
  • Conventional dates: 1760-1840 (Arnold Toynbee's span, used by Britannica); Britannica also notes that in 1760-1830 the Industrial Revolution was largely confined to Britain.

[Correction] The notes say the term was "coined by Auguste Blanqui, a French economist, and popularised by Arnold Toynbee, an English historian". (a) The French economist was Jerome-Adolphe Blanqui (1798-1854 †), who used revolution industrielle in his Histoire de l'economie politique en Europe (1837) - not his brother, the revolutionary Louis-Auguste Blanqui. (b) Blanqui did not strictly coin it: French writers used the phrase earlier (a use in Le Moniteur Universel of 17 August 1827 is recorded, and one claimed use by the diplomat Louis-Guillaume Otto in 1799 rests on weaker sources [Verification Required]). Friedrich Engels used it in The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845, in German). (c) Britannica: "although used earlier by French writers, the term Industrial Revolution was first popularized by the English economic historian Arnold Toynbee (1852-83)", whose lectures (given c. 1881) were published after his death as Lectures on the Industrial Revolution in England (1884). Do not confuse him with his nephew, the world historian Arnold J. Toynbee (1889-1975 †).

[Correction] "Originated in England": better Britain. Key centres were in Scotland (James Watt at Glasgow; Meikle's thresher) and Wales (South Wales iron) as well as England; the Kingdom of Great Britain existed from 1707 (see the England-Britain-UK note). The 2015 UPSC question says "England", so in answers write "England (Britain)".

Figure 4 - The class metaphor for the general causes.

The four general causes explained
CauseExplanation (class notes)
1. Background: demand due to world tradeThe Age of Discovery and the Commercial Revolution turned Europe's Mediterranean trade into oceanic trade and made world trade possible. This created demand for a variety of goods in large quantities, but traditional (hand, guild, domestic) methods of production were clearly inadequate.
2. Fuel: capitalismProfit-seeking capitalists took up this challenge, established control over the factors of production and began producing for a global market. To meet such vast demand they needed faster and better methods of production, logistics and communication.
3. Spark: inventions and innovationsTo improve production, capitalists funded research and development, leading to a series of inventions. Goods once painstakingly crafted by hand could now be made in factories, thanks to improvements in technology, mining, metal-making and transport. Thus the capitalist spirit incentivised invention, encouraged innovation and led to industrialisation.
4. Fire: imperialism and colonialismTwo needs: raw materials and markets. Industrialising countries needed an unlimited supply of cheap raw materials and captive markets to sustain industry and prosperity. They adopted imperial and colonial policies and created vast colonies; this fed industrialisation, and what started as a spark became a raging fire.

[Correction] Chronology of "Fire: New Imperialism". The New Imperialism (latter half of the 19th century to 1914) came after Britain's Industrial Revolution (c. 1760-1840); it was largely a result of industrialisation spreading to other countries, which then sustained it. For Britain's own take-off, the relevant empire was the older mercantile empire: Atlantic plantation colonies, the slave trade (6.3) and, after Plassey (23 June 1757), rule over Bengal. Write: old colonialism helped start the fire; new imperialism kept it burning.

[Additional] Caution on "capitalists funded R&D". Most early inventors were practical craftsmen and mechanics (Kay, Hargreaves, Arkwright, Crompton), not employees of organised research laboratories. Capitalists financed patents, partnerships and factories (for example, Matthew Boulton backed James Watt from 1775 †); organised industrial R&D belongs to the later Second Industrial Revolution (c. 1870 onwards †).

5.4 Origin: why the Industrial Revolution began in Britain

A. Political factors

  • Glorious Revolution (1688-89): England became a constitutional monarchy with the primacy of Parliament - a new political order.
    • Parliamentary government ensured political stability, the rule of law, and peace and order: conditions favourable to business and the security of property.
    • Parliament was open to commercial interests and encouraged mercantile policies, colonial expansion, foreign trade and entrepreneurship (see c11).
    • Government policies gave protection and incentives to local industries (tariffs, Navigation Acts, patents †), which encouraged industry and supported entrepreneurs.
Glorious Revolution / Bloodless Revolution (class side-note). It marked two landmark changes in England:
  1. England became a constitutional monarchy in which the powers of Parliament were supreme (Bill of Rights, 1689).
  2. Religious freedom was guaranteed for Protestants (see c12).
Details: 5 November note, section 8.3.

[Correction] "Parliament dominated by the capitalist class" overstates it. The 18th-century Parliament was dominated by the landed aristocracy and gentry, allied with merchant, financial and colonial interests (City of London, East India Company). Industrialists of the new towns (Manchester, Birmingham) won direct representation only with the Reform Act of 1832 †. What mattered was that this landed-commercial elite was itself market-minded (enclosure, canals, mines, trade).

[Correction] "Religious freedom for Protestants": the Toleration Act (24 May 1689) gave freedom of worship to Protestant Nonconformists (dissenters outside the Church of England); it did not apply to Roman Catholics or Unitarians (Britannica), and dissenters remained barred from many public offices †. Also, the revolution was "bloodless" mainly in England; there was fighting in Ireland and Scotland (1689-91 †). [Additional] Barred from office, many Nonconformists turned to business and science (Quakers in iron-making, such as the Darbys †).

B. Geographical factors

  1. Location - island geography:
    • Natural protection from invasion (no land war fought on British soil in this period).
    • Growth of the shipping industry, which gave a powerful navy and a strong merchant marine.
    • Natural harbours (London, Bristol, Liverpool, Glasgow) helped international trade and transport.
    • Climate: a cool and humid climate favoured the cotton textile industry (see c17).
  2. Natural water resources:
    • Hundreds of small rivers provided water power for early mills (for example, Arkwright's mill on the Derwent at Cromford), but water power had limits (seasonal flow, mills tied to river sites) - which made steam power attractive.
    • Rivers (with canals and coastal shipping) helped inland transport of heavy goods such as coal. [Additional] Canal building: Bridgewater Canal to Manchester, 1761 †.

[Correction / Verification Required] The damp climate argument (moist air stops cotton thread breaking during spinning, especially in Lancashire) is widely repeated but was found here only on popular history sites. A quantitative study (Crafts and Wolf, 2014, on the location of British cotton mills) found that only some such factors had explanatory power, and other work stresses cheap local coal, water power and the port of Liverpool. Treat climate as a minor, contributory factor. [Verification Required]

C. Economic factors

  1. Natural deposits of coal and iron:
    • Coal: cheaper and more abundant fuel than wood (see c13), so metal production cost less.
    • Iron: to manufacture machines and build new infrastructure and factories.
    • [Additional] Coal and iron often lay close together (Black Country, South Wales, Central Scotland - Map 2). Abraham Darby first smelted iron with coke at Coalbrookdale in 1709, freeing the iron industry from charcoal.
  2. Imperial and colonial policies: cheap raw materials and captive markets; profits from plantations and the slave trade; accumulation of wealth through exploitation, for example of Bengal (see c14). The accumulated capital was used to buy land, hire labour, build factories and fund R&D.
  3. Modern banking and finance: the Bank of England (founded 27 July 1694) and a stock market (see c15) gave a framework to fund industrial ventures. [Additional] Country banks multiplied in the 18th century †; most early factories were financed by family savings, partners and ploughed-back profits rather than shares †.
  4. Population growth: a larger domestic market, and (with the enclosure movement) a supply of cheap labour for industry (but see c07).

[Correction / Verification Required] "Coal = one-third the cost of wood": no source opened for this note gives that ratio, so it is kept as [Verification Required]. What is verified: Britannica says Darby showed coke was superior to charcoal in cost and efficiency, and making charcoal used very large amounts of wood (one estimate: about 8 tons of wood for 2 tons of charcoal, enough for 1 ton of pig iron), at a time when English woodland was shrinking. Write: coal was cheaper and more plentiful than wood and charcoal, without a fixed ratio.

[Correction] "Wealth from Bengal / slave trade financed the Industrial Revolution" is a contested interpretation, not a settled fact.
- For: Brooks Adams, The Law of Civilization and Decay (1895): "very soon after Plassey the Bengal plunder began to arrive in London, and the effect appears to have been instantaneous"; the Indian nationalist drain theory (Dadabhai Naoroji †); Eric Williams, Capitalism and Slavery (1944), on slave-trade and plantation profits.
- Against: key changes began before 1757 (Darby 1709, Newcomen 1712, Kay's flying shuttle 1733; Adams wrote that "in 1760 the flying-shuttle appeared", although Kay had patented it in 1733); colonial profits were a small share of British investment; critics of Williams include Seymour Drescher.
- Balanced line: colonial and slave-based wealth, markets and raw cotton contributed to capital, demand and the cotton industry, but were not by themselves the cause.

[Correction] "Stock market, 1770": the London Stock Exchange's own history gives 1698 (John Castaing publishes price lists at Jonathan's Coffee House), 1773 (brokers move to a building in Sweeting's Alley named "The Stock Exchange") and 1801-02 (formal membership from 1801; new building at Capel Court, 1802). There is no 1770 milestone.

D. Social factors

  • Prominent education and research: universities such as Oxford (teaching by 1096; see c16) and Cambridge (1209), and the Royal Society of London (first meeting 28 November 1660; royal charter 1662). Research and development led to technological advances.
  • Protestant dominance and work ethic: discipline, hard work, rationalism and thrift, which encouraged the capitalist spirit (Max Weber's thesis - contested; see the 5 November note, 5.2).
  • Tradition of craftsmanship: British craftsmen applied scientific progress (inventions) to solve practical problems.

[Correction] (a) Oxford 1096 is not a foundation date: the university says there is no clear date of foundation, but teaching existed in some form by 1096. (b) [Additional] Oxford and Cambridge were then mainly Anglican, classical and clerical; their direct role in industrial invention was small. More relevant were the Scottish universities (Watt worked at Glasgow University †), dissenting academies, the Royal Society and provincial societies such as the Lunar Society of Birmingham †, and the practical skill of craftsmen.

5.5 Salient features (class headings)

The class listed the four headings below; the explanations are [Additional] and may be expanded in the next class.

  1. Revolutions within a revolution - change in several linked sectors: textiles, power (steam), iron and coal, transport (canals, railways) and agriculture.
  2. It was all about cotton - cotton textiles were the leading sector: a chain of "challenge and response" inventions (a faster loom created a yarn shortage, answered by spinning machines, which in turn called for power looms).
  3. Rise of industrialism - the factory system replaced the domestic ("putting-out") system; wage labour, machines and inanimate power.
  4. A series of transformations - urbanisation, a new working class and middle class, new transport and communication, and later new ideologies (liberalism, socialism).

5.6 India link [Additional]

  • Before: Indian cotton textiles (Bengal, Gujarat, Coromandel) were world-leading; the East India Company carried them to Britain and, re-exported, to West Africa as trade goods in the slave trade (Map 3).

6. Questions raised in the class notes

6.1 What is crony capitalism?

  • Definition: an economic system in which business success depends on close relationships between business people and government officials, rather than on free competition and efficiency (Britannica's definition, seen via a secondary source - [Verification Required] against the Britannica page).
  • Features: favouritism in licences, permits, contracts and natural-resource allocation; tailored tax breaks and bailouts; "revolving door" between regulators and firms; rent-seeking (earning profit from political privilege, not production).
  • Effects: monopolies, inefficiency, corruption, inequality and loss of public trust; it distorts markets and weakens both capitalism and democracy.
  • Link to today's topics: the chartered monopolies of the mercantile age (for example, the East India Company's trade monopoly) are an early form of state-business privilege; Adam Smith (Wealth of Nations, 1776 †) attacked such monopolies.

6.2 What are socialism and communism?

  • Socialism: a doctrine and movement that holds that the means of production should be owned or regulated by society (the state, co-operatives or workers) and wealth distributed more equally. It arose in the early 19th century as a reaction to industrial capitalism - low wages, long hours, child labour and slums of the Industrial Revolution. Forms: utopian socialism (Robert Owen, Saint-Simon, Fourier †), Marxian / scientific socialism, Fabian / democratic socialism (reform through elections †).
  • Communism: Britannica calls it a political and economic doctrine that seeks to replace private property with public ownership of the major means of production, and treats it as a form of socialism - a higher and more advanced form, according to its advocates. Its classic statement is the Communist Manifesto of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels (London, February 1848): class struggle, revolution by the working class (proletariat), a dictatorship of the proletariat, and finally a classless, stateless society - "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs" (Marx, 1875 †).

6.3 The triangular (Atlantic) slave trade

  • Who: European merchants (Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, French and, in the 18th century, above all British) with African rulers and traders and American planters. When: 16th to 19th centuries; peak in the 18th century. Where: the Atlantic, between Europe, West and West-Central Africa, and the Americas.
  • The three legs (Britannica):
    1. Outward passage: manufactured goods (textiles, guns, metal goods, spirits) shipped from Europe to West Africa and exchanged for enslaved Africans.
    2. Middle Passage: enslaved Africans carried across the Atlantic in brutal conditions and sold to plantations in the Caribbean, Brazil and North America.
    3. Return passage: plantation produce - sugar, rum, tobacco, raw cotton - shipped back to Europe.
  • Scale: the SlaveVoyages database estimates 12,521,337 Africans embarked for the Americas, 1501-1866; Britannica's "10 to 12 million transported" is close because many died on the Middle Passage (embarked vs arrived figures differ).
  • Why it matters for today's class: profits, ships, insurance and port growth (Liverpool - Britannica's "Liverpool Triangle"), and the raw cotton grown by enslaved people for Lancashire mills, link the slave economy with the Industrial Revolution (the Williams debate, c14).
  • Abolition (Britain): Slave Trade Act, 25 March 1807 (trade abolished); Slavery Abolition Act, 1833 (royal assent 28 August 1833; in force 1 August 1834, with "apprenticeship" until 1838).
  • India link: Indian cotton cloth was among the goods traded for enslaved Africans; after 1834 British plantations turned to Indian indentured labour ("girmitiyas") for Mauritius, the Caribbean and later Fiji † - the origin of much of the Indian diaspora.

Mains Points

  • Why Britain first: use the four-way frame - political (1688 settlement, stable Parliament, property rights), geographical (island, harbours, rivers, coal-iron proximity), economic (capital from trade and empire, banking, agricultural surplus, home market) and social (science societies, craftsmanship, Nonconformist enterprise). End with a balanced view: no single cause; a unique combination.
  • Background-Fuel-Spark-Fire is a good memory frame for "general causes"; correct the chronology of New Imperialism (c10).
  • Agricultural Revolution → Industrial Revolution: food, population, capital, market and labour; present the enclosure-labour debate (c07).
  • Empire and industrialisation: cite Williams and the drain/plunder view, then the critics; conclude that colonies contributed markets, raw cotton and some capital (c15).
  • Enlightenment → modern state: liberty, rights, consent, separation of powers, popular sovereignty → American and French Revolutions → Indian Constitution.
  • Socialism as a response to industrial capitalism; crony capitalism as a distortion of capitalism.
  • India link: de-industrialisation of handicrafts, raw-cotton exports, indentured labour after 1834.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Colonialism was not only British, and did not end in the 18th century (c01).
  • Enlightenment = 17th-18th c., not 19th (c02).
  • Social contract: Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau - not Rousseau alone (c03).
  • Consent (not "concern") of the governed (c04).
  • Voltaire did not write "defend to the death" (2.2).
  • Jerome-Adolphe Blanqui (economist) ≠ Louis-Auguste Blanqui (revolutionary); Arnold Toynbee (1852-83) ≠ Arnold J. Toynbee (1889-1975) (c08).
  • England vs Britain as the home of the Industrial Revolution (c09).
  • New Imperialism came after Britain's first Industrial Revolution (c10).
  • Toleration Act ≠ full religious freedom: not for Catholics or Unitarians (c12).
  • Watt did not invent the steam engine (Newcomen 1712); he added the separate condenser (1765; patent 1769).
  • Flying shuttle = weaving (Kay); jenny, water frame, mule = spinning; power loom = weaving (Cartwright).
  • Cotton gin (Whitney) is American and cleans raw cotton; it is not a spinning or weaving machine.
  • Enclosure → urban labour is debated (c07); Bengal plunder → Industrial Revolution is contested (c15).
  • Socialism vs communism: communism is a revolutionary form of socialism aiming at a classless, stateless society.

30-Second Revision

Old imperialism (15th-18th c.; God, gold, glory) built trading and colonial empires for many European powers; colonialism is direct rule for exploitation. The Enlightenment (17th-18th c.) applied reason to society: Locke (liberty, consent), Montesquieu (separation of powers, 1748), Rousseau (social contract, 1762), Voltaire (toleration), Kant (Sapere aude, 1784), Sidney (republicanism), Franklin (science). The Agricultural Revolution - enclosure, seed drill, four-course rotation - raised output and freed (debated) labour. Industrial capitalism succeeded feudalism and mercantilism. The Industrial Revolution (1760-1840), a term popularised by Toynbee, grew from world-trade demand (background), capitalism (fuel), inventions (spark) and empire (fire); it began in Britain thanks to the 1688 settlement, island geography, coal and iron, capital and banks, and craft and science; its leading sector was cotton. Its dark side included the triangular slave trade (abolished 1807; slavery 1833-34); its critics produced socialism and communism.

1. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 (GS Paper 1, 12.5 marks)

Why did the industrial revolution first occur in England? Discuss the quality of life of the people there during the industrialization. How does it compare with that in India at present?

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): part 1 - the four factor groups of 5.4 (political, geographical, economic, social) plus the Agricultural Revolution (3) and capital from trade and empire, with the debates (c07, c15); part 2 - urban crowding, long hours, child labour, low wages, disease, but cheaper goods and, later, rising real wages; part 3 - compare with India today (informal sector, urban slums, labour codes, social security), noting both differences and parallels. Source: PYQ Astra: UPSC Mains GS-1 World History.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2024 (GS Paper 1, 15 marks)

How far was the Industrial Revolution in England responsible for the decline of handicrafts and cottage industries in India?

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): introduce India's pre-colonial textile leadership (5.6); show the mechanisms - machine-made cloth (inventions), British tariffs, the Company's control after Plassey and the Diwani, raw-cotton exports, railways opening the interior; then the "how far": other causes (loss of royal patronage, decline of courts, competition before 1813, regional variation) and the historiographical debate on the extent of de-industrialisation. Source: InclusiveIAS: UPSC Mains World History PYQs (its question number for 2024 is inconsistent; check the official paper).

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2013 (GS Paper 1, 10 marks)

"Latecomer" Industrial Revolution in Japan involved certain factors that were markedly different from what West had experienced. Analyze.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): set up the Western (British) model from this note - private capitalists, gradual inventions, agricultural surplus, colonies (5.3, 5.4); then contrast Japan's state-led industrialisation after the Meiji Restoration (1868 †): borrowed technology, state-built industries sold to zaibatsu, land tax as capital, military-strategic aims and later imperialism. The "spread" of the Industrial Revolution comes in a later class. Source: PYQ Astra.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2019 (GS Paper 1, 15 marks)

Explain how the foundations of the modern world were laid by the American and French Revolutions.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): the ideas came from the Enlightenment (2) - Locke's natural rights and consent, Montesquieu's separation of powers, Rousseau's popular sovereignty; show how the revolutions put them into practice (written constitutions, republics, rights declarations, nationalism, secularism, equality before law) and how they spread to later movements, including India's Constitution (2.4). Source: PYQ Astra.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2014 (GS Paper 1, 10 marks)

What were the major political, economic and social developments in the world which motivated the anti-colonial struggle in India?

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): political - Enlightenment ideals of liberty and self-government (2.3), the American and French Revolutions, nationalism; economic - recognition of colonial exploitation and the drain (c15), the Russian Revolution's critique of imperialism; social - liberal education, reform, the World Wars and the self-determination principle. This class supplies the ideological background only. Source: PYQ Astra.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2014 (GS Paper 1, 10 marks)

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): only the conceptual base is from today's class - socialism vs capitalism (table).
