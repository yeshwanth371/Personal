18. The Chinese Revolution (1911-1949) - concluded

18.15 Causes of the Chinese Revolutions (1911 and 1949) [Additional - requested in the notes]

The notes asked for the causes of the Chinese revolutions. China had two revolutions in the period of the 24 November note, and the causes must be kept apart:

  • the 1911 (Xinhai) Revolution - a political, republican revolution that ended the Qing dynasty and 2,000 years of monarchy (see 18.8);
  • the 1949 Communist Revolution - a social revolution, won by a peasant army, that created the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949 (see 18.14).

Exam point - revolution or reform? 1911 changed the form of government (monarchy to republic) but not society or land ownership; that is why it is often called an "incomplete" or "political" revolution. 1949 changed property, class and the state (land reform, one-party communist rule) - a social revolution. Interpretations differ: Western accounts stress KMT weakness and the war with Japan; official PRC accounts stress the CCP's mass line and Mao's leadership (see Table 18.5).

[Additional] Comparison for Mains: Russia 1917 was led by an urban party relying on industrial workers and soldiers in the capital; China 1949 was won by a peasant army that "surrounded the cities from the countryside". Both followed the collapse of an old dynasty (Romanov 1917, Qing 1912) and a weak intermediate regime (the Provisional Government; the KMT republic).

19. Decolonisation of Asia and Africa (1945-1999)

Outline of the chapter (as given in class): factors → survey → challenges → evaluation → impact, for the period 1945-1999. The handouts add the earlier wave in South America (1810-1828) and the Caribbean (19.9-19.10).

19.1 Meaning and scale: the dismantling of colonial empires

  • Decolonisation is the surrender of political sovereignty by colonial masters to colonial peoples - the dismantling of colonial empires.
  • The second half of the 19th century was the era of colonisation (the "New Imperialism" and the Scramble for Africa); the second half of the 20th century was the era of decolonisation.
  • It started after the Second World War: between 1945 and 1970 dozens of countries emerged from western imperial rule. [Additional] UN membership rose from 51 original members (1945) to 127 by 1970 (Figure 1); most of the new members were former colonies, which roughly bears out the class figure of "more than 70" new states - though some new members (for example Austria, Italy, Japan, admitted 1955-56) were not ex-colonies. The US Office of the Historian counts "three dozen new states in Asia and Africa" between 1945 and 1960 alone.
  • The process was complicated by the beginning of the Cold War and superpower competition (see factor 8).

[Correction] Decolonisation was not "completed" in the last decade of the 20th century. The handover of Hong Kong by Britain (1 July 1997 †) and of Macau by Portugal (20 December 1999 †) to China were transfers of sovereignty to an existing state, not independence, and they closed the European colonial presence in Asia. Decolonisation continued after them: Timor-Leste became independent in 2002 - the UN calls it "the first new sovereign State of the 21st century" - and the UN still lists 17 Non-Self-Governing Territories (for example Western Sahara, Gibraltar, the Falkland Islands (Malvinas), New Caledonia), home to fewer than 2 million people. Source: UN - The United Nations and Decolonization: About; UN - decolonisation at 70.

[Correction] The UN figure is 750 million, not "more than 740 million": "When the United Nations was established in 1945, 750 million people - almost a third of the world's population then - lived in Territories that were non-self-governing"; since then "more than 80 former colonies have gained their independence", including all 11 Trust Territories. Source: UN - About decolonization.

19.2 Factors behind decolonisation

  1. Nationalist resistance and anti-imperial struggles. During the Second World War nationalist forces in the colonies became militant, leading to anti-imperial upsurges - for example the Quit India Movement (August 1942 †). People even resorted to armed resistance when colonial powers tried to re-establish their power after the war - for example the First Indochina War (1946-1954 †) against France and the Indonesian war of independence (1945-1949) against the Dutch.
  2. Attitude of colonial soldiers. Colonial soldiers who fought in the Second World War saw the striking contrast between modernity in Europe and the primitive conditions in the colonies; this created an intense desire for independence, and many refused to serve the imperial powers. Imperial powers began to lose control of their colonial militaries - for example the Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny, Bombay, February 1946 †. [Additional] Scholarship on 1945-46 records protests by Indian seamen and ratings against the use of Indian troops to re-occupy Indonesia. Source: N. Manjrekar, "Subaltern Worldmaking after War" (Cornell University Press, 2025).
  3. Japanese conquest of South-East Asia. Japan's conquest of South-East Asia (1941-42) destroyed the notion of European invincibility, spread the sentiment that "Asia is for Asians", and encouraged anti-imperial movements leading to self-rule. [Additional] The US Office of the Historian notes that in Indonesia and French Indochina the nationalists "had been guerrillas fighting the Japanese after European surrenders, or were former members of colonial military establishments". Source: Office of the Historian - Decolonization of Asia and Africa, 1945-1960.
  4. A new world order after the Second World War. Developments such as the Atlantic Charter (14 August 1941 †), which promised "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live" †, strengthened democratic ideas and turned world opinion against colonialism, hastening the end of colonial empires. (Churchill later said the Charter did not apply to the British Empire †, which nationalists in India and Burma resented.)
  5. Solidarity of anti-imperial sentiment. The people and leaders of the Third World helped each other's struggles. Example: Indian leaders opposed sending Indian troops to South-East Asia after the war to restore colonial empires. [Additional] Nehru declared that "Indian troops must not be used for keeping down people of colonial territories in Indonesia and elsewhere"; India hosted the Asian Relations Conference (New Delhi, March-April 1947), took the Dutch "police action" in Indonesia to the UN Security Council (July 1947) and convened the New Delhi Conference on Indonesia of 19 nations (20-23 January 1949). International forums such as the Non-Aligned Movement (Belgrade, 1961 †) and the Organisation of African Unity (May 1963) condemned colonialism. Sources: Nehru Archive - Asian Conference on Indonesia, 1949; Nehru Archive - "The War in Indonesia", 28 July 1947; The Wire - India supported Indonesia's bid for independence.
  6. Weakness of the imperial powers. The economic and military weakness of the Europeans after the war, the rising cost of maintaining colonies, and the doubtful loyalty of colonial peoples and armies forced them to quit the Third World.
  7. Role of the UN. The UN Charter (1945) - Article 1(2) on "equal rights and self-determination of peoples", Chapter XI (Declaration regarding Non-Self-Governing Territories) and Chapters XII-XIII (the Trusteeship System and Council) - and the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (General Assembly resolution 1514 (XV), 1960), which proclaimed "the necessity of bringing colonialism in all its forms and manifestations to a speedy and unconditional end", encouraged decolonisation. The growing Third World membership of the UN amplified this. Source: UN - About decolonization.

    UN membership in the class notes: 1945 - 51; 1970 - 127; 1999 - 188; now - 193 (South Sudan was the 193rd, 2011). See Figure 1.

    [Correction] "1940 - 51" should read 1945 - 51: the UN came into existence in 1945 (Charter signed at San Francisco, 26 June 1945 †) with 51 original members, India among them. Source: UN - Growth in United Nations membership.

  8. The Cold War and superpower competition. American leaders believed that delaying decolonisation would give communism an opportunity in the Third World and feared a domino effect. [Additional] The Office of the Historian: the US "encouraged the European imperial powers to negotiate an early withdrawal", yet was torn because its NATO allies held colonies, and grew "increasingly concerned that ... Soviet-supported communist parties might achieve power in the new states". Source: Office of the Historian.
Second World War weakens Europe; Japan breaks the myth of invincibility → Militant nationalism, unreliable colonial armies, Atlantic Charter and UN → US pressure (fear of communism) and Third World solidarity → Wave of independence, 1945-1975

19.3 A survey of decolonisation: the imperial powers

  • The British Empire was the largest: India, Ceylon, Burma, Malaya, Cyprus, Hong Kong, the West Indies, large territories in Africa, and the mandates of Transjordan and Palestine. Class note: Britain controlled about 25% of the world. [Verification Required: the share - textbooks commonly give about a quarter of the world's land surface at the empire's inter-war peak; no exact figure was checked in this session.]
  • France was the second largest: Indochina, North and West/Equatorial Africa, territories in the West Indies, and the mandates of Syria and Lebanon - about 9% of the world's land in the class note [Verification Required: share not checked].
  • Others: Holland (Dutch East Indies, Suriname, Antilles); Spain (Spanish Morocco, Spanish Sahara, Spanish Guinea); Italy (Libya, Somalia, Eritrea); Portugal (Angola, Guinea, Mozambique, Timor, Goa, Macau); Belgium (Congo, Ruanda-Urundi); the USA (the Philippines, Puerto Rico).

Britain: ready to decolonise - and to keep influence

  • Britain got ready for decolonisation because of: economic and military weakness; having signed the Atlantic Charter; intense anti-imperial movements; a new government (Clement Attlee's Labour government, July 1945 †); and US pressure (fear of communism).
  • Exception: colonies with large white settler populations, such as Kenya and Southern Rhodesia, where Britain resisted (19.5).
  • Britain expected to continue influencing its old colonies - neo-colonialism - because the new states were politically, economically, socially and militarily weak. Methods: interference in partitions and in drafting new (pro-British) constitutions, encouraging the Westminster parliamentary model; economic aid; mediation in communal, ethnic and tribal conflicts; arms sales, military help and training; and the Commonwealth.

[Correction] "British Commonwealth, 1931" needs precision. The Statute of Westminster (1931) † gave legal form to the "British Commonwealth of Nations" of self-governing dominions (Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Irish Free State, Newfoundland). The modern, multiracial Commonwealth of Nations - the instrument of post-1945 influence - dates from the London Declaration of April 1949 †, which let republican India remain a member with the Crown as "Head of the Commonwealth".

France: resistance

  • France was against decolonisation because of national prestige, its claim to great-power status and the economic resources of the empire; it fought to hold Indochina (1946-54), Morocco, Algeria (1954-62) and even its small enclaves in India.
  • Britain "guided" its colonies towards parliamentary self-government; France, in the handout's words, "did not care for any of these".

[Correction] France was not uniformly against decolonisation. It fought long wars in Indochina and Algeria, but it granted independence to Morocco and Tunisia in 1956 and to almost all of French West and Equatorial Africa and Madagascar in 1960 largely by negotiation, keeping influence through currency, aid and defence agreements (the CFA franc zone †). Its Indian enclaves were transferred peacefully (Pondicherry de facto in 1954 †), unlike Portuguese Goa, which India took by force in December 1961 †. Source for the 1956-1960 dates: UN - Growth in membership (Morocco, Tunisia 1956; 17 states 1960).

19.4 Decolonisation of Asia

19.4.1 The Philippines (1946) - from Spain and the USA

  • A Spanish colony named after Philip II; Ferdinand Magellan reached the islands in 1521. The USA acquired it in 1898 after the Spanish-American War (Treaty of Paris, 10 December 1898). Filipino revolutionaries, who saw Spanish rule replaced by American rule, fought US forces but were suppressed.
  • The US Congress's Philippine Act of 1902 † provided limited self-government. Japan invaded during the Second World War; US forces liberated the islands in 1945.
  • Independence on 4 July 1946 by the Treaty of Manila (Treaty of General Relations), with a US-style presidential government (President Manuel Roxas).
  • Dependence on the USA continued in military, economic and political matters:
    • Trade and "parity": the Bell Trade Act (Philippine Trade Act, 30 April 1946) gave 8 years of free trade followed by declining preferences, fixed the peso to the dollar, restricted currency decisions and required "parity rights" - equal rights for Americans with Filipinos to exploit natural resources (minerals, forests) and run public utilities. The parity amendment to the Philippine Constitution was approved by plebiscite on 11 March 1947.
    • Bases: the Military Bases Agreement (1947) † gave the USA a 99-year lease on bases such as Clark Air Base and Subic Bay Naval Base to protect its strategic interests in Cold War Asia.
    • Alliance: the Mutual Defense Treaty (1951) †.
  • After independence a communist peasant rising, the Huk Rebellion, broke out; President Truman, alarmed at the spread of communism, supplied the Manila government, and it was suppressed by 1954 †.

[Correction] The Philippines was not a Spanish colony "since 1521". Magellan only made landfall at Cebu in March 1521 and was killed at Mactan; the first permanent Spanish settlement was founded by Miguel Lopez de Legazpi at Cebu in 1565, and Manila in 1571. Source: Britannica - Philippines: the Spanish period.

[Correction] The war-damage money did not come "under the Bell Act". Two US laws were approved on the same day, 30 April 1946: the Philippine Trade Act (the Bell Act - trade, currency and parity, Public Law 371) and the Philippine Rehabilitation Act (war damage and reconstruction, Public Law 370). Their politics were linked - parity was the price of rehabilitation money - which is why textbooks merge them. [Verification Required: the handout's "$800 million" for war damages - the amount was not confirmed in an official source in this session.] Sources: FRUS 1946, vol. VIII, doc. 730 (Trade Act terms); FRUS 1946, vol. VIII, doc. 430 (Rehabilitation Act); FRUS 1946, vol. VIII, doc. 683 (Treaty of General Relations, 4 July 1946).

19.4.2 India and Pakistan (1947)

  • The Indian Independence Act passed by the British Parliament (July 1947 †) gave India full independence on 15 August 1947; the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and north-east formed Pakistan (14 August 1947 †). East Pakistan became Bangladesh in 1971.
  • India's independence was the decisive precedent for the rest of Asia and Africa (Indian Modern History notes cover the details; only the world link is kept here).

19.4.3 Sri Lanka (Ceylon, 1948)

  • Independence from Britain in February 1948 (4 February †). About three-quarters of the people were Sinhalese (mainly Buddhist) and about a fifth Tamils (mainly Hindu).
  • Two Tamil groups: Sri Lankan (Jaffna) Tamils, descendants of Tamil-speakers settled for centuries in the north and east; and Indian (Up-Country, Malaiyaha) Tamils, descendants of labourers brought by the British to the tea plantations.
  • Discrimination after independence: the Ceylon Citizenship Act (1948) barred citizenship to most Indian Tamils, making hundreds of thousands stateless; Sinhala was made the sole official language; Tamils lost civil-service posts; in the 1970s "state-sponsored colonisation schemes" settled Sinhalese in Tamil areas.
  • The LTTE (Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam) waged an armed struggle for a separate Tamil state, "Eelam", in the north and east. Civil war from 1983 (after the "Black July" anti-Tamil riots) to May 2009, when government forces defeated the LTTE; atrocities were committed on both sides.

(2) Sources give the number made stateless by the 1948-49 citizenship laws variously as about 700,000 to about one million; the handout's 700,000 is the commonly cited figure. 19.4.4 Burma (Myanmar, 1948)

  • Independence from Britain in January 1948 (4 January †), outside the Commonwealth. A turbulent period followed: military regimes, democratic forces, ethnic minorities and communists fought a protracted struggle.
  • Parliamentary government until 1962, when General Ne Win seized power (2 March 1962 †): constitution suspended, press controlled, opposition banned, the country closed to the world; one-party rule of the Burma Socialist Programme Party (BSPP) aimed at a "Burmese way to socialism".
  • 1988: nationwide protests; Ne Win resigned as BSPP chairman in July 1988. On 18 September 1988 the army under Gen. Saw Maung seized power as the State Law and Order Restoration Council (SLORC); thousands of protesters were killed.
  • 27 May 1990 elections: the National League for Democracy (NLD) of Aung San Suu Kyi won 392 of 485 contested seats (about 80%), but the junta refused to hand over power. Suu Kyi won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1991. In 1997 the SLORC was renamed the State Peace and Development Council (SPDC).
  • The NLD won landslides again in 2015 and 2020; the army rejected the 2020 result and staged a coup on 1 February 2021, declaring a state of emergency. Ethnic and religious civil wars continue - the Kachin in the north and the Rohingya Muslims of Rakhine in the west.

[Correction] The SLORC did not rename the country in 1988: it seized power on 18 September 1988, and the name was changed from Burma to Myanmar in May 1989. Sources: Britannica - Myanmar since 1988; Inter-Parliamentary Union - Myanmar elections 1990.

[Correction] It was Aung San Suu Kyi, not "Aung San", who was kept under house arrest (from 20 July 1989). Aung San was her father, the nationalist leader assassinated in July 1947 †, before independence. Source: Britannica - Myanmar since 1988.

[Additional] Update to 2026: the junta held general elections in three phases (28 December 2025 - 25 January 2026), from which the NLD was excluded (it had been dissolved in 2023); the military-backed USDP won, and on 3 April 2026 parliament elected the coup leader Min Aung Hlaing as President - a nominal return to civilian government that observers consider a continuation of military rule. Sources: AP - Myanmar's parliament elects ruling general as president (3 Apr 2026); AP - military-backed party declared winner (4 Feb 2026).

19.4.5 Indonesia (1945 / 1949)

  • A Dutch colony (the Dutch East Indies) of thousands of islands. Japan conquered it in 1942; the Japanese were first welcomed as liberators, but their brutality strengthened Indonesian nationalism.
  • Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945. The Dutch tried to return (two "police actions", July 1947 and December 1948); after UN pressure and the Round Table Conference at The Hague, the Netherlands transferred sovereignty on 27 December 1949.
  • Crises of the new nation: enormous diversity (hundreds of ethnic groups and languages, all major religions) and frequent ethnic and religious clashes; a fragile economy, corruption and insecurity.
  • Sukarno called his authoritarian system "Guided Democracy" and became "president for life"; his leftward tilt and aid from the USSR and China worried the army and the Islamists.
  • After an attempted coup by an army faction in 1965, Sukarno had to transfer key powers to General Suharto, who became President formally in 1968; Sukarno was kept under house arrest until his death in 1970. Suharto's "New Order" was a repressive police state that pursued "de-Sukarnoisation".
  • The Asian financial crisis forced Suharto to resign in 1998, opening the transition to democracy; Megawati Sukarnoputri, Sukarno's daughter, became President in 2001.

[Correction] Indonesia is usually described as having about 17,000 islands, not 13,000, and over 700 languages, not 250. Island counts vary with the method: 17,508 under the 1996 law, 13,466 in the 2007-2010 national survey (which excluded features submerged at high tide), and 17,024 islands with standardised names by 2022-23. Sources: Seasia - why Indonesia's island count varies (Nov 2026); CNN Indonesia - 17,024 named islands (2023); Indonesia (Wikipedia) - "more than 700 languages".

[Correction] Two details. (1) The great bloodshed associated with Suharto was the mass killing of alleged communists in 1965-1966, after the failed coup, not deaths spread over his whole "police state"; [Verification Required: the toll - commonly given as 500,000 to 1 million - was not checked against an authoritative source]. (2) Megawati was chosen by the People's Consultative Assembly (MPR) in 2001; the first direct presidential election came only in 2004. Source: Epoch Institute - Indonesia 1945-present.

19.4.6 Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, 1953-1954)

  • A French colony occupied by Japan in the war; France's return led to the (First) Indochina War. The French were defeated (Dien Bien Phu, May 1954 †) and the Geneva Conference (1954) confirmed the independence of Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia.
  • Laos: civil war between the royalists and the communist Pathet Lao, backed by North Vietnam. In 1975 the monarchy was overthrown and the Lao People's Democratic Republic was proclaimed (December 1975 †); it signed agreements stationing Vietnamese troops and advisers and kept a one-party socialist system that continues today.
  • Cambodia: civil war between right and left; the Khmer Rouge under Pol Pot took Phnom Penh in April 1975 †, proclaimed Democratic Kampuchea and, with Chinese advisers and aid, tried to create total communism overnight: private property and households abolished, cities emptied into the villages, genocide.
  • Third Indochina War: Vietnam attacked Cambodia on 25 December 1978 and took Phnom Penh on 7 January 1979, installing a pro-Vietnamese regime. China, Pol Pot's patron, attacked Vietnam on 17 February 1979 to "teach Vietnam a lesson"; the short war changed nothing but added more death and destruction. Vietnamese forces left Cambodia only in 1989; after UN-sponsored elections in 1993 Cambodia became a constitutional monarchy.

[Correction] "In 1954 Indochina became free" compresses two steps. Laos (October 1953) and Cambodia (November 1953) † obtained independence from France before Geneva; the Geneva Accords of July 1954 ended the war, confirmed all three states and partitioned Vietnam provisionally at the 17th parallel (communist North, non-communist South), pending elections that were never held - the origin of the Vietnam War. Source for the Geneva partition: Office of the Historian (Vietnamese war against France 1945-54); dates †.

[Correction] The Sino-Vietnamese War did not last "17 days". China attacked on 17 February 1979, declared its "punitive mission" accomplished on 6 March and completed its withdrawal by 16 March 1979 - about four weeks; estimates suggest tens of thousands of military deaths on each side. Source: EBSCO Research Starters - China Invades Vietnam; Sino-Vietnamese War (Wikipedia).

19.4.7 Malaya and Malaysia (1957 / 1963)

  • A British colony conquered by Japan and re-occupied by Britain. Politically fragmented: nine Malay states under hereditary rulers (sultans) plus two British settlements, Malacca and Penang. Multi-racial: Malays and Chinese the major groups, Indians and Europeans minorities.
  • The Federation of Malaya was created in February 1948 as a British protectorate. The Malayan Emergency (1948-1960) followed: the mostly Chinese Malayan Communist Party and its army (the Malayan National Liberation Army) waged guerrilla war for a communist state; the emergency was declared in June 1948. Under the Briggs Plan (1950) Britain moved rural Chinese into guarded "New Villages" to cut the guerrillas off from food and recruits; Sir Gerald Templer added a "hearts and minds" policy and promises of independence.
  • Malaya became independent on 31 August 1957. In 1963 it joined with Singapore and British Borneo (Sarawak and Sabah) to form Malaysia (16 September 1963 †).

[Additional] UPSC asked about this case in 2017 (PYQ 5). Sources: Britannica - Malayan Emergency; National Army Museum - Malayan Emergency (over 400,000 people resettled in about 500 New Villages by the end of 1951).

19.4.8 Singapore (1965)

  • At the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula; the modern city was founded in 1819 by Stamford Raffles as a British trading post and later became a Crown Colony. Captured by Japan in the war, recaptured by Britain, then given increasing autonomy.
  • Thought too small to stand alone, it joined Malaysia in 1963. It was ethnically diverse - about three-quarters Chinese, 13% Malay, about 10% Indian †. Chinese-Malay race riots in 1964 led to its expulsion from Malaysia; it became a fully independent republic with a parliamentary government in August 1965 (9 August †).
  • The riots convinced its leaders to adopt a constitution stressing multiculturalism and multi-racialism and non-discrimination by race or religion. Under Lee Kuan Yew (Prime Minister 1959-1990) Singapore became one of the richest countries in the world.

19.4.9 East Timor (Timor-Leste, 2002)

  • The eastern half of the island of Timor, a Portuguese colony. After Portugal's 1974 revolution, Fretilin declared independence on 28 November 1975; Indonesia invaded on 7 December 1975 and in 1976 declared it its 27th province (the UN never recognised the annexation). Resistance and a savage war followed; at least 102,800 conflict-related deaths were estimated for 1974-1999.
  • In a UN-run referendum on 30 August 1999, 78.5% voted for independence; pro-Indonesian militias then attacked the population until an international force arrived. After UN administration, East Timor became independent on 20 May 2002 - the first new sovereign state of the 21st century - and joined the UN as its 191st member (27 September 2002).
  • It is the only Asian country lying wholly in the Southern Hemisphere †.

[Correction] The struggle was not led by Jose Ramos-Horta alone. Xanana Gusmao led the armed resistance (and the National Council of Timorese Resistance) and was elected the first President in April 2002; Jose Ramos-Horta was the movement's voice abroad, shared the 1996 Nobel Peace Prize with Bishop Carlos Belo, and became President in 2007. Sources: Britannica - History of East Timor; Independence Atlas - Timor-Leste.

[Additional] Current affairs: Timor-Leste became the 11th member of ASEAN on 26 October 2025 at the Kuala Lumpur summit - ASEAN's first expansion since the 1990s. Source: Reuters, 26 Oct 2025.

19.4.10 Siam (Thailand) - never colonised; and the North-South divide

  • Siam (Thailand) was the only South-East Asian country never colonised by a western power. Squeezed between French Indochina and British Burma, it kept its independence by playing the British and French against each other and by skilful diplomacy. In the Cold War it was a major US ally and a member of SEATO (1954), aimed at containing communism in Asia.
  • "North" and "South" classify countries by socio-economic and political level, not geography: the North comprises the First World and most of the Second World; the South denotes the Third World. Australia and New Zealand are "North" though in the southern hemisphere. [Additional] The divide was popularised by the Brandt Report (1980) † and its "Brandt Line".

19.5 Decolonisation of Africa

  • African nationalism intensified after the Second World War and the independence era opened in the 1950s. The first was Libya (1951), followed by Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia (1956) and Ghana (1957), the first sub-Saharan state to win independence from a European colonial power.
  • 1960 - the "Year of Africa": 17 African states became independent, most of them former French colonies. Between 1960 and 1980 almost all British and French colonies were freed; the Portuguese colonies (Guinea-Bissau, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Angola) followed in 1974-75 after the revolution in Portugal; Namibia (South West Africa, a League mandate administered by South Africa since the end of the First World War) became free in 1990. Class handout: 47 countries became independent in the three decades after 1951 [Verification Required: count not checked].
  • In the Cold War the USA backed non-communist factions and the USSR (and Cuba) backed communist ones.

[Correction] Egypt did not become independent "in 1954". Britain declared Egypt nominally independent on 28 February 1922 †, keeping control of defence and the Suez Canal; the 1952 revolution (Nasser and the Free Officers) ended the monarchy. What happened in 1954 was the Anglo-Egyptian Agreement of 19 October 1954, which replaced the 1936 treaty and provided for the withdrawal of British troops from the Suez Canal Zone (completed in June 1956 †). Source: FRUS 1952-54, vol. IX, doc. 1348 - Anglo-Egyptian Agreement.

19.5.1 Libya (1951) - the first independence through the UN

  • Under Ottoman rule from the 16th century; Italy invaded in 1911 (Italo-Turkish War) and obtained it from Turkey by treaty in 1912. After the Second World War it was under British (Tripolitania, Cyrenaica) and French (Fezzan) administration.
  • UN General Assembly resolution 289 (IV) of 21 November 1949 decided that Libya should become independent "as soon as possible and in any case not later than 1 January 1952". A constitution was adopted in 1951 and Libya became independent on 24 December 1951 as a constitutional monarchy under King Idris (1951-69). It was the first country to gain independence through the UN.
  • Muammar Gaddafi overthrew Idris in 1969 and ruled until 2011.

[Additional] The same resolution put Italian Somaliland under a ten-year UN trusteeship with Italy as administering authority (hence Somalia's independence in 1960). Sources: UNGA resolution 289 (IV), 21 Nov 1949 (Refworld); Digitheque MJP - Independence of Libya, 1951.

19.5.2 Ghana (Gold Coast, 1957)

  • The British Gold Coast; nationalists were organised by Kwame Nkrumah (Convention People's Party, 1949) on non-violent lines ("Positive Action" - strikes, boycotts, civil disobedience).
  • Independence on 6 March 1957 as Ghana (Gold Coast plus British Togoland). Nkrumah was Prime Minister, then President (1960) and president for life; he promoted education, industrialisation (the Volta River project) and health, and Pan-Africanism and non-alignment.
  • A military coup in 1966 (24 February †) overthrew him; he took refuge in Guinea.

Source: Library of Congress - Nkrumah's speech of 29 August 1957 ("On March 6, 1957, Ghana became the first country in sub-Saharan Africa to achieve independence from a European colonial power").

19.5.3 Congo (Zaire, 1960)

  • A Belgian colony (King Leopold II's personal possession until 1908). Political awareness rose sharply in the late 1950s; after the Leopoldville riots of January 1959 and mass protests under leaders such as Patrice Lumumba and Joseph Kasa-Vubu, a panicked Belgium granted independence on 30 June 1960 - rushed and unplanned.
  • Congo Crisis (1960-65): the army mutinied (5 July 1960); copper-rich Katanga seceded under Moise Tshombe on 11 July 1960 with Belgian support; the USA and USSR backed opposing sides - one of the first Cold War proxy conflicts in Africa. Lumumba (Prime Minister) and Kasa-Vubu (President) appealed to the UN, which sent a peacekeeping force (ONUC); Lumumba was overthrown, handed over to the Katangese and killed in January 1961; UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjold died in a plane crash (September 1961 †); Katanga's secession was crushed in January 1963. About 100,000 people died.
  • Mobutu took power in a second coup (November 1965), renamed the country Zaire (1971) and ruled until 1997.

[Correction] The Congo had about 200 ethnolinguistic groups at independence, not "more than 150". Source: EBSCO Research Starters - UN intervenes in the Congolese civil war ("nearly fifteen million people divided into some two hundred ethnolinguistic groups"); see also Britannica - the Congo crisis. [Additional] India link: India contributed a large contingent, including combat brigades, to ONUC †.

19.5.4 Somalia (1960) and "Greater Somalia"

  • In the Scramble for Africa the Somali people were divided among British, French and Italian territories and Ethiopia. After the war British and Italian rule ended amid Somali nationalism.
  • British Somaliland became independent on 26 June 1960; on 1 July 1960 it united with the former Italian Somaliland (a UN trust territory under Italy since 1950) to form the Somali Republic, with Aden Abdullah Osman Daar as President.
  • Greater Somalia - the idea of uniting all ethnic Somalis of the Horn of Africa (symbolised by the five-pointed star on the flag) - led to border conflicts with Ethiopia (the Ogaden; Ogaden War 1977-78), Kenya (the Northern Frontier District) and over Djibouti. Result: regional instability, militarisation, a drained economy and lasting tension with Ethiopia and Kenya.

[Correction] Two slips: the Somalis live in the Horn of Africa (East Africa), not "north Africa"; and French Somaliland did not join the Somali Republic in 1960 - it remained French (as the French Territory of the Afars and Issas) and became independent separately as Djibouti in 1977. Sources: Terralore - Chronicle of Somalia; World Empires - Somali Republic; UN membership (Djibouti, 1977).

19.5.5 Republic of South Africa (1961) and apartheid

  • In 1910 Britain created the Union of South Africa from four colonies - the Cape, Natal and the former Boer republics of Transvaal and Orange Free State - as a dominion. The Status of the Union Act (1934) † made it a sovereign state within the Empire.
  • Over 70% of the people were black Africans, yet whites dominated everything: blacks could not vote, did manual labour and lived in terrible conditions.
  • In 1948 the white-only National Party under D. F. Malan introduced apartheid ("apartness" in Afrikaans) - legal racial segregation. Discrimination existed from 1910, but the word became common only after 1948. Independence of India and transfers of power elsewhere alarmed whites, who wanted to keep supremacy by segregation.
  • Key laws: Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949); Population Registration Act (1950) (classification by race); Group Areas Act (1950) (segregated residential areas); Suppression of Communism Act (1950) (used against all protest). Separate schools, buses, trains, toilets, park benches, beaches, hospitals and even churches. Between 1960 and 1983 more than 3.5 million non-whites were forcibly relocated †.
  • South Africa became a republic on 31 May 1961, cutting its last constitutional ties with Britain, and left the Commonwealth under international pressure over apartheid.
  • Resistance: the African National Congress (founded 1912) organised defiance of apartheid laws. At Sharpeville (21 March 1960) police killed 69 protesters against the pass laws; the ANC and the Pan Africanist Congress (PAC) were banned and their leaders jailed. The Black Consciousness Movement (1960s-70s) of Steve Biko built black self-awareness. Nelson Mandela led the ANC's armed wing (Umkhonto we Sizwe, 1961) and was imprisoned.
  • International pressure: UN calls for sanctions and an arms embargo (made mandatory in 1977); the General Assembly rejected South Africa's credentials (1974); the US Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act (1986) † restricted loans and banned imports of farm goods, iron, coal, steel, uranium and textiles, pushing US firms out.
  • End of apartheid: pressure and the end of white rule in Zimbabwe (1980) forced President P. W. Botha to ease apartheid (mixed marriages permitted, pass laws abolished, mid-1980s †). President F. W. de Klerk lifted the ban on the ANC, PAC and Communist Party on 2 February 1990; Mandela was released on 11 February 1990 after 27 years. The last apartheid laws were repealed in June 1991. In the first non-racial elections (April 1994) the ANC won 62.65%; Mandela became the first black President (10 May 1994).

[Correction] Steve Biko died on 12 September 1977, not 1976, of brain injuries suffered in police custody (arrested 18 August 1977). Source: Britannica - Steve Biko; BBC On This Day - 12 September 1977.

[Correction] Mandela was arrested in 1962 (August 1962 †), not 1961; 1961 is the year Umkhonto we Sizwe was founded. He was sentenced to life imprisonment in the Rivonia Trial (1964). Source: EBSCO - UN sanctions South Africa ("Mandela was arrested in 1962").

[Correction] The ban on the ANC was lifted in February 1990, not 1989 (de Klerk became President in September 1989 and met Mandela in December 1989). The name is de Klerk. Sources: SAHO - ANC timeline 1990-1999; FW de Klerk Foundation - constitutional transformation.

[Additional] Precision on the UN and India link: In 1974 the General Assembly voted 91-22 to reject South Africa's credentials and it was kept out of the Assembly's work, but expulsion from the UN was vetoed in the Security Council by Britain, France and the USA. India first placed South Africa's racial policies on the UN agenda in 1946 (treatment of people of Indian origin). Sources: SAHO - South Africa and the UN, 1946-1990; PIIE - sanctions chronology.

19.5.6 Algeria (1962)

  • A French colony (legally part of France since 1830) with over one million European settlers, the colons (pieds-noirs). France refused independence, so nationalists formed the National Liberation Front (FLN); Ahmed Ben Bella was one of its nine "historic chiefs".
  • The war began on 1 November 1954 ("Toussaint Rouge"). The FLN used guerrilla war and terrorism against settlers and pro-French Algerians; the French army used mass torture and resettlement. Settlers demonstrated for integration with France; in April 1961 some French generals attempted a putsch †.
  • Charles de Gaulle (in power from 1958) recognised Algerians' right to self-determination in 1959. The Evian Accords (18 March 1962) brought a ceasefire; in the referendum of 1 July 1962 99.72% voted for independence, declared on 3 July (celebrated on 5 July †). Ben Bella became the first leader; he turned authoritarian and was overthrown by Houari Boumedienne in June 1965.

[Correction] (1) Ben Bella did not lead the war in the field throughout: he was a founding leader, but the French captured him in 1956 and held him until 1962 †. (2) "More than one million casualties" is one side of a disputed figure - the Algerian state cites 1.5 million dead, French historians about 400,000-500,000, and estimates range from about 350,000 to over a million; no single figure should be stated as fact. (3) De Gaulle was not simply "in favour of independence" when he took power in 1958; he moved to self-determination in 1959 and negotiated at Evian. Sources: Britannica - Algerian War; Independence Atlas - Algeria.

19.5.7 Rwanda (1962)

  • Part of German East Africa until the First World War; then a Belgian League of Nations mandate (Ruanda-Urundi) and, after 1946, a UN trust territory. Belgium ruled through the Tutsi minority (privileged, given administrative posts) over the Hutu majority, and introduced identity cards fixing "ethnicity".
  • The Hutu revolution (1959-1961) overthrew the Tutsi monarchy (republic declared January 1961); independence on 1 July 1962 under Gregoire Kayibanda, with Hutu elites in power. Tutsi persecution and refugee flight followed - the background to the 1994 genocide, in which an estimated 800,000 to one million people, overwhelmingly Tutsi, were murdered in about 100 days.

Sources: Britannica - Rwanda under German and Belgian control; UN Outreach Programme on the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi - historical background. The handout's "5 to 10 lakh Tutsis" matches these estimates.

19.5.8 Kenya (1963)

  • A British settler colony: European settlers took the best farmland (the "White Highlands") to grow coffee and tea with African labour; though never more than about 1% of the population, they held most political and economic power. Britain therefore refused independence at first.
  • Nationalists were led by Jomo Kenyatta (Kenya African Union); a militant, mainly Kikuyu movement, the Mau Mau, began a guerrilla war against settlers and loyalists. Britain declared an emergency (20 October 1952 - January 1960), detained tens of thousands in camps and forced over a million into guarded villages. Kenyatta, who had denounced Mau Mau violence, was nonetheless convicted in 1953 and imprisoned.
  • Independence on 12 December 1963; Kenyatta became Prime Minister, then President (1964). A believer in "African socialism", he set up one-party rule; a conflict with Somali separatists in the north (the "Shifta War" †) troubled the country.

[Verification Required] The handout's "more than 30,000 British settlers by the early 20th century" could not be confirmed; the settler population was far smaller before the First World War and reached tens of thousands only after the Second †. Sources for this section: Britannica - Kenya: World War II to independence; EBSCO - Mau Mau Uprising (settlers "never numbered more than 1 percent"); Independence Atlas - Kenya.

19.5.9 Angola (1975) and Portuguese Africa

  • A Portuguese colony that won independence on 11 November 1975 † after an armed struggle and Portugal's Carnation Revolution (25 April 1974 †). Portugal left without elections or a clear transfer of power, so the liberation movements fought for control.
  • Civil war (1975-2002): the communist MPLA (backed by the USSR and Cuba) against UNITA (backed by the USA and South Africa) — a Cold War proxy war. It ended after UNITA leader Jonas Savimbi was killed in 2002 † and UNITA gave up armed struggle. [Additional] A third movement, the FNLA, also fought in 1975 †.

19.5.10 Zimbabwe (Southern Rhodesia, 1980)

  • A British colony whose white settlers opposed black majority rule. Ian Smith's government issued the Unilateral Declaration of Independence (UDI) on 11 November 1965; no country recognised it and the UN imposed mandatory sanctions (1966, 1968).
  • A guerrilla war by ZANU (Robert Mugabe) and ZAPU (Joshua Nkomo) - the "Second Chimurenga" - forced talks; the Lancaster House Agreement (21 December 1979) led to elections in February 1980, in which both blacks and whites voted. ZANU-PF won and Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980.

[Correction] Mugabe was Prime Minister from 1980 and President from 1987 to 2017 (removed after an army intervention in November 2017 †); "dictator (1980-2017)" is a judgement on his increasingly authoritarian rule, not an office. Sources: Britannica - Zimbabwe: Rhodesia and the UDI; National Archives of Australia - Independence of Zimbabwe.

19.6-19.8 Challenges, evaluation and impact of decolonisation

19.6 Challenges to decolonisation

  1. Problems in creating a national identity. Imperial powers drew borders for their own convenience, forcing rival groups into one territory and dividing single ethnic or cultural groups among several. A common national identity became impossible in many places, leading to civil wars, genocides (Rwanda) and cross-border conflicts (Somalia). [Additional] The Office of the Historian: colonial rule "drew arbitrary natural boundaries where none had existed before, dividing ethnic and linguistic groups" (compare the 2013 Mains question, PYQ 1).
  2. Pressure of the Cold War. New states endured the push and pull of the USA and USSR and became victims of bipolar politics and proxy wars (Congo, Angola, Indochina).
  3. Lack of leadership. Colonial governments kept local people in the lower levels of administration, leaving most colonies with inexperienced leaders.
  4. Difficulty in establishing new governments. Colonial rule had been oppressive and exploitative; this bred a general distrust of government, political instability and authoritarian regimes.
  5. Corruption, poverty and illiteracy. Dictatorships encouraged corruption and nepotism; despite rich resources many Third World countries, especially in Africa, became "Fourth World" (least-developed) nations.
  6. Economic dependence. Colonies had been economic appendages - suppliers of raw materials and markets for finished goods. As victims of the drain of wealth they lacked capital, infrastructure, modern industry and skilled labour, so they stayed dependent on their former rulers.

19.7 Evaluation of decolonisation

  1. Attitudes of the imperial powers varied: some resisted (France in Indochina and Algeria, Portugal, settler regimes), others accepted it reluctantly (Britain, the Netherlands after 1949, Belgium).
  2. It was slow and often violent: nationalist struggles, guerrilla wars, civil wars and even genocides.
  3. The Cold War complicated it, producing proxy wars and authoritarian regimes.
  4. The time for decolonisation had come, but many new states were unprepared because of deep political, economic and social weaknesses.
  5. New states faced social unrest, military weakness, border disputes, shortage of capital, weak industry, poor education and infrastructure, and food insecurity.
  6. Desperate for money and expertise, most turned to their former rulers; Western aid often carried conditions that deepened dependence - neo-colonialism.

19.8 Impact of decolonisation

  1. Diverse ethnic and religious groups struggled to form a national identity: ethnic conflict, discrimination and instability.
  2. Indigenous languages, traditions and customs revived, but colonial influences stayed embedded in education, law and administration.
  3. The Third World emerged as a significant force in world politics (Bandung 1955 †, NAM 1961 †, the Group of 77 †).
  4. The balance of power in the UN shifted, increasing the influence of Third World countries (Figure 1).
  5. The UN's agenda widened to decolonisation itself, racism (apartheid), genocide, proxy conflicts and development.
  6. Cold War rivalry made both superpowers pour economic and technical aid into the Third World, aiding development.
  7. European powers lost global dominance - the end of the Eurocentric world order.
Colonial borders and economies → Weak new states: identity crises, dependence, inexperienced leaders → Cold War proxy wars and authoritarian rule → Neo-colonialism and aid dependence → Yet: Third World voice in the UN, NAM, end of Eurocentrism

Distinguish: colonialism = direct political rule over another people; imperialism = the wider policy of extending power and control (formal or informal); neo-colonialism = continued economic, military or cultural control of formally independent states (Nkrumah popularised the term in 1965 †). Decolonisation is the process of ending colonial rule; independence is its legal outcome for a particular state.

19.9-19.10 Decolonisation of South America and the Caribbean (handouts)

19.9 Decolonisation of South America (1810-1828)

Between about 1810 and 1828 a wave of independence movements ended about three centuries of Spanish and Portuguese rule. It was shaped by Enlightenment ideas, the example of the American and French Revolutions, and the weakening of Spain and Portugal in the Napoleonic Wars.

[Correction] The handout's title "from the 18th century" and its dates need fixing. The independence wars belong to the early 19th century (1810-1828); the late 18th century supplied only precursors (Tupac Amaru II, 1780-81) and inspirations. The American Revolution ran from 1775 to 1783 (Treaty of Paris †), not "1775-1983". Source: Preceden - Latin American independence timeline (1808-1826).

Colonial background and early resistance

  • Spain ruled most of the continent through viceroyalties (New Granada, Rio de la Plata, Peru) and a rigid mercantilist system; Portugal ruled Brazil separately.
  • A discriminatory hierarchy: peninsulares (Spain-born) above creoles (American-born whites); indigenous peoples, mestizos and African slaves at the bottom. Creole resentment grew into revolutionary sentiment.
  • Tupac Amaru II rebellion (1780-81) in Peru - an indigenous-led rising against forced labour, taxation and racial hierarchy; crushed, but a symbol of resistance. Enlightenment ideas of liberty, equality and self-government spread among creole intellectuals.

External influences

  1. American Revolution (1775-1783) - showed colonial liberation was possible.
  2. French Revolution (1789-1799) - spread Enlightenment values and popular sovereignty.
  3. Haitian Revolution (1791-1804) - inspired enslaved and oppressed peoples, especially in northern South America.
  4. The most immediate catalyst: Napoleon's invasion of Portugal and Spain (1807-1808). With the Spanish monarchy in disarray (Ferdinand VII deposed, 1808 †), creole leaders set up local juntas (Caracas, April 1810; Buenos Aires, May 1810) and revolutionary leaders rose.

Major independence movements

  1. Venezuela and the north - Simon Bolivar, "El Libertador". Venezuela declared independence on 5 July 1811 (the first in South America) and secured it at Carabobo (24 June 1821). Bolivar's victory at Boyaca (7 August 1819) freed New Granada (Colombia); his lieutenant Antonio Jose de Sucre won Pichincha (24 May 1822), freeing Ecuador. Gran Colombia (Venezuela, Colombia including Panama, Ecuador) was short-lived (1819-1830/31).
  2. Argentina and the south - Jose de San Martin. The May Revolution (25 May 1810) in Buenos Aires; formal independence at the Congress of Tucuman, 9 July 1816. San Martin led the Army of the Andes across the mountains into Chile and won at Chacabuco (12 February 1817) and Maipu (5 April 1818); Chile declared independence on 12 February 1818. He then sailed to Peru (independence proclaimed in Lima, 28 July 1821), met Bolivar at Guayaquil (July 1822) and withdrew; Sucre's victory at Ayacucho (9 December 1824) ended Spanish rule on the continent. Upper Peru became Bolivia (6 August 1825).
  3. Brazil - a peaceful, monarchical independence. Fleeing Napoleon, the Portuguese royal family moved to Brazil (arrived 1808), raising its status. On 7 September 1822 (the "Cry of Ipiranga") Dom Pedro I, son of King Joao VI, declared independence and became Emperor. Brazil remained a monarchy until the republic of 15 November 1889 †.

Factors and legacy

  • Factors: creole nationalism (exclusion from high office); economic exploitation under mercantilism; Enlightenment ideas and the example of other revolutions; European instability (Napoleonic wars) creating a power vacuum; militarised leadership (Bolivar, San Martin, Sucre, O'Higgins).
  • Aftermath: caudillismo (rule by military strongmen); weak institutions; regionalism and fragmentation (Gran Colombia broke up in 1830; Bolivar's dream of a federation failed at the Congress of Panama, 1826); continued economic dependence on Britain and later the USA - an early form of neo-colonialism.
  • Legacy: the first great wave of anti-colonial struggle; it created modern nation-states and inspired liberation movements elsewhere. [Additional] The USA's Monroe Doctrine (1823) † warned Europe against re-colonising the Americas.

19.10 Decolonisation of the Caribbean

Often overlooked, Caribbean decolonisation was largely gradual, constitutional and negotiated, shaped by plantation economies, racial hierarchies and Cold War geopolitics.

Colonial legacy

  • Colonised from the 15th century by Britain (Jamaica, Barbados, Trinidad, Bahamas ...), France (Saint-Domingue/Haiti, Guadeloupe, Martinique), Spain (Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico) and the Netherlands (Suriname, Aruba, Curacao).
  • Sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans created enduring racial and class divisions that outlived the abolition of slavery in the 19th century; nationalist movements centred on labour rights, civil equality, racial justice and economic self-determination.
  • Haiti (1791-1804) - the first successful slave revolt to create an independent state (1 January 1804); an inspiration to anti-colonial movements and a cause of panic among colonisers, who tightened control elsewhere. The rest of the Caribbean did not follow Haiti's revolutionary path; constitutional reform, labour unionism and "decolonisation from above" became the norm.

The world wars and the 1930s

  • Caribbean soldiers fought in imperial wars but returned to racism and poverty.
  • The Depression brought mass strikes and riots across the British West Indies (1934-1939), forcing Britain to appoint the Moyne Commission (1938) and to introduce labour reforms and wider political rights; labour leaders became party leaders (Bustamante and Manley in Jamaica, Grantley Adams in Barbados). After 1945 the UN's decolonisation agenda and the decline of European empires accelerated demands for autonomy.

[Correction] The major labour rebellions were in Trinidad and Barbados in 1937 but in Jamaica in 1938 (the Frome sugar-estate riots, May-June 1938), not "1937 in Jamaica, Trinidad and Barbados". Sources: British West Indian labour unrest of 1934-1939 (Wikipedia); CAPE History - British Caribbean independence and integration.

Pathways to independence

  1. British Caribbean - phased and peaceful: universal adult suffrage and internal self-government in the 1940s-50s (Jamaica 1944); the West Indies Federation (1958-1962), which failed over inter-island disputes (Jamaica voted to leave in 1961 †); then independence - Jamaica and Trinidad and Tobago (1962), Barbados (1966), Bahamas (1973), Grenada (1974), Dominica (1978), St Lucia (1979). Many kept the British monarch as head of state (Commonwealth realms); [Additional] Barbados became a republic on 30 November 2021 †.
  2. French Caribbean - integration, not independence: in 1946 Martinique, Guadeloupe and French Guiana became overseas departments of France, with French citizenship, institutions and subsidies but limited political autonomy. Haiti had been independent since 1804.
  3. Spanish Caribbean:
    • Cuba - independent in 1902 after the Spanish-American War (1898), but a US neo-colony under the Platt Amendment (1901; it gave the USA a right to intervene and a naval base at Guantanamo; repealed in 1934). The Cuban Revolution of Fidel Castro overthrew the US-backed Batista regime in 1959 and set up a communist government - a Cold War flashpoint.
    • Dominican Republic - independent from Haiti in 1844, re-annexed by Spain and free again in 1865; long periods of US occupation and dictatorship (notably Trujillo, 1930-61 †).
    • Puerto Rico - a US territory since 1898; a Commonwealth with limited self-rule (since 1952 †).
  4. Dutch Caribbean - Suriname became independent on 25 November 1975; the island colonies were reorganised within the Kingdom of the Netherlands.

[Correction] "St Lucia, Grenada, Dominica - 1970s-1980s" should read all in the 1970s: Grenada 1974, Dominica 1978, St Lucia 1979 (the 1980s cases were St Vincent and the Grenadines 1979/80, Antigua and Barbuda and Belize 1981, St Kitts and Nevis 1983). Source: UN - Growth in membership (admission years).

[Correction] Not all of the Netherlands Antilles became "autonomous countries". Post-independence challenges and resilience

  • Economic dependence on tourism, remittances and aid; brain drain and small domestic markets; vulnerability to climate change and hurricanes.
  • External interference, particularly by the USA - for example the US invasion of Grenada (October 1983) †.
  • Yet most Caribbean states built strong democratic institutions and high human development, and cooperate regionally through CARICOM (Treaty of Chaguaramas, 1973).
  • Conclusion: Caribbean decolonisation, less dramatic than elsewhere, shows gradualist, negotiated independence and the ability of small states to balance autonomy with economic and geopolitical realities.

Mains Points

  • Why decolonisation after 1945? Combine internal pressure (militant nationalism, unreliable colonial armies) with external change (Europe's exhaustion, Japan's victories, the Atlantic Charter, UN Charter and Res. 1514, US anti-colonialism shaped by Cold War fears, Third World solidarity) (19.2).
  • Britain vs France: negotiated transfer and Commonwealth influence vs wars of retention followed by rapid negotiated exits; both practised neo-colonialism (Table 19.1).
  • Settler colonies were the hardest cases - Algeria, Kenya, Rhodesia, South Africa - three-sided conflicts (Table 19.3).
  • Artificial borders explain many post-colonial conflicts - Congo/Katanga, Somalia/Ogaden, Rwanda, Nigeria (19.6; PYQ 2013).
  • Cold War overlay: proxy wars in Congo, Angola and Indochina; US bases and treaties in the Philippines; Soviet and Cuban support for the MPLA.
  • Post-colonial state-building: military rule and one-party states (Burma, Indonesia, Ghana, Kenya), ethnic majoritarianism (Sri Lanka), but also success stories (Singapore, India, the Caribbean democracies).
  • India's role: precedent of 1947; Nehru's opposition to using Indian troops in Indonesia; Asian Relations Conference 1947 and New Delhi Conference on Indonesia 1949; raising apartheid at the UN (1946); ONUC in Congo; NAM.
  • Two waves compared (Table 19.6): creole-led 19th-century independence in Latin America vs indigenous mass nationalism in 20th-century Asia and Africa.
  • Chinese revolutions: 1911 political (end of monarchy) vs 1949 social (land, class, state) - long-term and immediate causes in Table 18.6.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Hong Kong and Macau went to China, not to independence; decolonisation continued after 1999 (Timor-Leste 2002; 17 NSGTs remain) (c01).
  • Libya (1951), not Ghana, was the first post-war African independence; Ghana (1957) was the first sub-Saharan one.
  • Egypt: 1922 nominal independence; 1952 revolution; 1954 agreement on the Suez base; 1956 British exit and Suez Crisis (c16).
  • Burma vs Myanmar: SLORC seized power in 1988; renamed the country in 1989 (c09). Aung San (father) vs Aung San Suu Kyi (daughter) (c10).
  • Indonesia: proclaimed 1945, sovereignty 1949 - both dates are asked.
  • Bell Trade Act (trade, parity) vs Philippine Rehabilitation Act (war damage) - same day, different laws (c07).
  • Geneva 1954 partitioned Vietnam (17th parallel), not Korea (38th parallel, 1945/1953).
  • Sri Lankan Tamils vs Indian (Up-Country) Tamils - different histories; the 1948 Citizenship Act hit the second group.
  • Somali Republic (1960) = British + Italian Somaliland; Djibouti (French) separate, 1977 (c18).
  • Biko 1977, not 1976 (1976 was the Soweto uprising †) (c19); ANC unbanned 1990, not 1989 (c21).
  • Apartheid began in 1948, the republic in 1961, majority rule in 1994 - three different dates for South Africa.
  • Uruguay won independence from Brazil (1828), not directly from Spain (c25).
  • Brazil became independent as an empire (1822), not a republic (1889).
  • Haiti (French Saint-Domingue) - independent 1804, long before Martinique and Guadeloupe became French departments (1946).
  • Statute of Westminster (1931) vs London Declaration (1949) - old dominion Commonwealth vs modern multiracial Commonwealth (c04).
  • Colonialism vs imperialism vs neo-colonialism - see 19.8.

30-Second Revision

China had two revolutions: 1911 ended the monarchy (Qing decay, foreign humiliation, Wuchang trigger); 1949 was a peasant-based social revolution that the CCP won because the KMT was corrupt, war-weakened and indifferent to land reform. After 1945 an exhausted Europe, militant nationalism, Japan's wartime victories, the Atlantic Charter, the UN (Res. 1514, 1960) and US Cold War calculations ended empires: 750 million people lived in dependencies in 1945, and the UN grew from 51 to 193 members. Asia went first (Philippines 1946, India 1947, Burma and Ceylon 1948, Indonesia 1949, Indochina 1953-54, Malaya 1957), Africa next (Libya 1951, Ghana 1957, 17 states in 1960, Algeria 1962 after war, Kenya 1963, Portuguese Africa 1975, Zimbabwe 1980, Namibia 1990, majority rule in South Africa 1994), the Caribbean by negotiation (1962-83). Britain negotiated and kept the Commonwealth; France fought in Indochina and Algeria. Artificial borders, Cold War proxy wars, weak economies and inexperienced leaders produced coups, civil wars and neo-colonial dependence - but also a Third World voice and the end of Eurocentrism. The earlier Latin American wave (1810-1828) was creole-led, triggered by Napoleon's invasion of Spain, and left caudillos and fragmentation.

The anti-colonial struggles in West Africa were led by the new elite of Western-educated Africans. Examine.
