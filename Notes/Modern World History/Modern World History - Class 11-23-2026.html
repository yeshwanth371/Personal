17. The Cold War (1945-1991) - continued: causes, intensification and phases

17.4 Causes of the Cold War (part 2)

Causes 1 (ideological conflict) and 2 (historical complications) are in the 20 November note.

17.4.3 Role of the leaders - the Big Three and their conferences

  • The Big Three - Franklin D. Roosevelt (USA), Winston Churchill (Britain) and Joseph Stalin (USSR) - had deep ideological differences but were forced to cooperate against Hitler. The alliance was a marriage of convenience; once the common enemy was gone, the differences surfaced.
  • Dissolution of the Comintern (May 1943): to reassure his allies that the USSR was not exporting revolution, Stalin had the Communist International dissolved - the decision was taken on 15 May 1943 and announced on 22 May †.

The Yalta reparations protocol says the Soviet and American delegations agreed that the Moscow Reparation Commission should take "as a basis for discussion" the Soviet suggestion of a total of $20 billion, 50% of it to the USSR (hence $10 billion). The British delegation held that no figures should be mentioned. At Potsdam, Truman and Byrnes replaced this with reparations taken by each power from its own zone. [Correction] "USSR control over Manchuria" overstates the deal. The secret Agreement regarding Japan (11 February 1945) restored "the former rights of Russia" lost in 1904-05: southern Sakhalin and the Kurile Islands to the USSR, the lease of Port Arthur as a Soviet naval base, an internationalised port of Dairen (Dalian), and joint Soviet-Chinese operation of the Manchurian railways - "it being understood that... [Correction] Stalin did not promise to "violate" the pact as such. He agreed that the USSR would enter the war against Japan "in two or three months after Germany has surrendered". In practice this meant ending the Neutrality Pact of 13 April 1941: the USSR denounced it on 5 April 1945 and declared war on Japan on 8 August 1945 - exactly three months after Germany's surrender (see the 20 November note).

[Correction] The promise of elections was a joint one, not Stalin's alone: in the Declaration on Liberated Europe all three governments pledged to help form interim governments "broadly representative of all democratic elements" and to hold free elections. For Poland the reorganised government was "pledged to the holding of free and unfettered elections as soon as possible". The Polish elections, held in January 1947 †, were rigged in the communists' favour - which the West read as proof of Soviet bad faith. Source: Avalon Project - Yalta.

Result: by May 1945 the Axis had been defeated in Europe and Germany was divided into four Allied occupation zones (US, British, French, Soviet), with Berlin, deep inside the Soviet zone, also divided into four sectors (see 20 November note, Map 5).

Potsdam (17 July - 2 August 1945) and the satellite states

  • The leadership had changed: Harry Truman was the new US President, and Clement Attlee replaced Churchill as British Prime Minister during the conference.
  • Stalin set up a series of satellite communist states in eastern Europe, from the Baltic Sea in the north to the Adriatic Sea in the south, as a buffer or protective shield for the USSR - in the Soviet view, self-defence against a third invasion from the west (after 1914 and 1941). This irritated the USA and Britain.
  • Germany: in the notes, the Western leaders proposed a unified Germany, Stalin opposed it, and the West refused him reparations from western Germany, which Stalin also wanted. The corrected position is in c07. The differences of opinion began here.
  • The atomic bomb: Truman kept great secrecy over the development and use of nuclear weapons. On 24 July 1945 he told Stalin only that the USA had a new weapon of unusual destructive force; Stalin already knew of the programme through Soviet intelligence. After Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August), Stalin saw the US bomb as a threat to the USSR ("atomic diplomacy").

[Correction] "London - Clement Attlee" should read Britain - Clement Attlee. Churchill attended the first part of the conference; when Labour won the 1945 general election, Attlee replaced him as Prime Minister (from 26 July 1945) and as Britain's delegate. Source: Office of the Historian - Potsdam.

The real quarrel was over reparations: Truman and Byrnes insisted that each power take reparations mainly from its own zone, plus a share of industrial equipment from the western zones for the USSR; the American military governor stopped deliveries from the US zone in May 1946 †. Later, in March 1952, it was Stalin who offered a united, neutral Germany (the "Stalin Note"), which the West rejected †. Both sides wanted German unity only on their own terms. 17.4.4 Post-war problems

(a) The Paris Peace Conference (29 July - 15 October 1946)

  • Decisions were prepared by the Allied Council of Foreign Ministers; the Soviet Foreign Minister was V.M. Molotov.
  • Molotov's demands (class notes): a divided Germany and reparations from West Germany as well; the Italian territory of Trieste for Yugoslavia; Italian colonies in Africa for the USSR. All of these were opposed by the other Allied ministers.
  • Molotov refused to withdraw Soviet troops from Iran (and, according to the notes, from Turkey - see c09).
  • The differences of opinion grew from here.

[Correction] The Paris Peace Conference did not deal with Germany. It drafted the peace treaties with Germany's former allies - Italy, Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria and Finland - which were signed on 10 February 1947 †. Germany was argued over in separate Council of Foreign Ministers meetings (Moscow, March-April 1947; London, November-December 1947 †), which failed. On Trieste the result was a compromise: a Free Territory of Trieste under UN Security Council guarantee (1947), divided between Italy and Yugoslavia in 1954 †. On the colonies, the USSR had asked (in 1945) for a trusteeship over Tripolitania (western Libya), not for all of Italy's colonies †; Italy renounced its colonies, and Libya became independent in 1951 †. Source: FRUS 1946, Paris Peace Conference - Molotov on Trieste.

[Correction] There were no Soviet troops in Turkey. The two cases were different: Soviet troops stayed in northern Iran beyond the agreed withdrawal date of early March 1946 and backed separatists in Iranian Azerbaijan, leaving only in May 1946 after Iran's complaint to the UN Security Council and US pressure; Turkey faced Soviet demands for bases and joint control of the Turkish Straits (and claims to Kars and Ardahan †). The US Office of the Historian lists both - with Soviet rejection of the Baruch Plan (June 1946) - among the "four setbacks" of 1946. Source: Office of the Historian - Truman Doctrine.

(b) Stalin's election speech (9 February 1946)

In this situation Stalin delivered a speech, which the class notes summarise as:

  1. Capitalism and communism cannot exist in the same world.
  2. Peaceful coexistence with the West was impossible until the final victory of communism.

[Correction] These two lines are the Western reading of the speech, not Stalin's words. Speaking to voters at the Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow, on 9 February 1946, Stalin said that the Second World War had broken out "as the inevitable result of the development of world economic and political forces on the basis of present-day monopolistic capitalism", that the "uneven development of capitalist countries" leads to crises and wars, and that Soviet victory proved the Soviet system superior; he called for new Five-Year Plans to triple industrial output. Western officials heard this as a declaration that war with the capitalist world was inevitable. Source: Seventeen Moments in Soviet History (MSU) - text of the speech; CFR - primary sources.

(c) Decolonisation and Western suspicion

Atlantic Charter (1941): right of peoples to choose their government → Japan's conquest of East and South-East Asia shatters European prestige → UN Charter (1945): self-determination → Decolonisation of Asia and Africa
  • Many newly independent Asian and African countries adopted socialist policies (planning, state-led industry, land reform); the West suspected Soviet involvement.
  • Domino theory: a Cold War geopolitical theory that if one non-communist country fell to communism, its neighbours would also fall, like a row of dominoes. [Additional] The image was given by President Eisenhower at a press conference on 7 April 1954, about Indochina †; it justified US involvement in Vietnam (17.6.2).

17.5 Intensification of the Cold War (1946-1955)

From 1946 each side read the other's moves as aggressive and answered in kind. The West adopted containment; the USSR tightened its grip on eastern Europe. Germany, the alliances and the bomb turned the quarrel into a lasting structure. Figure 1 pairs the main moves.

17.5.1 Kennan's Long Telegram (22 February 1946) and the policy of containment

  • George F. Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent the "Long Telegram" to Washington. He highlighted Stalin's speech, the spread of communism in eastern Europe and the growing influence of the USSR.
  • He suggested what became the policy of containment: restrict Soviet influence to eastern Europe and do not let communism spread further.

[Correction] The Long Telegram (22 February 1946 †) analysed the sources of Soviet behaviour and argued that Soviet power would yield to firm resistance; the word and the policy of "containment" were set out publicly in Kennan's anonymous "X" article, "The Sources of Soviet Conduct", in Foreign Affairs, July 1947: "a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies". Source: Office of the Historian - Kennan and Containment.

17.5.2 Churchill's Fulton speech - the "Iron Curtain" speech (5 March 1946)

  • Speaking at Westminster College, Fulton, Missouri (USA), with President Truman on the platform †, Churchill said that "from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent" - pointing to the USSR's unshakeable hold over eastern Europe (Map 1, places 9 and 5).
  • He warned that the USSR could go on to dominate Europe, and proposed close Anglo-American cooperation to counter the USSR and the spread of communism.

[Correction] Churchill did not propose an alliance "like the Atlantic Charter". His formal title was "The Sinews of Peace" †, and his remedy was a "fraternal association of the English-speaking peoples" - a special relationship between Britain and the USA, including military cooperation - within the UN. Churchill was then Leader of the Opposition, not Prime Minister †, and Stalin denounced the speech as a call to war †. The Atlantic Charter (1941) was a joint statement of war aims, not an alliance.

17.5.3 The Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947) and Kennan's "X" article (July 1947)

  • President Harry Truman promised political, economic and military help to countries threatened by the USSR and communism. In his words it became "the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures".
  • Critics argued that such an open-ended commitment could drag the USA into another war.
  • Kennan's "Mr X" article in Foreign Affairs insisted on containment but asked American leaders to explore options other than war. [Additional] Kennan saw the Soviet threat as mainly political and favoured economic aid (the Marshall Plan) and psychological warfare; Paul Nitze's militarised reading won out in NSC-68 (1950), which called for a large expansion of US military spending. John Foster Dulles attacked containment as too passive and spoke of "rollback" and "liberation" in 1952.

[Correction] The Truman Doctrine was not "influenced by the Pentagon". Its immediate cause was a British announcement (February 1947) that Britain could no longer aid the Greek government against communist insurgents after 31 March, and that Turkey too had depended on British aid. Truman asked Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey in a speech to a joint session on 12 March 1947. Ironically, Stalin had deliberately withheld support from the Greek communists. Source: Office of the Historian - Truman Doctrine.

17.5.4 The Marshall Plan (European Recovery Program), 1947-1952

  • Announced by Secretary of State George C. Marshall at Harvard University on 5 June 1947, the plan proposed the economic revival of Europe through fresh US-led investment, to generate employment and prosperity and so counter the spread of communism and Soviet influence.
  • It was accepted: the US Congress passed the Economic Cooperation Act (March-April 1948), and about $13 billion was invested in western Europe (see c14).
  • Western efforts succeeded and containment worked in western Europe.
  • [Additional] Aid was offered to the USSR and eastern Europe too, but Moscow refused and made its satellites (including Czechoslovakia and Poland, which were interested) refuse as well †; Marshall became the only general to receive the Nobel Peace Prize (1953 †).

[Correction] "American parliament" is the US Congress. On the amount: the US Office of the Historian says Congress approved funding "that would eventually rise to over $12 billion"; many textbooks give about $13 billion (1948-1952) - the figure depends on what is counted. Use "$12-13 billion". Source: Office of the Historian - Marshall Plan.

17.5.5 The Soviet response

  1. Cominform (Communist Information Bureau), September 1947 †: set up by Stalin to coordinate the European communist parties and spread anti-imperialist, anti-Western propaganda; it called the world divided into "two camps". (Dissolved 1956 †.)
  2. Berlin Blockade, 24 June 1948 - 12 May 1949: after the Western powers merged their zones (Bizonia, 1 January 1947) and introduced the new Deutschmark in their zones and West Berlin (June 1948), the Soviets cut all road, rail and canal links to West Berlin - holding it hostage. The West answered with the Berlin Airlift (US "Operation Vittles" from 26 June 1948; British "Operation Plainfare"); at its height a plane landed at Tempelhof every 45 seconds. The blockade failed and was lifted in May 1949.

Source: Office of the Historian - The Berlin Airlift, 1948-1949.

17.5.7 Rival alliances

  • The USA considerably expanded its geopolitical influence through new groupings: NATO (1949), ANZUS (1951), SEATO (1954) and the Baghdad Pact / METO (1955), renamed CENTO (1959).
  • The USSR set up COMECON (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955). COMECON was the Soviet answer to the Marshall Plan and the Western economic order (the World Bank and IMF, which the USSR did not join †): it helped the communist states of Europe economically. The Warsaw Pact was the military answer to NATO - signed between the USSR and its satellites days after West Germany joined NATO.
  • Jupiter missiles in Turkey: the notes record that the USA set up a secret nuclear missile base in Turkey targeting the USSR (corrected: c17).

Cold War rivalry → Arms race (nuclear) → Missiles (ICBMs) → Space race (Sputnik 1957) → MAD - "balance of terror" → Bipolar world, proxy conflicts

The Cold War thus created a bipolar world and bipolar politics - a tense, war-like situation expressed through a series of proxy conflicts. It passed through several stages (17.6).

17.6.2 First phase (1945-1962): confrontation and brinkmanship

This sub-section follows the class handout. The handout text is rearranged where it was garbled (for example the Geneva Accords) and checked.

(a) The Arab-Israeli War of 1948 (First Arab-Israeli War; Israel's War of Independence; for Palestinians, the Nakba, "catastrophe")

  • Background: the end of the British Mandate in Palestine and the UN Partition Plan (General Assembly Resolution 181, 29 November 1947 †), which proposed separate Jewish and Arab states with Jerusalem under international rule. The Jewish leadership accepted it; the Arab states and Palestinian Arabs rejected it, and civil war followed.
  • On 14 May 1948 Israel declared independence (Tel Aviv). Five Arab states - Egypt, Transjordan (Jordan), Syria, Lebanon and Iraq - intervened next day to prevent a Jewish state and claim parts of Palestine.
  • Though outnumbered, the newly formed Israel Defense Forces won critical victories. By early 1949 Israel held more territory than the UN plan had allotted. Jordan took the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and Egypt occupied the Gaza Strip.
  • Over 700,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled - a major refugee crisis. Armistice agreements were signed in 1949 (with Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria †), but no lasting peace was achieved.
  • Significance: it entrenched the Arab-Israeli divide, led to further wars (1956, 1967, 1973) and transformed the political geography of the Middle East. It remains a deeply contested, foundational moment of the Israel-Palestine conflict - Israeli and Palestinian narratives of 1948 differ sharply.
  • Cold War angle [Additional]: both the USA and the USSR recognised Israel in May 1948 †; superpower alignment came later (the USSR with Egypt and Syria from 1955, the USA with Israel especially after 1967).
  • India link [Additional]: India voted against the Partition Plan in 1947, recognised Israel in September 1950 †, but established full diplomatic relations only in 1992 †.

[Correction] In 1948 Jordan was officially the Hashemite Kingdom of Transjordan; it took the name Jordan in 1949 †. Its control of the West Bank dates from the 1948-49 war, but its formal annexation came in April 1950 † (recognised by very few states) - not "by early 1949" as the handout implies.

(b) The Chinese Civil War (1946-1949) and the Chinese Revolution (1949)

  • Fought between the Nationalist Kuomintang (KMT) under Chiang Kai-shek and the Communist Party of China (CPC) under Mao Zedong after the Japanese occupation ended. The two sides had cooperated against Japan (the Second United Front, 1937 †) but fought again soon after Japan's defeat. The USA supported the KMT; the USSR gave limited aid to the CPC.
  • The Communists drew strength from peasant support, superior guerrilla tactics and land reform. Corruption, inflation and low morale weakened the KMT. By late 1949 the CPC had won decisively and the KMT retreated to Taiwan (Republic of China).
  • On 1 October 1949 Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China (PRC) in Peking (Beijing). The revolution replaced a "semi-feudal, semi-colonial" order (the Marxist description) with a Marxist-Leninist one-party state: land redistribution, nationalisation of industry and authoritarian rule.
  • Internationally it extended the Cold War to Asia: the Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship (February 1950) † created a communist bloc from the Elbe to the Pacific, and the USA kept recognising the ROC on Taiwan as "China" (including in the UN) until the 1970s.
  • India link [Additional]: India recognised the PRC at the end of December 1949 † - among the first non-communist states to do so - and argued for its seat in the UN.

(c) The Korean Crisis - the Korean War (1950-1953)

  • After World War II Korea (a Japanese colony since 1910) was divided along the 38th parallel: Soviet troops in the north, US troops in the south. The North became a communist state under Kim Il-sung, the South a capitalist republic under Syngman Rhee (both 1948 †).
  • On 25 June 1950 North Korea invaded the South to unify the peninsula under communist rule. A UN coalition led by the USA intervened, authorised by the Security Council while the USSR was boycotting it over China's seat †.
  • Rapid advances and reversals: the North pushed the UN forces to the Pusan perimeter; the Inchon landing (September 1950) † turned the tide and UN forces drove towards the Yalu; Chinese intervention (from October 1950) forced them back south. By 1951 the front stabilised near the original line, and a bloody stalemate followed.
  • An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953, but no peace treaty: Korea is technically still at war. The Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) still separates North and South.
  • Significance: it solidified the Cold War divide in Asia and militarised US foreign policy (NSC-68 put into effect, US forces in Japan and Korea, aid to the French in Indochina).
  • India link [Additional]: India sent a medical unit (60th Parachute Field Ambulance) †, helped break the deadlock over prisoners of war (V.K. Krishna Menon's UN resolution, December 1952 †) and chaired the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission (General K.S. Thimayya), with an Indian Custodian Force †.

(d) The Vietnam Wars

I. First Vietnam War (First Indochina War), 1946-1954: the anti-colonial struggle

  • Fought between France and the Viet Minh, a communist-nationalist movement led by Ho Chi Minh, which had declared Vietnam's independence on 2 September 1945 †. France tried to reassert colonial control; full war began in December 1946 †.
  • The Viet Minh used effective guerrilla tactics, with support from the USSR and (from 1950) China. The decisive French defeat came at Dien Bien Phu (March-May 1954) †.
  • The war ended with the Geneva Accords (July 1954) †:
    • temporary division of Vietnam along the 17th parallel;
    • Ho Chi Minh's communist government in the North (Hanoi);
    • the US-backed State of Vietnam under Emperor Bao Dai in the South (from 1955 the Republic of Vietnam under Ngo Dinh Diem);
    • national elections scheduled for 1956 - never held, as the South (backed by the USA, which had not signed the accords) refused.
  • India link [Additional]: India chaired the International Control Commission (India, Canada, Poland) that supervised the Geneva Accords †.

II. Second Vietnam War (the Vietnam War), 1955-1975: the Cold War proxy conflict

  • Fought between North Vietnam (supported by the USSR, China and the Viet Cong insurgents in the South) and South Vietnam (backed by the USA and its allies). The USA aimed to contain communism, guided by the domino theory.
  • US involvement escalated after the Gulf of Tonkin incident (August 1964) and Congress's Tonkin Gulf Resolution †. Despite technological superiority, US forces struggled against guerrilla tactics and North Vietnamese resilience.
  • Massive bombing, the Tet Offensive (January-February 1968) † and opposition at home led President Nixon to withdraw troops under "Vietnamization". [Additional] The Paris Peace Accords (27 January 1973) † ended direct US participation.
  • The war ended with the fall of Saigon (30 April 1975) and the unification of Vietnam under communist rule (formally 2 July 1976 †).
  • Impact: very heavy casualties; massive destruction of Vietnam's land and infrastructure (including by chemical defoliants such as Agent Orange †); a military and moral defeat for the USA; and growing anti-war sentiment and support for non-alignment worldwide.

[Verification Required] The handout's "over 3 million lives lost" is within the range of estimates, which differ widely by source and by what is counted (combatants and civilians, all of Indochina, 1955 or 1965 to 1975) - roughly 1.3 million to over 3 million. Quote a range, not a single figure.

Together, both wars show the clash of colonialism, nationalism and Cold War ideologies.

(e) The Suez Canal Crisis (1956) - the Second Arab-Israeli War

  • Cause: on 26 July 1956 Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal (the Mediterranean-Red Sea route, previously run by an Anglo-French company) to pay for the Aswan High Dam, after the USA and Britain withdrew their offer of finance (July 1956 †).
  • Tripartite aggression: Britain, France and Israel secretly planned intervention (the Protocol of Sevres †). On 29 October 1956 Israel invaded Sinai; Britain and France bombed Egypt and landed at Port Said, on the pretext of separating the combatants and securing the canal.
  • Backlash: the USA, the USSR and the United Nations condemned the invasion and forced withdrawal. A UN Emergency Force (UNEF) was deployed - the first armed UN peacekeeping force † - and Egypt kept the canal.
  • Significance: the decline of British and French imperial power; Nasser became the hero of Arab nationalism and a leader of Non-Alignment. A turning point in post-colonial international relations and Cold War dynamics. [Additional] It coincided with the Soviet crushing of the Hungarian uprising (October-November 1956) †, which the West, divided over Suez, could not oppose effectively.
  • India link [Additional]: Nehru criticised the invasion; India contributed troops to UNEF (1956-67) †. India's trade with Europe depended on the canal.

[Correction] The handout says Suez marked "the emergence of the US and USSR as superpowers". They were already superpowers by 1945; Suez confirmed it - by showing that Britain and France could no longer act against the wishes of the USA (which used financial pressure on sterling †) and that the USSR could threaten intervention in the Middle East.

(f) The Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962)

  • A 13-day confrontation between the USA and the USSR - the closest the world came to nuclear war. It began when US U-2 spy planes photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba (pictures taken 14 October 1962), about 90 miles from the US coast.
  • Causes: the Soviet move answered the US missiles in Turkey and was meant to protect Fidel Castro's communist Cuba from another invasion after the failed Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961).
  • President John F. Kennedy imposed a naval "quarantine" (22 October; the word avoided "blockade", which legally implied war) and demanded removal of the missiles.
  • After intense diplomacy and brinkmanship, Khrushchev agreed on 28 October to withdraw the missiles in return for a US public pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret undertaking to remove US missiles from Turkey.
  • Outcomes: a direct hotline between Washington and Moscow; the first arms-control agreements; recognition of the dangers of nuclear escalation.

Khrushchev's conditions for removing the missiles (class notes): (1) end the naval blockade of Cuba; (2) the USA not to invade Cuba; (3) the USA to remove its nuclear missiles from Turkey.

[Correction] Only condition (2) was part of the public settlement. Condition (3) appeared in Khrushchev's second message (27 October); Kennedy publicly answered only the first message, while Robert Kennedy secretly told Ambassador Dobrynin that the Jupiters would be removed soon but could not be part of any public deal. The quarantine was lifted on 20 November 1962, after the USSR also agreed to remove its IL-28 bombers; the Jupiters left Turkey in April 1963. Source: Office of the Historian - The Cuban Missile Crisis.

17.6.3 Second phase (1962-1979): detente

Detente (French: "relaxation") means the easing of tension between the USA and the USSR. It was a shift from direct confrontation to negotiation and cooperation, under the threat of nuclear war. It did not end the Cold War, but it built diplomatic frameworks - arms control above all - that shaped the treaties of the 1980s.

Background and need for detente:

  • By the early 1960s both superpowers had nuclear arsenals capable of Mutually Assured Destruction; the Cuban Missile Crisis showed how close catastrophe had come.
  • The Vietnam War strained US resources and morale.
  • The Sino-Soviet split weakened the communist bloc and gave the USA an opening.
  • Economic stagnation in the USSR pushed it to seek Western trade and technology.

The Sino-Soviet split - reasons (class notes)

  1. Boundary disputes along the long Sino-Soviet frontier, from the "unequal treaties" of the nineteenth century † - culminating in armed clashes on Zhenbao (Damansky) Island on the Ussuri River in March 1969 † (Map 5, place 9).
  2. The USSR did not share nuclear technology: it cancelled its 1957 agreement to help China build a bomb (1959) and withdrew its technical experts in 1960 †. China tested its own bomb in 1964 †.
  3. Differences in communist ideology: Mao rejected Khrushchev's de-Stalinisation (secret speech, February 1956) and "peaceful coexistence" with the West as "revisionism" †.
  4. Competition for leadership of the communist ("second") world and of the Third World.

Nixon's Realpolitik and Zhou Enlai's "ping-pong diplomacy"

  • Realpolitik - policy based on power and national interest rather than ideology - guided President Richard Nixon and his adviser Henry Kissinger: open relations with communist China to isolate North Vietnam and gain leverage over the USSR. China, frightened by the 1969 clashes, wanted the same.
  • Ping-pong diplomacy: after friendly contacts between US and Chinese players at the world championships in Japan, Premier Zhou Enlai's government invited the US table-tennis team to China in April 1971; the Chinese team visited the USA in April 1972.
  • [Additional] Kissinger made a secret trip to Beijing in July 1971, using a channel through Pakistan's President Yahya Khan; the PRC took China's UN seat in October 1971; Nixon visited China on 21-28 February 1972 (the Shanghai Communique). Source: Office of the Historian - Rapprochement with China.
  • India link [Additional]: the US-China-Pakistan alignment of 1971, at the time of the Bangladesh crisis, pushed India to sign the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation (9 August 1971) †.

17.6.4 Third phase (1979-1989): the "Second Cold War" and the winds of change

The Second Cold War was a renewed period of heightened tension from 1979 to 1989, reversing the gains of detente.

Causes:

  1. Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979): the USSR sent in troops to save the communist regime (PDPA) from an Islamic insurgency; troops killed President Hafizullah Amin and installed Babrak Karmal. The USA saw it as expansionist aggression - the only Soviet invasion of a country outside the Eastern bloc - and it marked the collapse of detente. The Carter Doctrine pledged to defend the Persian Gulf, and the USA armed the Afghan mujahideen. Soviet troops left only in February 1989 †. (Map 5, place 10.)
  2. Ronald Reagan (President from 1981): a hard-line anti-communist; called the USSR an "evil empire" (March 1983 †) and greatly increased defence spending.
  3. Revival of the arms race: both powers accelerated weapons development and deployment (Soviet SS-20s; US Pershing II and cruise missiles in Europe from 1983 †). Reagan launched the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI, "Star Wars") in March 1983 † to neutralise Soviet missiles, which escalated tension.
  4. Third World conflicts: proxy wars intensified in Central America (Nicaragua, El Salvador), Africa (Angola, Ethiopia) and Asia (Afghanistan), with each power backing opposing sides.
  5. Human rights and the Helsinki commitments: the USA criticised Soviet abuses, especially in eastern Europe (for example the suppression of Poland's Solidarity under martial law, 1981 †).

Winds of change (1985-1989):

  • Mikhail Gorbachev became Soviet leader in March 1985. His policies of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) aimed to reform the USSR and ease tensions.
  • The Reykjavik Summit (October 1986) † and later negotiations led to the INF Treaty (8 December 1987) †, which eliminated a whole class of intermediate-range nuclear missiles.
  • Cooperation resumed and hostility declined. The Second Cold War brought the world close to nuclear conflict again (notably the war scare of autumn 1983 †) but also prepared the end of the Cold War: diplomacy, internal reform in the USSR and economic pressure all played a part by 1989.

Mains Points

  • Why the wartime alliance broke up: a marriage of convenience against Hitler; vague Yalta promises (Poland, elections, reparations) read differently by each side; new leaders at Potsdam; the bomb and US secrecy; the satellite states as Soviet security versus Western fear of expansion (17.4.3, Map 1).
  • Post-war disputes as causes: peace treaties, Trieste and the colonies, Iran and the Straits (the "four setbacks" of 1946), and Stalin's speech read as a declaration of hostility (17.4.4).
  • Containment as a strategy: Kennan (political-economic) versus NSC-68 (military); the Truman Doctrine as the turn from isolation to global commitment; the Marshall Plan as economic containment and its success in western Europe (17.5.1-17.5.4).
  • Germany as the core of the Cold War in Europe: reparations, currency reform, the blockade and airlift, two German states, the Wall, reunification (17.5.6, Map 2).
  • Bloc formation: NATO and the Warsaw Pact, the "pactomania" of the 1950s (SEATO, Baghdad Pact) and its weaknesses; COMECON versus the Marshall Plan; the impact on South Asia (17.5.7, Map 3).
  • Nuclear deterrence: arms and space races, MAD as the reason the Cold War stayed "cold", brinkmanship and its limits (Cuba) (17.5.8, 17.6.2).
  • Globalisation of the Cold War: the Chinese Revolution, Korea and Vietnam as proxy wars; the Middle East (1948, Suez); decolonisation entangled with bloc rivalry (17.6.2).
  • Detente - causes, achievements and limits: MAD, Vietnam, the Sino-Soviet split, Soviet economic needs; arms control and Helsinki; collapse over SS-20s, human rights, Africa and Afghanistan (17.6.3).
  • The Second Cold War and its end: Afghanistan, Reagan's build-up and SDI, then Gorbachev's reforms and the INF Treaty (17.6.4).
  • India and the Cold War: non-alignment; Pakistan in SEATO/CENTO and India's tilt to the USSR; Korea (NNRC), Indochina (ICC), Suez (UNEF); PTBT yes, NPT no; the 1971 Indo-Soviet Treaty as a response to US-China-Pakistan alignment.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Long Telegram (Feb 1946, secret cable) vs "X" article (Jul 1947, public, coined "containment") - both by Kennan.
  • Fulton (Missouri, USA) is where Churchill gave the "iron curtain" speech - not in Britain; Churchill was then not Prime Minister.
  • Truman Doctrine (military and economic aid, Greece and Turkey, Mar 1947) vs Marshall Plan (economic recovery of Europe, Jun 1947).
  • Cominform (1947) is not the Comintern (1919-43); COMECON (1949) is economic, the Warsaw Pact (1955) military.
  • NATO (1949) came six years before the Warsaw Pact (1955).
  • SEATO had only two South-East Asian members; CENTO was in the Middle East, not Central Asia; the USA was not a member of CENTO.
  • Paris Peace Conference 1919 (Versailles) vs Paris Peace Conference 1946 (treaties with Germany's allies, not Germany).
  • FRG founded 1949, not 1948; Berlin Wall 1961 was built 12 years after the blockade (1948-49).
  • Explorer 1 (Jan 1958) preceded NASA (Oct 1958); Sputnik 1 (Oct 1957) was first overall.
  • Project Sundial was a proposal, never built; the largest bomb actually exploded was the Soviet Tsar Bomba (1961).
  • 38th parallel = Korea; 17th parallel = Vietnam.
  • First Indochina War = France vs Viet Minh (1946-54); Vietnam War = USA/South vs North/Viet Cong (1955-75).
  • Suez 1956 = "Second Arab-Israeli War"; first Arab-Israeli war = 1948.
  • Cuban Missile Crisis: the no-invasion pledge was public; the Turkey missile removal was secret.
  • PTBT bans tests in the atmosphere, outer space and under water - not underground. India signed the PTBT but not the NPT.
  • SALT I (1972) was ratified; SALT II (1979) was signed but never ratified. INF (1987) eliminated a class of missiles; SALT only limited them.
  • Third phase starts 1979 (Afghanistan), not 1978.

Allies against Hitler (Tehran 1943, Yalta Feb 1945, Potsdam Jul 1945) fell out over Poland, Germany, reparations and the bomb. Stalin built a buffer of satellites; Iran, the Straits and the peace treaties added friction (1946). Kennan (Long Telegram, X article) gave containment; Churchill named the iron curtain; Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan (1947) met Cominform and the Berlin Blockade (1948-49). Two Germanys (1949), NATO (1949) vs Warsaw Pact (1955), SEATO and CENTO ringed the bloc. Bombs (1945, 1949, 1952-55) and Sputnik (1957) produced MAD. Phase 1 (1945-62): brinkmanship - 1948 Arab-Israeli war, Chinese Revolution, Korea, Indochina, Suez, Cuba. Phase 2 (1962-79): detente - Hotline, PTBT, NPT, Sino-Soviet split, Nixon in China, SALT I, Helsinki. Phase 3 (1979-89): Afghanistan, Reagan, SDI - then Gorbachev, Reykjavik and INF. Corrections Made

  1. c01 - Yalta reparations: $20 bn "basis for discussion", 50% to the USSR; not a promise of $10 bn.
  2. c02 - Yalta Far East deal: specific rights in Manchuria; China kept sovereignty.
  3. c03 - Stalin promised to enter the war against Japan 2-3 months after Germany's surrender; pact denounced 5 Apr 1945.
  4. c04 - Free elections were a joint pledge (Declaration on Liberated Europe; Poland).
  5. c05 - Attlee was Britain's (not "London's") new PM at Potsdam.
  6. c06 - Yugoslavia and Albania not Soviet-installed; takeovers gradual, 1944-49.
  7. c07 - The German dispute was over reparations; both sides wanted unity only on their own terms (Stalin Note 1952).
  8. c08 - Paris Peace Conference 1946 dealt with Germany's allies, not Germany; Trieste compromise; Tripolitania.
  9. c09 - No Soviet troops in Turkey; Iran (troops) and Turkey (Straits demands) were separate crises.
  10. c10 - Stalin's 9 Feb 1946 speech: what he said vs the Western reading.
  11. c11 - "Containment" was set out in the X article (July 1947), not the Long Telegram.
  12. c12 - Fulton: a "fraternal association of the English-speaking peoples", not an Atlantic Charter-type alliance.
  13. c13 - Truman Doctrine triggered by Britain's withdrawal from Greece and Turkey, not by the Pentagon.
  14. c14 - US Congress; Marshall aid $12-13 bn.
  15. c15 - FRG founded 23 May 1949, not 1948.
  16. c16 - Germany's division: 1949 states, 1952 border sealed, 1955 sovereignty, 1972 mutual recognition.
  17. c17 - Jupiter missiles in Turkey: 1959-63, known, not secret; removal was the secret.
  18. c18 - SEATO membership; CENTO in the Middle East (Baghdad Pact, 1955; CENTO 1959).
  19. c19 - Soviet H-bomb 1953/1955; Project Sundial never built.
  20. c20 - Explorer 1 (Jan 1958) came before NASA (Oct 1958).
  21. c21 - Third phase from 1979; detente dates.
  22. c22 - Transjordan in 1948; West Bank annexed April 1950.
  23. c23 - Suez confirmed, not created, superpower status.
  24. c24 - Cuban deal: public no-invasion pledge; secret Turkey undertaking.
  25. c25 - SALT II was signed (18 Jun 1979) but never ratified.
