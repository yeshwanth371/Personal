1. What is History? Periodisation

1.1 Definitions

  • History is the study of the political, economic and socio-cultural evolution of mankind within the fabric of space and time.
  • History is an unending dialogue between the past and the present, oriented towards a better future. [Additional] The phrase echoes E. H. Carr, What Is History? (1961), who called history "a continuous process of interaction between the historian and his facts, an unending dialogue between the present and the past" †.

1.2 "World history is also called European history"

The rough notes say that world history is also called European history. See c01: the two are not the same, though this course starts with Europe because the developments of the 15th-18th centuries that shaped the modern world (voyages, capitalism, colonialism, industrialisation) began from Europe.

[Correction] "World history is also called European history." World history studies all regions and their interactions (Asia, Africa, the Americas and Europe). Treating it as European history is a Eurocentric habit, not a definition. Better: the modern world was, to a large extent, shaped by European expansion, so Europe is the starting point of this syllabus. [Additional] This matters in answers: many "modern" developments (printing, gunpowder, the compass, paper, the spice trade, Arab mathematics and medicine) came from Asia and the Islamic world.

2. Development 1: The Old Order (Medieval Europe)

The Old Order refers to the political and socio-economic conditions of medieval Europe.

2.1 Polity

  • Europe mainly had hereditary monarchy; "also had theocracy" (c03).
  • It created the system called feudalism: a combination of political, legal, socio-economic and military customs that developed in medieval Europe.
  • Broadly, it was a way of structuring society around relationships that arose from control of land, in return for labour, services, taxes, loyalty and tribute.
  • [Additional] Key terms: fief (land held in return for service), lord and vassal, homage and fealty (the oath of loyalty), manor (the lord's estate), serf (a peasant tied to the land).
  • Structure in the class: Monarch → Nobility → Knights → Commoners (mostly peasants), called the feudal pyramid; land was transferred downwards, and money, services and loyalty moved upwards (Figure 2, c04).
  • Feudalism was a decentralised system, and monarchs were relatively weak (a lord's loyalty was first to his own overlord, not to the king).

[Correction] "Europe mainly had hereditary monarchy, also had theocracy." Medieval Europe was not a theocracy in the strict sense (rule by priests or God's direct representatives). It had two rival centres of authority: kings and the emperor on one side, and the Pope and the Church on the other. The Church was very influential in politics (see 2.2) but kings ruled in their own right; the long struggle over who appointed bishops (the Investiture Controversy, late 11th-early 12th century †) shows the conflict. Not every ruler was hereditary: the Holy Roman Emperor and the Pope were elected †.

[Correction] "Monarch → Nobility → Knights → Commoners." The model is right in outline but needs three fixes. (a) The second tier is the great lords (dukes, counts, barons) who held land directly from the king; knights were their lesser vassals. (b) The base is peasants (freemen and serfs); townspeople (merchants, craftsmen) were a later, separate group and are not in the pyramid. (c) Land was held, not owned: it was granted as a fief in return for service, rather than "transferred" as property. The clergy stood outside this pyramid.

2.2 Society

  • Feudal society was dominated by privileged classes - royalty and nobility.
  • Common people had no political power, did not own land, had no basic rights, and the society was full of inequalities.
  • A powerful institution, the Catholic Church (founded on the teachings of Jesus Christ) (c05); the Bible had the status of a constitution (the supreme authority, as interpreted by the Church; [Additional] the Church's own law was canon law).
  • Head: the Pope (at Rome); the priestly class: the clergy.
  • The Church influenced politics and dominated society: it controlled arts, education and social beliefs.
  • [Additional] The three-estate model (clergy, nobility, commoners) was used to describe society, especially in France †.

[Correction] "Catholic church ... known as catholism - orthodox christianity." Roman Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy are two different branches of Christianity, not one. Western Europe followed the Pope of Rome; the Byzantine (eastern) world followed the Patriarch of Constantinople. A de facto schism between them is dated to 1054, and relations soured further after the Crusades (Britannica, Fall of Constantinople and Crusades). "Orthodox" in the notes may mean "traditional", but in history "Orthodox" normally means the Eastern church.

2.3 Economy

  • Agrarian economy plus medieval industries powered by human and animal labour.
  • Industries were run by masters and apprentices (skilled workers) and regulated by guilds and associations. [Additional] A craftsman rose from apprentice to journeyman to master; the guild fixed quality, prices and entry.
  • The economy was static: goods were produced for the local market, so there was no need to improve traditional methods of production.
  • Trade was mainly local and based on barter. [Additional] Coin and credit were also used, especially in towns; "mainly barter" is a simplification.
  • Foreign trade also existed, mostly in spices: Asia (the "Indies") produced the spices and Europe needed them (see 2.4).

2.4 Spice trade, Silk Route and Muslim control

  • The spice trade began early; the class dates it to the 10th century BCE (c06).
  • The Silk Route from China to Europe started for silk; later the spice trade also used it. It had a northern path, a southern path and a maritime Silk Route (Map 1).
  • Because so much money flowed through Central Asia along these routes, the region became conflict-prone.
  • Europe's insatiable appetite for spices created trade routes between East and West, along which people, goods, wealth and ideas moved. [Additional] Spices were wanted for flavouring, preserving food, medicine and as luxury goods (Britannica on Columbus: "herbs and spices for cooking, preserving, and medicine"); the main spices were pepper (Malabar coast), cinnamon, cloves and nutmeg (the Spice Islands), and ginger.
  • At the same time Islam was born and spread (7th-10th centuries CE) and consolidated; Muslim powers came to dominate these trade routes and also controlled the holy places of Christianity (c07).

[Additional] Who carried the trade? The route had many middlemen: Chinese, Central Asian, Persian, Indian and Arab merchants, then Muslim traders in the eastern Mediterranean and Egypt, and finally Italian (Venetian and Genoese) merchants who sold to Europe. Each stage added cost, so spices reached Europe at many times their Asian price. The name "Silk Road" was coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877 †. India link: the Malabar coast (Calicut) was the centre of the pepper trade and is where Vasco da Gama landed in 1498 (7).

[Correction] "Spice trade started around the 10th century BCE." [Verification Required] No source opened for this note supports a precise start. Indian Ocean exchange of spices is much older than the Silk Route, and the overland Silk Route is usually dated from the Han dynasty (c. 2nd century BCE) †. Treat "10th century BCE" as the teacher's approximation and check it against NCERT.

[Correction] "7th to 10th centuries BCE" for the birth, spread and consolidation of Islam should read CE: Islam arose in the 7th century CE in Arabia. [Additional] The "Muslim control of Christian holy places" is not new in the 11th century: Jerusalem came under Muslim rule in the 7th century †; what changed in the 11th century was the advance of the Seljuq Turks, who defeated the Byzantines at Manzikert (1071), occupied most of Asia Minor, and made pilgrimage to Jerusalem difficult (Britannica, Crusades).

3. Development 2: The Crusades (1095-1291)

  • The Crusades were holy wars organised by Christendom (c08). They lasted about 200 years (1095-1291) and, in the class count, 8 major crusades were fought.
  • In the end the Crusaders were defeated: the last Latin Christian possessions in Syria were lost in 1291 (c08).
  • At the same time, the Crusades opened the East to the West.

[Additional] Why and how they began. Britannica: the Crusades were military expeditions by western European Christians, answering centuries of Muslim expansion; the aims were to check Islam, retake the Holy Land, conquer pagan areas and recapture formerly Christian lands, and many took part as a way of redemption for sin. The Byzantine emperor Alexius Comnenus asked the West for help after the Seljuqs reached close to Constantinople. Pope Urban II convoked the Council of Clermont on 18 November 1095 and called for the First Crusade; the crowd cried "Deus le volt" ("God wills it") and those who went wore a cross.

3.1 Effects

  • Crusaders needed food, weapons, clothing, medicines, luxury goods and horses; to get them they established new trading contacts with the Easterners.
  • This led to increased trade and prosperity; urbanisation increased and trading cities such as Genoa and Venice grew (Map 2, places 4-5).
  • New employment opportunities led to rural-urban migration; the migration and the shortage of labourers (commoners) in the villages weakened feudalism.
  • [Additional] Other effects: nobles spent fortunes and often died, which weakened them; kings gained power by taxing the war effort (see 4); the papacy's prestige rose and later fell (Britannica: crusading declined in the 16th century with the Reformation and the decline of papal authority); Europeans met Islamic learning, sugar, cotton and new goods †.

[Additional] Caution on cause and effect. Britannica notes that an economic revival was in full swing before the First Crusade and that Italian shipping was already challenging Muslim predominance in the Mediterranean. The Crusades therefore speeded up contact and trade; they did not start it.

[Correction] (a) "Crusades got defeated at the end" is only part of the story. The First Crusade succeeded (Jerusalem, 1099 †) and the Crusader states lasted about two centuries; they were finally lost in 1291 (fall of Acre). (b) The wars were called by the papacy (Pope Urban II), not by "Christendom" as a single authority. (c) "8 major crusades" follows one traditional numbering; books count seven, eight or nine, and Britannica notes numerous expeditions, including to Spain and the Baltic, continuing after 1291.

4. Development 3: Strengthening of European Monarchies

  • Europe faced frequent foreign invasions (c09), which increased the importance of European monarchs.
  • Europeans adopted better technology - gunpowder, cannon and artillery - and created standing armies.
  • Dependence on the nobility declined, which led to the decline of feudalism and centralisation of power.
  • This led to the rise of absolute monarchs, called autocrats; when cruel and suppressive they were called despots or tyrants (c10).
  • The doctrine of the divine right of kings developed, and the Church's influence on politics reduced.

[Additional] Why technology mattered. A knight in armour and a castle wall could be defeated by cannon, and only a king could pay for cannon, gunpowder and a standing (permanent, paid) army; taxes replaced feudal service. Britannica's account of 1453 shows the effect: the Ottoman cannon cast by the Hungarian gunsmith Urban broke the walls of Constantinople (see 6). Examples of the "new monarchies": France, England (the Tudors), and Spain (the union of Ferdinand and Isabella) †. The Hundred Years' War (1337-1453) between England and France pushed both kings to raise permanent armies and national taxes †.

[Additional] Divine right meant that the king's authority came directly from God, not from the Pope, the nobles or the people; therefore he was answerable only to God. It was used by Stuart kings in England and Louis XIV in France †; the Church could no longer claim to control kings (a famous example: Henry VIII's break with Rome, Act of Supremacy 1534 †). Contrast: the Glorious Revolution (1688-89) ended divine-right absolutism in England.

[Correction] "europe was facing feq foriegn invasions" - [Verification Required] the rough note is unclear: it reads like "frequent" invasions (so it is kept that way above) but could be "few". Frequent invasions and wars (Vikings and Magyars earlier; Mongols; Ottomans and the long English-French wars) are what the argument needs: constant danger made people look to a strong central ruler. Confirm with the class teacher.

[Correction] "Absolute monarchs are called autocrats - cruel and suppressive - despots / tyrants." Absolutism means the monarch held all state power without effective legal limits; it does not mean that every absolute monarch was cruel. "Despot" and "tyrant" are value judgements, not neutral labels (the "enlightened despots" of the 18th century are a counter-example). Use "absolute monarch" in answers and "tyrant" only when describing a particular ruler's conduct.

5. Development 4: The Renaissance (14th-17th centuries)

  • Time: 14th-17th centuries. It started in Florence, Italy (Map 2, place 3).
  • Europeans developed a sudden interest in ancient Greek and Roman literature - Homer, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Ptolemy, Virgil, Cicero.
  • The ancient knowledge and wisdom awakened curiosity, liberated minds and infused rationalism, leading to a cultural explosion.
  • It led to the birth of humanism, the development of vernacular literature and the rise of national languages.
  • New art forms - Leonardo da Vinci, Raphael - and a revival of Greco-Roman architecture.
  • Many inventions took place - the compass, the printing press, the telescope (c11).
  • Birth of Protestantism and the religious Reformation (c12). Protestants encouraged modern education and contributed to the growth of modern science and technology, and also to capitalism (see the question below).
  • The Scientific Revolution in Europe in the 16th and 17th centuries led to modern science and astronomy.

[Additional] Why Florence, why Italy? Italian cities (Florence, Venice, Genoa) were rich from the Mediterranean trade that the Crusades had expanded (3); wealthy bankers such as the Medici family of Florence paid for art and scholarship †. Italy also had the ruins and manuscripts of Rome, and after the fall of Constantinople in 1453 Greek scholars moved to Italy (Britannica: the exodus of Greeks to Italy is seen by many scholars as marking the end of the Middle Ages and the start of the Renaissance). Humanism put the study of human nature, ethics and the classics beside theology. The poets Dante (writing in Italian) and Petrarch are early figures †; Michelangelo is the third great Italian artist usually named with Leonardo and Raphael †. Socrates wrote nothing; we know him through Plato and Xenophon. Much classical Greek learning also reached Europe through Arab translators.

5.2 Reformation and Protestantism

  • Martin Luther's protest against the Church (the Ninety-Five Theses, Wittenberg, 31 October 1517 †) began the Reformation. The name "Protestant" first appeared at the Diet of Speyer in 1529, when a protest was read on 19 April 1529 by six Lutheran princes and 14 free cities of Germany against Charles V's cancelling of the 1526 provision that let each ruler decide whether to enforce the ban on Luther's writings (Britannica, Protestantism).
  • Britannica adds that the Reformation took place against the background of a "rich ferment" in the late medieval church (critics such as Wycliffe, Hus and the Christian humanist Erasmus), and that Europe was not simply "ripe" for reform.

[Correction] "Birth of protestantism - led to religious reformation." The cause and effect are reversed: the Reformation (a movement from 1517) produced Protestantism, and the name "Protestant" dates from 1529. Renaissance humanism (return to the original texts, criticism of Church abuses) helped prepare the Reformation.

[Additional] Question in the class notes: How did Protestantism lead to capitalism? (a contested thesis, not a settled fact)

  • Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904-05): Weber noticed that Protestants were prominent in capitalist ventures in Germany. He argued that Calvin's doctrine of predestination (God has already chosen the saved, and nobody can know who they are) created anxiety; believers looked for signs of grace in success in a worldly "calling", so they worked hard without pause and practised ascetic restraint (no luxury spending). The practical result was the rapid accumulation of capital (Britannica).
  • R. H. Tawney, Religion and the Rise of Capitalism (1926) accepted the general idea but stressed the ethic of hard work and individualism more than theology (Britannica).
  • Critics: highly developed capitalism existed centuries before Calvin (Italian banking, section 5); Swedish historian Kurt Samuelsson (Religion and Economic Action, 1957) attacked the thesis. Weber himself accepted that other material and psychological conditions also mattered (Britannica).
  • Answer in one line: Protestantism did not "create" capitalism; it supplied a work-and-thrift ethic and weakened the Church's control over economic life, which, with trade, colonies and banking, helped it grow.

6. Development 5: Fall of Constantinople, 1453

  • The Ottomans controlled many places in the Middle East and the Balkans and badly wanted Constantinople: a Christian city, a centre of knowledge, and strategically located.
  • It gave control of the Bosporus and Dardanelles straits (Map 3).
  • The city was well protected ("7 layers of protection": c13).
  • Mehmed II conquered Constantinople in 1453.
  • The Ottoman conquest disrupted trade along the old routes, which forced Europeans to find alternate trade routes (c14).

  • Portugal and Spain were the leaders; Henry the Navigator promoted the early voyages (c15).

  • Inspired by this, Columbus reached the Bahamas in 1492 (c16).

[Additional] Why the voyages happened. Britannica's account of Columbus lists commercial, political, personal and religious motives: the search for a route to the spice and gold lands, the demand for spices (cooking, preservation, medicine), a wish to outflank the Muslim powers who held the land routes and the Red Sea, the rivalry between Spain and Portugal, and missionary zeal. [Correction] (a) "16-18th centuries." Henry sponsored earlier voyages down the African coast; his "school" at Sagres is a debated legend [Verification Required].

[Correction] "Columbus reached Bahamas in the Caribbean islands, 1492." The date is right: 12 October 1492, when land was sighted (the exact first landfall, Guanahani, is disputed; Britannica prefers San Salvador in the Bahamas). Geographically, the Bahamas lie in the Atlantic Ocean, north of the Caribbean Sea, though they are part of the West Indies. Columbus sailed for Spain, was born in Genoa, and believed he had reached Asia. Britannica also notes he is called the "discoverer" of the New World although Vikings had visited North America five centuries earlier, and that the older heroic view is now widely challenged.

[Correction] "Vasco da Gama reached koppad / kappad in Calicut on 20th May 1498." Britannica gives Calicut reached on 20 May 1498 after leaving Lisbon on 8 July 1497. Kappad (Kozhikode district, Kerala) is the traditional landing place and is widely stated in Indian textbooks [Verification Required] - it is not named in the Britannica page opened for this note. Calicut = Kozhikode; the ruler was the Zamorin (Samoothiri).

8. Development 7: The Commercial Revolution (16th-18th centuries)

  • The Age of Discovery led to long-distance, large-scale and direct sea trade.
  • It boosted European agriculture and industries and led to the expansion of trade and commerce: the Commercial Revolution; it led to world trade.
  • This trade was extremely profitable and extremely risky.
  • This led to a new breed of merchants, who created joint-stock companies - for example the British East India Company (Thomas Smyth) (c18).
  • Joint-stock companies led to stock markets; central banks and a money-based economy were created.
  • The new system was based on foreign trade and created by merchant capitalists (mercantilists); its philosophy is mercantilism (9).

(c) At first it was not a permanent joint stock: until 1612 it ran separate, separately subscribed voyages, and temporary joint stocks followed until a permanent joint stock in 1657 (Britannica). India link: a trading company became a ruler after Plassey (1757), and in 1773 the Boston Tea Party protested the company's tea monopoly (Britannica).

8.3 Question in the class notes: The Glorious Revolution of 1688

[Additional] What was the Glorious Revolution (1688-89)? (Britannica, Glorious Revolution and Bill of Rights)

  • What: the events of 1688-89 that deposed King James II and put his daughter Mary II and her husband William III of Orange (stadholder of the Dutch Republic) on the English throne. Also called the Bloodless Revolution.
  • Why: James II's open Roman Catholicism and his Declarations of Indulgence alienated most people; the birth of a Catholic son (June 1688) promised a Catholic line. Seven leading Englishmen of both Whig and Tory parties invited William with an army.
  • How: William landed at Brixham on Tor Bay on 5 November 1688 and advanced on London; support for James collapsed (including his daughter Anne and his general John Churchill) and James fled to France.
  • Settlement: the Convention Parliament (met 22 January 1689) treated the flight as abdication and offered the crown with a Declaration of Rights to William and Mary, which became the Bill of Rights: no suspending or dispensing with laws, no taxes or peacetime standing army without Parliament, free elections and free speech in Parliament, and no Roman Catholic monarch. The Toleration Act (1689) granted religious toleration to Protestant dissenters.
  • Result: Parliament became the ruling power and monarchy became conditional - the end of divine-right absolutism in England (4); Locke's social-contract ideas gained support.
  • Link to commerce (a widely held interpretation, not from the page opened): the settlement made royal power predictable and property secure, and was followed by the Bank of England (1694) and a funded national debt, which helped England finance its wars and trade †.
  • Caution: "bloodless" applies mainly to England; there was fighting in Ireland and Scotland (1689-91) †.

9. 7.A Mercantilism

  • Mercantilists believed that the world's wealth is static (a fixed stock that one country gains only at the expense of another).
  • Mercantilism focuses on moving goods from a cheaper market to a costlier market to make profit rather than on changing the mode of production.
  • Mercantilist countries: Britain and France (Map 6).
  • They tried to maximise exports and minimise imports (c19) so as to accumulate wealth in the form of gold and silver; hence mercantilism is sometimes called bullionism (c20).
  • Mercantilism created competition and enmity among the mercantile powers of Europe, which fed nationalist sentiment and the European wars of the 17th and 18th centuries, e.g. the Anglo-Dutch and Anglo-French wars.
  • Gradually mercantilism replaced feudalism as the new economic system and eventually became the precursor of modern capitalism.
  • Mercantilism was also the driving force behind imperialism and colonialism.

[Additional] What Britannica says. Mercantilism was the economic theory and practice common in Europe from the 16th to the 18th century, promoting government regulation of a nation's economy to augment state power at the expense of rivals; it was the economic counterpart of political absolutism (4). Its 17th-century publicists - Thomas Mun (England), Jean-Baptiste Colbert (France) and Antonio Serra (Italy) - never used the term: it was popularised by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776). Principles: precious metals are indispensable; trade balances must be "favourable" (exports above imports); colonies should be markets and suppliers of raw materials, manufacturing in colonies was forbidden and colonial commerce was a monopoly of the mother country; a large population gives labour, markets and soldiers; thrift is a virtue. Mercantilism "provided the favourable climate for the early development of capitalism". Critics (laissez-faire thinkers) argued that trade benefits both sides and that wealth is not fixed.

[Additional] India link. Britannica on the East India Company: its mercantile policies and concessions "gradually destroyed Indigenous crafts and industries, such as textile manufacturing", reduced India to a supplier of raw materials and a consumer of imported goods, and the Company's revenue policy is regarded as a direct cause of the Bengal famine of 1770. Historiography: the class view that mercantilism earned profit by trading rather than by changing production matches the Marxist idea of "merchant capital"; later industrial capitalism changed production itself (to be studied with the Industrial Revolution).

[Correction] "Tried to maximise exports and minimise wealth." It should read maximise exports and minimise imports: the aim was a favourable balance of trade and the accumulation of wealth (Britannica). "Minimise wealth" would contradict the theory.

[Correction] "Hence mercantilism also called bullionism." Bullionism is the idea that a country's wealth is the stock of gold and silver, and is an early and narrower form of mercantilism (associated with Spain). Mercantilism is wider: it also stresses a favourable trade balance, manufactures, shipping, colonies and state regulation. They overlap but are not the same word; treat "bullionism" as a part of mercantilism.

  • Feudal pyramid: King → great lords → knights → peasants (land down; service, loyalty and taxes up). The Church was a separate parallel hierarchy.
  • Catholic (Pope, Rome) ≠ Eastern Orthodox (Constantinople); schism 1054.
  • Crusades 1095-1291; Council of Clermont (Urban II) 18 Nov 1095; Jerusalem 1099 †; Acre falls 1291.

  • Strong monarchies: gunpowder, cannon, standing armies; divine right of kings.
  • Renaissance started in Florence (14th century); humanism; vernacular; Leonardo, Raphael; printing press c. 1450 †; telescope 1608-09 †; compass and gunpowder came from China.
  • Reformation 1517 (Luther) †; "Protestant" from 1529; Weber, Protestant Ethic (1904-05).

Mains Points

  • Decline of feudalism: combine causes - the Crusades and trade (new money economy), urbanisation and labour shortage, gunpowder and standing armies, stronger monarchs and taxes. Give a cause chain, not a list.
  • Crusades: religious and political aims; four effects (economic, political, cultural, religious) - note both the failure to hold the Holy Land and the long-term gains of Italian trade.
  • Renaissance and Reformation: humanism and printing as the new ideas; Reformation as a religious break; Weber vs Tawney vs critics for the link to capitalism.
  • Fall of Constantinople: a symbolic end to the Middle Ages and a spur (not the sole cause) to ocean exploration; Greek scholars to Italy.
  • Age of Discovery: motives (God, gold, glory), enablers (ship design, navigation, gunpowder), consequences for Europe, Africa, Asia and the Americas; shift from Mediterranean to Atlantic.
  • Commercial Revolution and mercantilism: joint-stock companies, banks, colonies; the link to imperialism, the American Revolution and the Industrial Revolution; the India link (East India Company, Plassey, de-industrialisation).
  • Glorious Revolution: constitutional monarchy and secure property as conditions for commerce.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • World history ≠ European history (c01).
  • CE ≠ BCE: Islam's rise is 7th century CE (c07).
  • Catholic ≠ Orthodox (c05).
  • Reformation → Protestantism (not the reverse) (c12).
  • Dias (Cape, 1488) ≠ da Gama (India, 1498) ≠ Columbus (Bahamas, 1492); Henry the Navigator did not reach the Cape (c15).
  • Bahamas are in the Atlantic, not the Caribbean Sea (c16); Columbus was Genoese but sailed for Spain.
  • Compass and gunpowder are Chinese in origin (c11).
  • Mercantilists want to minimise imports, not wealth (c19); bullionism is a narrower idea (c20).
  • England ≠ Britain ≠ UK (8.2); the English (not British) East India Company in 1600 (c18).
  • Constantinople (Byzantine capital, fell 1453) vs Edirne (Ottoman capital before 1453).
  • Not every absolute monarch was a tyrant (c10).

30-Second Revision

Medieval Europe's Old Order was a feudal pyramid (king, nobles, knights, peasants) with a powerful Catholic Church and a local, agrarian, guild economy; only spices and silks came on long Silk Route and maritime routes, with Muslim and Italian middlemen. Seven developments ended it: the Crusades (1095-1291) opened trade and weakened feudalism; gunpowder and standing armies strengthened monarchs (divine right); the Renaissance (Florence, humanism, printing) and the Reformation and Scientific Revolution changed ideas; the fall of Constantinople (29 May 1453) spurred the search for sea routes; the Age of Discovery (Dias 1488, Columbus 1492, da Gama 1498) turned Mediterranean trade into oceanic trade; the Commercial Revolution created joint-stock companies, stock exchanges and banks; and mercantilism (exports over imports, bullion, colonies) drove rivalry, wars, imperialism and colonialism - the road to the East India Company and Plassey.
