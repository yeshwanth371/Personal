16. World War II (1939-1945) - continued: outbreak, course, victory and impact

The war began as a European war over Poland in September 1939, became a war of German conquest in 1940-41, turned into a true world war when Germany invaded the USSR (June 1941) and Japan attacked the USA (December 1941), and ended with Germany's surrender in May 1945 and Japan's in August-September 1945. The sub-sections follow the order of the class notes.

16.5 From the Nazi-Soviet Pact to war (August-September 1939)

  • The pact (recap): by the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact of 23 August 1939, the USSR and Germany openly agreed to mutual non-aggression, but in a secret protocol they decided to divide Poland between them (details: 19 November note, 16.3.8).
  • The USSR was also given a free hand in the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, later Lithuania), Finland and Bessarabia. In return Hitler got a free hand in the west: he could attack Poland and then turn on Britain and France without fearing a two-front war.

[Correction] The secret protocol did not "allow the USSR to annex" the Baltic states and Finland; it placed them in the Soviet sphere of influence. Lithuania was first assigned to Germany and was moved into the Soviet sphere by the German-Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty of 28 September 1939 in exchange for more of central Poland †. What the USSR did with its sphere - bases, invasion, annexation - followed in 1939-40 (16.6).

Immediate cause of the war

Nazi-Soviet Pact, 23 Aug 1939 → Germany invades Poland, 1 Sep 1939 → Britain and France declare war, 3 Sep 1939 → USSR invades eastern Poland, 17 Sep 1939 → Poland partitioned (Warsaw falls 27-28 Sep)
  • After the non-aggression pact, Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and occupied its western part. Realising that Hitler would not stop with Poland (he had already broken the Munich promise by seizing Prague in March 1939), Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939 and World War II began.
  • The German method, Blitzkrieg ("lightning war"), combined tanks, motorised infantry and dive-bombers to break through and encircle the enemy quickly. Poland was overrun in about five weeks †.

[Correction] "Sept 19389" is a slip for September 1939.

How much of Finland was lost? The notes say 11%. The Imperial War Museums give 11 per cent; other accounts give about 9%. About 420,000 Finns (12% of the population) left the ceded areas. Finland was defeated but kept its independence, and the Red Army's poor performance (with Stalin's purges of the officer corps in the background) encouraged Hitler to underestimate it. What began on 22 June 1941 was its war against the Axis, on the Allied side.

16.7 The war in the west, 1939-1941: Blitzkrieg, the fall of France and the Battle of Britain

16.7.1 The "Phoney War" (September 1939 - April 1940)

Britain and France were at war with Germany but did almost nothing for about seven months while Poland was crushed: apart from a brief French probe into the Saar † and the war at sea, the armies sat behind the Maginot Line and threatened rather than attacked. The British called it the "Phoney War", the French the drôle de guerre, the Germans the Sitzkrieg ("sitting war").

16.7.2 Denmark and Norway (April 1940)

Hitler broke the silence. On 9 April 1940 Germany occupied Denmark (in a day) and invaded Norway (Norway held out until June 1940). Why: to secure the import of Swedish iron ore, shipped in winter from the ice-free Norwegian port of Narvik, and to gain naval and air bases on the Atlantic. The Norwegian fiasco brought down Chamberlain: Winston Churchill became British Prime Minister on 10 May 1940.

16.7.3 The Low Countries and the fall of France (10 May - 22 June 1940)

  • On 10 May 1940 Germany invaded the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg. The Allied armies moved north into Belgium to meet what they expected to be the main attack.
  • The real blow came simultaneously through the Ardennes forest, thought to be impassable for tanks: German panzers crossed it in about three days, reached the Meuse at Sedan on 13 May and raced to the Channel coast by 20 May, cutting the Allied armies in two. Troops were kept awake with the methamphetamine Pervitin, about 35 million tablets of which were ordered for the campaign - hence the notes' "super humans".
  • The Netherlands capitulated on 14-15 May (after the bombing of Rotterdam) and Belgium on 28 May 1940 †. Germany entered Paris on 14 June; France signed an armistice at Compiègne on 22 June 1940. Italy declared war on Britain and France on 10 June 1940.
  • Division of France: Germany occupied the north and the Atlantic coast; in the south an authoritarian, collaborationist French state under Marshal Pétain governed from Vichy ("Vichy France"). After the Allied landings in North Africa, Germany occupied the Vichy zone too (November 1942), establishing direct control. General de Gaulle led the "Free French" from London.

[Correction] The notes present the conquest of the Low Countries "by May 1940" as a preliminary "to facilitate" the attack on France. Both were one offensive (Fall Gelb, "Case Yellow") launched on the same day, 10 May 1940: the push into Belgium and the Netherlands drew the Allies north while the decisive thrust went through the Ardennes.

16.7.4 Dunkirk: Operation Dynamo (26 May - 4 June 1940)

British and French troops were pinned against the northern French coast around Lille and Dunkirk. A German "halt order" (24-26 May) gave them time. The new Prime Minister, Churchill, backed the Royal Navy's evacuation, Operation Dynamo, which with over 800 vessels - warships and the famous "little ships" - rescued about 3.4 lakh (338,226) troops, a third of them French. The army was saved but left almost all its tanks and heavy guns behind; Churchill called it "a miracle of deliverance" but warned that "wars are not won by evacuations".

[Correction] Operation Dynamo was organised by the Royal Navy and directed from Dover by Vice-Admiral Bertram Ramsay, with the War Office ordering the evacuation on 26 May; Churchill, PM since 10 May, did not personally "order" it. About 338,000 men were brought out, which matches the notes' 3.4 lakh.

16.7.5 Britain alone: peace offer, Operation Catapult and Operation Sea Lion

  • Hitler's two options for Churchill: make peace or face invasion. In his "appeal to reason" speech of 19 July 1940 Hitler offered peace; Britain rejected it †.
  • Operation Catapult (3 July 1940): the British attacked the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir (near Oran, French Algeria) and seized French ships in British ports, so that the navy of defeated France could not fall into German hands and be used against Britain. About 1,300 French sailors were killed †, which poisoned Anglo-Vichy relations.
  • Operation Sea Lion (Seelöwe): Hitler ordered preparations for an invasion of southern England on 16 July 1940, on condition that the Luftwaffe first won air superiority over the Channel.

[Correction] Operation Sea Lion was never launched. It was a plan (Hitler's directive of 16 July 1940) that depended on defeating the RAF; when that failed it was postponed indefinitely on 17 September 1940 † and finally dropped as Hitler turned east.

[Correction] The order of events is reversed in the notes. Operation Catapult (3 July 1940) came before the Sea Lion directive (16 July) and was a response to the French armistice of 22 June, not to Sea Lion: Britain feared that the French fleet, the fourth largest in the world, would end up under German or Italian control.

16.7.6 The Battle of Britain (10 July - 31 October 1940) and the Blitz (September 1940 - May 1941)

  • Hitler ordered the Luftwaffe (Hermann Göring) to destroy the RAF and win control of the air. The Battle of Britain was the first major military campaign fought entirely by air forces.
  • Britain's advantages: the Chain Home radar network linked to the Dowding system of fighter control (early warning, so fighters met raids in time); fighting over home territory (downed RAF pilots could fly again, German crews were captured); the Hurricane and Spitfire and rising aircraft production; and some signals intelligence from the breaking of Enigma ("Ultra") at Bletchley Park.
  • The Luftwaffe suffered heavy losses: around 1,733 German aircraft were destroyed against 915 British, according to the Imperial War Museums. Germany's failure was its first major defeat of the war.
  • From 7 September 1940 Hitler changed strategy to bombing British cities, industries and infrastructure - the Blitz (7 September 1940 - 11 May 1941) - to break civilian morale; London, Coventry and other cities were hit and over 40,000 civilians were killed †, but Britain fought on. With the RAF undefeated, Hitler abandoned Sea Lion.

[Correction] "First aerial war" overstates it: air combat and bombing of cities already occurred in World War I. The Battle of Britain was the first major campaign fought entirely in the air.

[Correction] Two of the "advantages" need fixing. (1) "Experience of the RAF": the Luftwaffe was the more experienced force (Spain, Poland, France); the RAF's edge was radar plus the Dowding control system, home ground and production. (2) The Lorenz cipher (German high command teleprinter traffic, "Tunny") was not broken until 1942 †, so it played no part in 1940; Enigma intelligence helped only partly in the Battle of Britain. Both became important Allied advantages later in the war (16.13).

[Correction] "Luftwaffe: 50% destroyed" could not be confirmed as a percentage. The Imperial War Museums give about 1,733 German and 915 British aircraft destroyed (other counts: 1,887 against 1,023-1,547, depending on what is counted); Germany began with about 2,550 aircraft. Use the absolute numbers, not a percentage.

16.7.7 The Tripartite Pact, the Mediterranean and the Balkans (1940-1941)

  • The Tripartite Pact (Rome-Berlin-Tokyo Axis) was signed on 27 September 1940: mutual assistance if any of the three was attacked by a power not yet at war - aimed at the USA (Table 16.1).
  • Each partner was fighting its own war: Italy invaded Egypt from Libya (September 1940) and Greece from Albania (28 October 1940) and was beaten back in both; Germany fought Britain; Japan was in the Second Sino-Japanese War.
  • Hitler was forced to rescue Mussolini. In North Africa, General Erwin Rommel's Afrika Korps (from February 1941) drove the British back towards Egypt. In the Balkans, Germany conquered Yugoslavia and Greece in April 1941 (Crete by airborne assault in May 1941) †. The Axis now dominated the Balkans and the eastern Mediterranean.
  • Hungary, Romania, Slovakia and Bulgaria joined the Tripartite Pact, so the Axis controlled western, central and south-eastern Europe by mid-1941.

[Correction] Dates and members: Hungary (20 November 1940), Romania (23 November 1940), Slovakia (24 November 1940) and Bulgaria (1 March 1941) joined the Tripartite Pact †; Yugoslavia signed on 25 March 1941 but a coup two days later led Hitler to invade it. Finland never joined the Axis: it fought the USSR alongside Germany from June 1941 (the "Continuation War") as a co-belligerent, to recover the land lost in 1940.

16.8 Operation Barbarossa and the Eastern Front (1941-1945)

  • Invasion: violating the non-aggression pact, Hitler invaded the USSR on 22 June 1941 - Operation Barbarossa, with more than 3.8 million Axis troops on a 2,900 km front in three army groups (North towards Leningrad, Centre towards Moscow, South towards Kiev and the Caucasus). Aims: destroy "Judeo-Bolshevism", win Lebensraum and the grain of Ukraine and the oil of the Caucasus. The USSR was forced into the war; the Soviets call 1941-45 the Great Patriotic War.
  • Britain and the USSR: Britain and the USSR signed an agreement for joint action on 12 July 1941 and a twenty-year alliance in May 1942. Stalin immediately asked Churchill to open a second front in the west to draw German divisions away. Churchill refused and delayed (Britain was too weak; the Western Allies chose North Africa and Italy first), and the second front came only on 6 June 1944. Stalin became suspicious that the West wanted Germany and the USSR to bleed each other - a root of the Cold War (17.4).
  • Scorched earth: as the Red Army retreated, Stalin (radio speech of 3 July 1941 †) ordered everything useful to the enemy - crops, factories, railways, bridges - to be destroyed or evacuated; about 1,500 factories were moved east to the Urals †. Even so, Germany overran about 1.6 million sq km of Soviet territory by December 1941 and captured millions of prisoners.
  • Siege of Leningrad (St Petersburg), 8 September 1941 - 27 January 1944: about 872 days; hundreds of thousands of civilians starved †; the city never fell.
  • Battle of Moscow (October 1941 - January 1942): the Germans reached the outskirts; the Soviet counter-offensive of 5-6 December 1941 (Zhukov, fresh Siberian divisions, a ferocious winter) drove them back - the USSR's first major victory and the failure of Blitzkrieg in the east.
  • Battle of Stalingrad (Volgograd), August 1942 - 2 February 1943: Hitler's 1942 offensive aimed at the Caucasus oilfields and at Stalingrad, an industrial city on the Volga bearing Stalin's name. The German Sixth Army was encircled (Operation Uranus, November 1942) and surrendered (Paulus, 31 January - 2 February 1943). Stalingrad is the turning point on the Eastern Front; after it the USSR took the offensive.
  • Soviet offensive, 1943-45: Kursk (July 1943, the largest tank battle) ended Germany's last major offensive in the east; Operation Bagration (June-August 1944) destroyed Army Group Centre; the Red Army freed eastern Europe from the Axis (1943-45) and took Berlin (2 May 1945).

[Correction] There was no "Treaty of Friendship and Alliance, 1941". Britain and the USSR signed the Anglo-Soviet Agreement of 12 July 1941 (mutual help, no separate peace) and the Anglo-Soviet Treaty of 26 May 1942 (a twenty-year alliance) †. The 1941 agreement fits the notes' context (Stalin's immediate demand for a second front).

[Correction] The notes link the scorched-earth policy to Germany's conquest of "8 million sq km in 6 months". Two problems: (1) scorched earth was the Soviet response meant to deny resources to the invader, not the cause of German gains; (2) the area is far too large - the whole European USSR is only about 5 million sq km. Germany and its allies captured about 1.6 million sq km (600,000 sq miles) by the end of Barbarossa (5 December 1941), and somewhat more by late 1942.

Moscow vs Stalingrad: Moscow (December 1941) was the first major Soviet victory - it stopped Blitzkrieg; Stalingrad (February 1943) was the decisive turning point - it destroyed a whole German army and began the Soviet advance. Both statements in the notes are right in this sense.

16.9 The USA: from isolation and neutrality to war (1935-1941)

  • Isolationism: after World War I the USA followed a policy of isolation and neutrality. Congress passed Neutrality Acts banning the sale of arms and loans to countries at war. The President was Franklin D. Roosevelt (in office 4 March 1933 - died 12 April 1945; the only President elected four times).
  • Roosevelt saw Nazi Germany as a threat and gradually moved away from strict neutrality, with two main plans to help Britain:
    1. Cash and carry (1939-41): under the Neutrality Act of November 1939, belligerents could buy US arms if they paid cash and carried them in their own ships - in practice only Britain and France, which controlled the seas.
    2. Lend-Lease Act (11 March 1941): when Britain ran out of money, the USA lent or leased war materials to countries whose defence it considered vital - Britain, then China and (from late 1941) the USSR. Roosevelt called the USA the "arsenal of democracy" (December 1940) †.
  • Earlier, under the destroyers-for-bases deal (2 September 1940), the USA gave Britain 50 old destroyers in exchange for leases on British bases in the Atlantic † [Additional].

[Correction] The Neutrality Acts were passed in 1935, 1936, 1937 and 1939, not only 1937-39. The 1937 Act introduced cash and carry for non-military goods; the Neutrality Act of 4 November 1939 extended cash and carry to arms, which is the "cash and carry" of the notes †.

16.9.1 The Atlantic Charter (14 August 1941)

  • Who / where / when: President Roosevelt and Prime Minister Churchill met on warships in Placentia Bay, Newfoundland (9-12 August 1941) and issued a joint declaration of common principles for "a better future for the world", published on 14 August 1941. It was a statement of aims, not a treaty; the USA was not yet at war.
  • Eight points (summary): (1) no territorial gains sought; (2) no territorial changes against the wishes of the peoples concerned; (3) the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live, and restoration of self-government to those forcibly deprived of it; (4) equal access to trade and raw materials; (5) economic co-operation and better labour standards; (6) a peace "after the final destruction of the Nazi tyranny" giving freedom from fear and want; (7) freedom of the seas; (8) abandonment of force and disarmament of aggressors pending a wider system of security †.
  • So it supported free trade and self-government and opposed dictatorship and aggression. The USSR also endorsed the Charter at an Inter-Allied meeting in London in September 1941 †, and its principles were written into the Declaration by United Nations (1 January 1942) - the seed of the UN.
  • It was an important factor leading to decolonisation: colonial peoples read point 3 as a promise to them.

[Correction] The Charter did not explicitly oppose colonialism. Point 3 spoke of "all peoples", and on 9 September 1941 Churchill told the House of Commons that the declaration did not qualify British policy on "the development of constitutional government in India, Burma or other parts of the British Empire": what had been "primarily in mind" was the restoration of sovereignty to European nations "under the Nazi yoke". Its anti-colonial force came from how colonial peoples - and many Americans - interpreted it.

[Additional] Importance of the Atlantic Charter and its impact on the Indian freedom movement (requested in the notes)
  • Raised hopes: Indian nationalists asked whether point 3 applied to India; in the Commons on 9 September 1941 an MP asked Churchill to extend clause 3 to India.
  • Churchill's exclusion (9 September 1941) caused deep resentment. Even the official Council of State in Delhi passed a resolution (about 18 November 1941) asking the Viceroy to convey its discontent, warning that the statement would harm India's war effort (US State Department records). The Burmese Premier U Saw, refused the same assurance in London, was publicly disappointed.
  • American pressure: US officials feared that Indian non-co-operation would weaken the war effort; the State Department discussed urging dominion status for India, and Roosevelt pressed Churchill privately †. This pressure, the fall of Singapore and Rangoon and the Japanese threat to India led Britain to send the Cripps Mission (March 1942); its failure was followed by the Quit India Movement (August 1942) †.
  • Exposed a double standard: Britain was fighting for freedom in Europe while denying it in Asia - a central argument of Gandhi and Nehru. The principle of self-determination later entered the UN Charter (1945), which India signed as an original member in 1945 even before independence †.
  • Exam point: the Charter did not free India, but it changed the moral and diplomatic climate: it made colonial rule look inconsistent with the Allies' stated aims and gave the USA a voice on India. (Indian Modern History details: Cripps, Quit India - see those notes.)

16.9.2 The undeclared naval war in the Atlantic (September-December 1941)

German U-boats attacked American warships escorting convoys: the destroyer USS Greer was attacked (unhurt) on 4 September 1941, after which Roosevelt ordered US ships to "shoot on sight"; USS Kearny was torpedoed on 17 October and USS Reuben James sunk on 31 October 1941 †. From autumn 1941 there was an undeclared naval war between Germany and the USA in the Atlantic.

16.10 The rise of Japan and the war in Asia and the Pacific (1937-1945)

16.10.1 Japan's road to Pearl Harbor

  • Dependence: Japan, poor in raw materials, imported most of its oil - commonly put at about 80% from the USA † - as well as scrap iron and steel.
  • Expansion: Japan dominated the western Pacific; it occupied Manchuria and set up Manchukuo (1932), then invaded China in July 1937, starting the Second Sino-Japanese War (19 November note, 15.11). Its next target was South-East Asia, rich in oil, rubber and tin.
  • The USA helped China with loans and, from 1941, Lend-Lease supplies.

[Verification Required] The notes' figure of "2 billion dollars" of US aid to China could not be confirmed for the period before Pearl Harbor; US credits to China before 1941 were far smaller, and the large Lend-Lease totals refer to the whole war. Treat the figure as unverified.

The USA (with Britain and the Netherlands) froze Japanese assets (26 July 1941) and imposed an oil embargo (1 August 1941).   • Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941): aircraft from six Japanese carriers (Admiral Yamamoto's plan) struck the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. 19 US ships, including 8 battleships, were damaged or destroyed and 2,403 Americans were killed (2,008 of them Navy personnel and 68 civilians). The US carriers were at sea, and the fuel tanks and repair yards were spared.

[Correction] Japan did not "conquer" Indochina in 1941. It occupied it in two stages - the north in September 1940 and the south in July 1941 - under agreements forced on the Vichy French authorities, who kept a nominal administration until the Japanese coup of March 1945 †.

[Correction] The notes say the oil embargo "angered the US". It was the other way round: Japan's occupation of southern Indochina (July 1941) led the USA to freeze Japanese assets and stop oil exports, and the embargo angered Japan, which decided to seize the oil of the Dutch East Indies and to knock out the US fleet first.

[Correction] "19 US vessels destroyed, 2,300 US sailors": the 19 ships were damaged or destroyed - most were later raised and repaired; only the battleships Arizona and Oklahoma (and the old target ship Utah) were total losses †. The dead numbered 2,403, including 2,008 Navy personnel, 218 Army, 109 Marines and 68 civilians (National WWII Museum).

16.10.2 Japanese conquest and "Asia for the Asians" (December 1941 - mid-1942)

  • While the USA rebuilt its Pacific fleet, Japan conquered almost the whole of South-East Asia in 1941-42: Hong Kong, Malaya and Singapore (15 February 1942, the largest British surrender in history †), the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies and Burma, reaching the borders of India and the waters off Australia.
  • Japan proclaimed the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere - "Asia for the Asians" - claiming to free Asia from Western rule. In practice its rule was harsh and exploitative, but the speed of Western defeat destroyed the myth of European invincibility, armed and organised nationalists (Burma, Indonesia, the Indian National Army under Subhas Chandra Bose) and so hastened decolonisation after 1945.
  • India link: the Japanese occupied the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (1942-45); Bose hoisted the national flag at Port Blair on 30 December 1943 †. The Japanese-INA offensive into India was defeated at Imphal and Kohima (March-July 1944), largely by Indian divisions of the British Fourteenth Army.

16.10.3 Midway and the Allied recovery (June 1942 - 1945)

  • Battle of the Coral Sea (4-8 May 1942): the first carrier battle in history; it stopped the Japanese move on Port Moresby (New Guinea).
  • Battle of Midway (4-7 June 1942) - "Japan's doom": warned by US codebreakers, American carrier aircraft sank all four Japanese fleet carriers (Akagi, Kaga, Soryu, Hiryu) for the loss of the USS Yorktown. Japan lost several hundred aircraft and many of its best pilots and lost control of the Pacific; it was on the defensive from then on.
  • Recovery (1942-45): the Americans struck back at Guadalcanal (Solomon Islands, August 1942 - February 1943), then advanced by "island hopping": General Douglas MacArthur through New Guinea to the Philippines (Leyte, October 1944), Admiral Chester Nimitz across the central Pacific (Gilberts, Marshalls, Marianas, Iwo Jima and Okinawa, 1945). In Burma, Lord Louis Mountbatten (South-East Asia Command) and General William Slim (Fourteenth Army) reconquered Burma (Rangoon, May 1945).
  • By mid-1945 Japan had been pushed back towards its home islands, but it still held Korea, Manchuria, much of China and large parts of South-East Asia.

[Correction] The four carriers are right. For aircraft, sources differ: Britannica gives about 320 Japanese planes lost, other accounts nearly 250; the notes' "332" is not supported by the sources checked. About 3,000 Japanese and 300-360 Americans were killed.

[Correction] "USA's military experts MacArthur + Mountbatten": MacArthur (US Army) commanded the South-West Pacific and Nimitz (US Navy) the central Pacific; Mountbatten was British, Supreme Allied Commander of South-East Asia Command (from late 1943), responsible for Burma and South-East Asia, not an American commander.

[Correction] "In 1945 Japan retreated to its home islands but controlled Korea and Manchuria" is incomplete. At the surrender in August 1945 Japan still held Korea, Manchuria, large parts of eastern China, Indochina (with Thailand as an ally), Malaya, Singapore, most of the Dutch East Indies and many Pacific islands; it had lost the Philippines (largely), Burma, the Marianas, Iwo Jima and Okinawa.

16.11 The war in Europe after the USA's entry (1942-1945)

  • El Alamein (second battle, 23 October - 11 November 1942): the British Eighth Army under Bernard Montgomery, including Indian, Australian, New Zealand and South African troops, defeated Rommel at El Alamein in Egypt and drove the Axis out of Egypt and Libya. Churchill: "Before Alamein we never had a victory. After Alamein we never had a defeat" †.
  • Operation Torch (8 November 1942): Anglo-American forces under General Dwight Eisenhower landed in Morocco and Algeria (Casablanca, Oran, Algiers). Caught between Torch and the Eighth Army, the Axis forces in Africa surrendered in Tunisia on 13 May 1943. Germany occupied Vichy France in reply (November 1942).
  • Stalingrad (2 February 1943): the USSR's decisive victory; it went on the offensive (16.8).
  • Operation Husky (10 July 1943): British and American forces invaded Sicily, and then landed in southern mainland Italy (Calabria and Salerno, September 1943).
  • Fall of Mussolini: after the Fascist Grand Council turned against him, King Victor Emmanuel III dismissed and arrested Mussolini (25 July 1943). The new government under Marshal Badoglio signed an armistice (signed 3 September, announced 8 September 1943). Germany occupied northern and central Italy; German commandos rescued Mussolini (12 September 1943) from the Gran Sasso, and he headed a puppet Italian Social Republic at Salò in the German-held north. Rome was liberated on 4 June 1944.
  • The Big Three and the Tehran Conference (28 November - 1 December 1943): Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin met for the first time and planned their war strategy - above all, coordinated attacks on Axis Europe: an Anglo-American invasion of France in 1944 together with a Soviet offensive in the east †. This led to Operation Overlord.
  • D-Day and Operation Overlord (6 June 1944): under Eisenhower's supreme command, the Western Allies landed on the beaches of Normandy, broke through the German defences and liberated France (Paris, 25 August 1944). A second landing in southern France followed (Operation Dragoon, 15 August 1944). Britain, the USA, Canada and the Free French sent some 2-3 million soldiers into western Europe, while the Red Army with about 6 million men advanced through eastern Europe; one by one they recovered Europe from the Axis.
  • Yalta Conference (4-11 February 1945): the Big Three agreed on the occupation of Germany, the founding conference of the United Nations and Soviet entry into the war against Japan within three months of Germany's defeat †.
  • End in Europe: on 28 April 1945 Mussolini was shot by Italian partisans; Hitler committed suicide on 30 April 1945 in his Berlin bunker; Berlin fell on 2 May. Germany surrendered unconditionally at Reims on 7 May 1945, effective 8 May (V-E Day); a second signing in Berlin took place on 8 May (9 May Moscow time, Victory Day in Russia).
  • Germany divided: Germany (and Berlin) was divided into four Allied occupation zones - American, British, French and Soviet - and its lands east of the Oder-Neisse line were placed under Polish (and in northern East Prussia, Soviet) administration; this was confirmed at the Potsdam Conference (17 July - 2 August 1945).

16.12 The end of the war in Asia (July - September 1945)

  • The Allies demanded the unconditional surrender of Japan (Potsdam Declaration, 26 July 1945). Japan's leaders refused, chiefly because it gave no guarantee for the Emperor and was seen as humiliating.
  • This forced the USA to prepare an invasion of Japan (Operation Downfall), which planners expected to cost very heavy casualties - estimates ran from hundreds of thousands to millions of lives †.
  • Atomic bombs: the USA dropped "Little Boy" on Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and "Fat Man" on Nagasaki (9 August 1945).
  • Soviet entry: in between, the USSR declared war on Japan on 8 August 1945 and invaded Manchuria (and then northern Korea, southern Sakhalin and the Kurils) on 9 August, as promised at Yalta.
  • Surrender: Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's surrender on 15 August 1945 (V-J Day in Britain); the formal instrument was signed aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay on 2 September 1945, officially ending World War II.

[Correction] "USA wanted revenge for Pearl Harbor and nuked Hiroshima and Nagasaki" is not the official or the accepted explanation. The US government's stated aim was to force a quick surrender and avoid a costly invasion. Historians debate other motives: the wish to end the war before the USSR could share in the Pacific settlement and to impress the USSR ("atomic diplomacy", G. Alperovitz), the momentum of the Manhattan Project, and racial attitudes and wartime anger. Separate the official position from these interpretations.

[Correction] On the "violation" of the neutrality pact: the USSR had formally denounced (given notice not to renew) the Soviet-Japanese Neutrality Pact on 5 April 1945 †, though by its terms it ran until April 1946, so the attack of 8-9 August 1945 still broke it. Stalin acted on his Yalta promise to enter the war within three months of Germany's defeat.

16.13 Causes of victory for the Allied powers

16.13.1 Allied strengths

  1. World sympathy: the Allies were seen as the saviours of democracy against aggression and racism.
  2. Extensive resources: the combined manpower, industry and raw materials of the USA, USSR and the British Empire; US production and Lend-Lease.
  3. India's contribution: the Indian Army grew from under 200,000 men in 1939 to about 2.5 million by August 1945 - the largest volunteer army in history - and fought in East and North Africa, Italy and above all Burma; over 87,000 Indian servicemen died. India also supplied food, textiles, coal, iron and steel, war loans and taxes.
  4. Unified command: the Combined Chiefs of Staff, Eisenhower's Supreme Headquarters (SHAEF) and regular Big Three conferences.
  5. Naval superiority (convoys, carriers) and, increasingly, air superiority.
  6. Advanced radar.
  7. Codebreaking: knowledge of the Enigma and Lorenz ciphers (Ultra), and of Japanese naval codes (Midway).
  8. Nuclear technology of the USA (the Manhattan Project).
  9. The USA's entry strengthened the Allies decisively and turned the war in their favour.

[Verification Required] The notes' further Indian figures - "14 million civilians, 35% of cotton production, 197 million tonnes of coal, 6 million tonnes of iron ore, 1.2 million tons of iron and steel" - could not be verified in this session and are not repeated as facts. The 2.5 million soldiers figure is confirmed by the Imperial War Museums.

16.13.2 Weaknesses of the Axis powers

  1. Geographical separation: Germany and Italy in Europe and Japan in Asia could not supply or reinforce each other; joint logistics were impossible.
  2. Coordination was difficult - there was no common war plan.
  3. Lack of basic resources: iron, petroleum, rubber.

16.13.3 Strategic mistakes of the Axis

  • Lack of a unified command.
  • Ideological extremism and violence in conquered territories (the Holocaust, mass killing and enslavement in the East, Japanese atrocities), which turned potential collaborators into resisters.
  • Over-extension of resources: too many fronts at once.
  • Hitler prioritised air power over naval power (and Germany never built enough U-boats early on or a real surface fleet).
  • Abandoning Operation Sea Lion (leaving Britain undefeated as a base for the later invasion of Europe).
  • Underestimating the USSR; the over-ambitious Operation Barbarossa (and Hitler's refusal to let the Stalingrad army retreat).
  • Germany declaring war on the USA (11 December 1941).
  • Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor, which brought in the USA, and its strategy at Midway, which backfired.
  • Lack of advanced radar.

16.14 Impact of the Second World War (from the class handout)

The Second World War (1939-1945) had far-reaching effects that reshaped the world. Its aftermath saw political realignment, economic reconstruction and profound social change.

16.14.1 Political impact

  1. Birth of nuclear warfare: the use of atomic bombs on Japan (Hiroshima, 6 August; Nagasaki, 9 August 1945) began the nuclear age. The USSR tested its first bomb in August 1949 †, starting the nuclear arms race.
  2. Emergence of superpowers: the war catalysed the rise of the USA and the USSR as the new superpowers of the world.
  3. Beginning of the Cold War: the ideological conflicts and historical complications between the two superpowers resulted in the Cold War, a bipolar world and bipolar politics (section 17).
  4. Decolonisation: intense national sentiment aroused by the war, and developments like the Atlantic Charter (1941), created a strong anti-imperial attitude that led to the decolonisation of the Third World - beginning with the Philippines (1946), India and Pakistan (1947), Burma and Ceylon (Sri Lanka) (1948) and Indonesia (1949) †.
  5. European domination of the world ended: the war gravely weakened the European powers (Britain, France, the Netherlands, Germany, Italy) that had dominated world politics for four centuries.
  6. Renewed international cooperation: the failure of the League of Nations led to the founding of the United Nations in 1945 (Charter signed at San Francisco on 26 June 1945, in force 24 October 1945 †) to prevent future conflicts and promote peace, setting the stage for the growth of international law and diplomacy.
    • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (10 December 1948) reflected a global consensus on human rights.
    • The UN Charter (1945) - with its principle of "equal rights and self-determination of peoples" and its chapters on non-self-governing and trust territories - and the UN Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (General Assembly Resolution 1514, 14 December 1960) † supported decolonisation.
  7. Peace treaties: the Paris Peace Conference met from 29 July to 15 October 1946 †. The Council of (Allied) Foreign Ministers (USA, USSR, Britain, France and, at first, China) negotiated and finalised the peace treaties with Italy, Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria and Finland, signed in Paris on 10 February 1947. No peace treaty with Germany was possible because the Allies quarrelled over it (a final settlement came only in 1990); Japan's was the Treaty of San Francisco (1951) †.
  8. Borders versus people: as the historian Tony Judt put it, at the end of World War I borders were invented and adjusted while people were on the whole left in place; after 1945 the opposite happened: "with one major exception boundaries stayed broadly intact and people were moved instead". The exception was Poland, shifted westwards (losing its east to the USSR and gaining German lands up to the Oder-Neisse line). Some 12-14 million Germans were expelled from eastern Europe †.

[Correction] The handout's sentence on boundaries is a paraphrase of Tony Judt, Postwar: A History of Europe Since 1945 (2005) and should be attributed as an interpretation. It also drops Judt's qualification "with one major exception": Poland's frontiers moved some 200 km west, Germany lost about a quarter of its 1937 territory, and the USSR annexed the Baltic states, eastern Poland, part of East Prussia, Bessarabia and parts of Finland and Czechoslovakia (Ruthenia) †.

16.14.2 Economic impact

  • Economic losses: the war was far more devastating than World War I. The handout puts the economic losses at about US $1.3 trillion [Verification Required: estimates of the total cost of World War II vary widely with method and base year; the US$1.3 trillion figure could not be traced to an authoritative source in this session].
  • Reconstruction and trade institutions: the International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank) were agreed at the Bretton Woods Conference (July 1944), and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) was signed in 1947, for post-war economic reconstruction and to promote international trade.
  • Technological and industrial advances: the war accelerated innovations such as radar, jet engines and computers (Colossus at Bletchley Park, 1944; ENIAC in the USA, 1945 †), as well as rockets (the German V-2), nuclear energy, synthetic rubber and the mass production of penicillin [Additional]. These fuelled economic growth after the war, especially in manufacturing and technology.
  • [Additional] The USA ended the war as the world's leading creditor and industrial power; the dollar became the anchor of the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates (gold-dollar standard). American aid to Europe under the Marshall Plan (1948-52) belongs to the Cold War story of the next class.

[Correction] The IMF and World Bank were agreed at Bretton Woods in July 1944 (the IMF's Articles of Agreement were adopted on 22 July 1944), but they came into existence on 27 December 1945, when the Articles entered into force. GATT was signed on 30 October 1947 and applied from 1 January 1948 †.

16.14.3 Social impact

  • Destruction, devastation, despair: the handout gives 60-80 million people killed (about 3% of the world's population), 25 million wounded, 25 million homeless and 21 million forced to migrate; 6 million Jews were killed in the Holocaust; and about 3 million Indians starved to death in a man-made famine in eastern India (the Bengal famine of 1943). The war caused immense loss of life and displacement; after it, Europe saw large demographic shifts, including migration to North America and Australia.
  • Role of women: with men conscripted, women took on jobs traditionally reserved for men (factories, transport, auxiliary military services - "Rosie the Riveter" in the USA †), altering gender roles and perceptions. After the war this led to a re-evaluation of women's rights and their role in society and contributed to the feminist movements of the 1960s and beyond. (France and Italy gave women the vote in 1944-46 † [Additional].)

On the numbers: estimates of total deaths range widely (about 60-85 million), because civilian deaths in China, the USSR and occupied Europe are uncertain; "about 3% of the world's 1940 population" (about 2.3 billion) is a common way to express it †. The wounded, homeless and migrant figures in the handout [Verification Required: the 25 million wounded, 25 million homeless and 21 million migrants could not be traced to one authoritative source]. For the Bengal famine, estimates run from about 2.1 million (demographic reconstruction; the scholarly consensus cited by Cormac Ó Gráda) to about 3 million (Britannica; Amartya Sen) deaths. Calling it "man-made" reflects the view - associated with Amartya Sen's "entitlement failure" analysis - that wartime inflation, military procurement, the "denial" policies in coastal Bengal, hoarding and the failure to import food and declare a famine, rather than a large crop failure, caused the deaths; how much weight to give crop loss versus policy is still debated.

16.14.4 The Nuremberg Trials (20 November 1945 - 1 October 1946)

  • Who: the International Military Tribunal (IMT), created by the London Agreement and Charter (8 August 1945) of the USA, Britain, the USSR and France, each of which supplied a judge and a prosecution team; 19 other states adhered to the Charter, so it is often said to have represented 23 nations †.
  • Where / when: the Palace of Justice, Nuremberg (chosen partly because it had been the city of the Nazi Party rallies); opened 20 November 1945; verdicts 30 September - 1 October 1946.
  • Charges (four counts): (1) conspiracy (common plan) to commit the crimes below; (2) crimes against peace (planning and waging a war of aggression); (3) war crimes; (4) crimes against humanity.
  • Defendants and verdicts: 24 leading Nazis were indicted and 22 were tried (Robert Ley killed himself before the trial; Gustav Krupp was too ill; Martin Bormann was tried in absentia). 12 were sentenced to death, 3 to life imprisonment, 4 to 10-20 years, and 3 were acquitted (Schacht, von Papen, Fritzsche). Ten were hanged on 16 October 1946; Göring killed himself hours before his execution, and Bormann (already dead) was never caught. The Nazi leadership corps, the SS, the Gestapo and the SD were declared criminal organisations.
  • Significance: the first trial in which leaders were held personally responsible under international law for aggression and crimes against humanity; "obeying orders" was rejected as a defence. The UN General Assembly affirmed the Nuremberg Principles (1946), and the trials are the ancestors of the international criminal tribunals for Yugoslavia (1993) and Rwanda (1994) and the International Criminal Court (Rome Statute, 1998).

[Correction] The handout says that of the 22 Nazi leaders charged "most of them had committed suicide. Ten of them were proven guilty and executed". In fact none of the 22 tried had committed suicide before the trial; the leaders who killed themselves - Hitler, Himmler and Goebbels - never stood trial (and Ley killed himself before it opened). Of the 22, 19 were convicted and 12 sentenced to death; 10 were hanged (16 October 1946) because Göring committed suicide first and Bormann was tried in absentia.

[Additional] India link - the Tokyo Trials: the International Military Tribunal for the Far East (Tokyo, May 1946 - November 1948) tried Japan's wartime leaders (Tojo Hideki and six others were hanged †). The Indian judge, Justice Radhabinod Pal, wrote a famous dissenting judgment acquitting all the accused, arguing that "crimes against peace" were ex post facto law and that the tribunal was victors' justice; he is still honoured in Japan †.

17. The Cold War and the fall of the USSR (1945-1991): introduction, definition and causes

  • The end of World War II was a landmark in world history. The war witnessed the decline of the European powers and the rise of the USA and the USSR as the new superpowers.
  • The superpower era and the sustained geopolitical confrontation between the two superpowers is called the Cold War. It complicated decolonisation (newly independent states were pulled into rival camps) and ended with the fall (dissolution) of the USSR on 26 December 1991 †.
  • Dates: the class notes use 1945-1991. Historians differ on the start - 1945 (Yalta, Potsdam, the atomic bomb), 1946 (Churchill's "iron curtain" speech, 5 March 1946) or 1947 (Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan) - and on the end - 1989 (fall of the Berlin Wall), 1990 (German reunification) or 1991 (end of the USSR). These are interpretations, not settled facts.

17.1 The term "Cold War"

The notes say the term was coined by Bernard Baruch, an American capitalist, and popularised by Walter Lippmann, an American journalist.

[Correction] The usual credit needs refining. George Orwell used "cold war" in his essay "You and the Atom Bomb" (Tribune, 19 October 1945) for a world of "two or three monstrous super-states" armed with the bomb, living in a "peace that is no peace" - apparently the first application to the post-war world (the phrase itself is older). Bernard Baruch - a financier and adviser to several US Presidents - was the first statesman to use it for the US-Soviet rivalry, in a speech in Columbia, South Carolina, on 16 April 1947: "Let us not be deceived: we are today in the midst of a cold war" - words written by his speechwriter Herbert Bayard Swope. Walter Lippmann popularised it with his columns and book The Cold War (1947), a reply to George Kennan's "X" article on containment.

17.3 Definition

  • The Cold War was the antagonistic relationship and geopolitical stand-off between the USA and the USSR from the end of World War II until the fall of the USSR in 1991.
  • The two superpowers competed in every domain - polity, economy, military, and science and technology (the arms race, the space race). Each adopted policies to strengthen itself and weaken the other in order to achieve global hegemony.
  • It is called "cold" because the USA and the USSR never fought each other directly - though they might have, had circumstances allowed (and nearly did, in the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 †). Nuclear weapons made a direct war suicidal.
  • A divided world: the war-like situation split Europe into a capitalist West and a communist East (the "iron curtain"), which in turn split the world into a capitalist bloc ("First World") led by the USA and a communist bloc ("Second World") under the USSR. The Cold War thus created a bipolar world and bipolar politics, leading to a series of proxy conflicts (Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan and others) that characterised the era.
  • [Additional] The term "Third World" (tiers monde) was coined by the French demographer Alfred Sauvy in 1952 † for countries aligned with neither bloc - many of which, led by India, formed the Non-Aligned Movement (Belgrade, 1961).

17.4.2 Historical complications

  1. Anti-Bolshevik support: Britain, France, the USA, Japan and others sent troops to Russia and aided the anti-Bolshevik Whites during the Russian Civil War (1918-20) †, and the USA did not recognise the USSR until 1933. Soviet leaders never forgot this attempt to strangle the revolution.
  2. Psychological fear of the communist world in the West - of the Comintern, of strikes and revolution at home (the "Red Scare" of 1919-20 †).

Mains Points

  • Why Germany won in 1939-41: Blitzkrieg (tanks, aircraft, radio, surprise), the Ardennes gamble, Allied defensive thinking (Maginot Line), Soviet neutrality under the pact (16.7).
  • Why Britain survived 1940: Dunkirk saved the army; the Battle of Britain won by radar and the Dowding system, home ground and production; the Royal Navy; Churchill's leadership and American aid (16.7.6, Figure 2).
  • The turning of the war, 1941-43: Barbarossa and Pearl Harbor brought in the two powers with the largest resources; Moscow, Midway, El Alamein and Stalingrad are the turning points (Figure 3).
  • Causes of Allied victory: resources (USA, USSR, Empire incl. India), unified command, sea and air power, radar and codebreaking, the atomic bomb, against Axis separation, resource shortage, overstretch, brutality and strategic errors (16.13).
  • The atomic bombs: official rationale (end the war, avoid invasion) versus revisionist interpretations (atomic diplomacy towards the USSR) (16.12).
  • Atlantic Charter and decolonisation: self-determination proclaimed for Europe, claimed by Asia; Churchill's 9 September 1941 exclusion of India; American pressure; Cripps and Quit India; UN Charter and Res. 1514 (16.9.1).
  • Japan and decolonisation: the fall of Singapore shattered European prestige; "Asia for the Asians", armed nationalists (INA, Burma, Indonesia) - the colonial order could not be restored after 1945 (16.10.2).
  • Impact: superpowers and bipolarity; the end of European dominance; the UN and Bretton Woods order; human rights and international criminal law (Nuremberg, Tokyo, UDHR, Genocide Convention); demographic upheaval and the changing role of women (16.14, Figure 4).
  • League vs UN: universality, the veto, majority voting, enforcement, trusteeship (Table 16.6).
  • Roots of the Cold War: ideology plus history - intervention of 1918-20, appeasement, the Nazi-Soviet Pact, the delayed second front - and the historiographical debate (orthodox, revisionist, post-revisionist) (17.4).
  • India link: India's war contribution (2.5 million volunteers, resources) became an argument for independence; the war's costs (famine, inflation) and the weakening of Britain hastened 1947 (16.14).

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Winston Churchill (1874-1965): British PM 1940-45 and 1951-55; led Britain alone in 1940; co-author of the Atlantic Charter.
  • Franklin D. Roosevelt (1882-1945): US President 1933-45; Lend-Lease, Atlantic Charter; died 12 April 1945.
  • Harry S. Truman: US President from 12 April 1945; ordered the atomic bombings; at Potsdam.
  • Joseph Stalin: Soviet leader; the Nazi-Soviet Pact; war leader of the USSR; one of the Big Three.
  • Philippe Pétain: French marshal; head of the Vichy state 1940-44.
  • Charles de Gaulle: leader of the Free French from London (appeal of 18 June 1940).
  • Hermann Göring: head of the Luftwaffe; Nuremberg defendant; suicide 15 October 1946.
  • Hugh Dowding: head of RAF Fighter Command in the Battle of Britain.
  • Bertram Ramsay: Vice-Admiral who directed the Dunkirk evacuation (and the naval side of D-Day).
  • Erwin Rommel: German general; the Afrika Korps ("Desert Fox").
  • Bernard Montgomery: British general; victor of El Alamein (1942).
  • Dwight D. Eisenhower: US general; commanded Torch (1942) and Overlord (1944) as Supreme Allied Commander.
  • Georgy Zhukov: Soviet marshal; Khalkhin Gol, Moscow, Stalingrad, Berlin.
  • Isoroku Yamamoto: Japanese admiral; planned Pearl Harbor and Midway.
  • Douglas MacArthur / Chester Nimitz: US commanders in the South-West and Central Pacific.
  • Louis Mountbatten: British Supreme Allied Commander, South-East Asia Command (1943-46); later last Viceroy of India.
  • William Slim: commander of the British-Indian Fourteenth Army in Burma.
  • Victor Emmanuel III / Pietro Badoglio: King who dismissed Mussolini (1943) / PM who signed Italy's armistice.
  • Hirohito: Emperor of Japan; broadcast the surrender on 15 August 1945.
  • Subhas Chandra Bose: led the Indian National Army with Japanese help (1943-45).
  • Radhabinod Pal: Indian judge at the Tokyo Tribunal; dissented.
  • Bernard Baruch: US financier and presidential adviser; first used "cold war" in a public speech (1947).
  • Herbert Bayard Swope: journalist who wrote Baruch's 1947 speech.
  • Walter Lippmann: US journalist; The Cold War (1947).
  • George Orwell: British writer; "You and the Atom Bomb" (1945).
  • Tony Judt: historian; Postwar (2005).
  • Amartya Sen: economist; "entitlement" analysis of the Bengal famine (Poverty and Famines, 1981).
  • Luftwaffe / RAF / Wehrmacht / Afrika Korps: German air force / British Royal Air Force / German armed forces / German army in North Africa.
  • Blitzkrieg / the Blitz: lightning war (German method) / German bombing of British cities, 1940-41.
  • Phoney War: the inactive Western Front, 1939-40.
  • Vichy France: Pétain's collaborationist French state, 1940-44.
  • Lend-Lease: US programme supplying Allies with war materials from 1941.
  • Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere: Japan's bloc under the slogan "Asia for the Asians".
  • Ultra / Enigma / Lorenz: Allied intelligence from breaking German machine ciphers at Bletchley Park.
  • Manhattan Project: the US programme that built the atomic bomb.
  • Operation names: Dynamo (Dunkirk), Catapult (French fleet), Sea Lion (planned invasion of Britain), Barbarossa (USSR), Torch (North Africa), Husky (Sicily), Overlord (Normandy), Bagration (Belorussia), Downfall (planned invasion of Japan).
  • IMT / IMTFE: the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals.
  • Bretton Woods institutions: IMF and IBRD (World Bank). GATT: General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (1947).
  • Cold War / bipolarity / proxy war: superpower rivalry without direct war / a world of two power centres / a conflict fought through allies or clients.
  • First, Second and Third World: capitalist bloc, communist bloc, and the non-aligned / developing countries.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Battle of Britain (air, Jul-Oct 1940) vs the Blitz (night bombing, Sep 1940 - May 1941) vs Blitzkrieg (German land method).
  • Operation Sea Lion was planned, never launched; Operation Catapult (July 1940) targeted the French fleet, not the Germans.
  • Operation Husky = Sicily; Torch = Morocco and Algeria (Vichy-held); Overlord = Normandy; Dynamo = Dunkirk.
  • Atlantic Charter (1941) was a joint declaration, not a treaty, and the USA was not yet at war; Churchill excluded India from point 3.
  • Lend-Lease (1941) vs cash and carry (1939): cash and carry required payment; Lend-Lease did not.
  • Tehran (1943) = second front agreed; Yalta (Feb 1945) = zones, UN, Soviet entry against Japan; Potsdam (Jul-Aug 1945) = Germany's administration and the ultimatum to Japan.
  • Moscow (Dec 1941) = first major Soviet victory; Stalingrad (Feb 1943) = turning point; Kursk (Jul 1943) = largest tank battle.
  • Coral Sea = first carrier battle; Midway = decisive carrier battle.
  • Mussolini fell (25 Jul 1943) before Italy's armistice (8 Sep 1943); he was killed by partisans on 28 Apr 1945, two days before Hitler's suicide.
  • V-E Day 8 May (Victory Day in Russia 9 May); Japan: announcement 15 Aug, formal surrender 2 Sep 1945.
  • Finland was not an Axis member (co-belligerent); Thailand allied with Japan; Vichy France was neutral-collaborationist.
  • Nuremberg Laws (1935) vs Nuremberg Trials (1945-46); Hitler, Himmler and Goebbels were never tried.
  • IMF and World Bank: agreed 1944, in existence 1945; GATT 1947; WTO 1995.
  • "Cold war" coined: Orwell (1945) wrote it first; Baruch (1947) first used it as a statesman; Lippmann (1947) popularised it.
  • Mountbatten was British (SEAC), not an American commander; MacArthur and Nimitz were the American Pacific commanders.

30-Second Revision

1939-41 (Axis triumphs): Pact (Aug 39) → Poland (1 Sep) → war (3 Sep) → Phoney War → Norway (Apr 40) → Case Yellow, Ardennes (May) → Dunkirk → France falls (Jun) → Catapult → Battle of Britain (Sea Lion dropped) → Blitz → Balkans (Apr 41).

1941 (world war): Barbarossa (22 Jun) → Atlantic Charter (Aug) → Moscow holds (Dec) → oil embargo → Pearl Harbor (7 Dec) → USA at war (8-11 Dec).

1942-43 (turning points): Singapore falls → Midway (Jun 42) → El Alamein, Torch (Nov 42) → Stalingrad (Feb 43) → Tunisia, Kursk, Sicily → Mussolini falls → Tehran.

1944-45 (Allied victory): D-Day → Bagration → Yalta → Hitler dies → V-E Day → Potsdam → Hiroshima, Soviet entry, Nagasaki → Japan surrenders (15 Aug / 2 Sep).

Impact: nuclear age; USA + USSR superpowers; Cold War; decolonisation; Europe eclipsed; UN; Bretton Woods + GATT; ~3% of humanity dead, Holocaust, Bengal famine, mass expulsions; Nuremberg and Tokyo; UDHR.

Cold War: 1945/47-1991; term Orwell-Baruch-Lippmann; bipolar world; causes = ideology + history (1918-20 intervention, appeasement, Nazi-Soviet Pact, delayed second front) ...

Corrections Made

  1. c01 - The secret protocol put the Baltic states, Finland and Bessarabia in the Soviet sphere of influence; it did not "allow annexation" (Lithuania was added on 28 September 1939).
  2. c02 - "Sept 19389" is a slip for September 1939.
  3. c03 - The Baltic states were forced into mutual assistance treaties in Sep-Oct 1939, occupied in June 1940 and annexed in August 1940 - not annexed "by November 1939".
  4. c04 - Before June 1941 the USSR was already using force (Poland, Finland, Baltics, Bessarabia) as a non-belligerent partner of Germany; June 1941 began its war against the Axis.
  5. c05 - The Low Countries and the Ardennes thrust were one offensive (Case Yellow) launched on the same day, 10 May 1940, not a preliminary conquest.
  6. c06 - Operation Dynamo was organised by the Royal Navy and directed by Vice-Admiral Ramsay (War Office order of 26 May); about 338,000 were rescued.
  7. c07 - Operation Sea Lion was never launched: ordered 16 July 1940, postponed indefinitely 17 September 1940.
  8. c08 - Operation Catapult (3 July 1940) came before Sea Lion and responded to the French armistice, to keep the French fleet from German hands.
  9. c09 - The Battle of Britain was the first major campaign fought entirely by air forces, not the "first aerial war" (air war existed in WWI).
  10. c10 - The RAF's edge was radar, the Dowding system, home ground and production, not greater experience; the Lorenz cipher was broken only from 1942.
  11. c11 - "Luftwaffe 50% destroyed" is unverified; IWM gives about 1,733 German and 915 British aircraft destroyed.
  12. c12 - Hungary, Romania and Slovakia joined the Tripartite Pact in November 1940 and Bulgaria on 1 March 1941; Finland never joined (co-belligerent from June 1941).
  13. c13 - No "Treaty of Friendship and Alliance, 1941": Anglo-Soviet Agreement 12 July 1941; twenty-year Anglo-Soviet Treaty 26 May 1942.
  14. c14 - Scorched earth was the Soviet response, not the cause of German gains; Germany captured about 1.6 million sq km by December 1941, not 8 million.
  15. c15 - The Neutrality Acts were of 1935, 1936, 1937 and 1939; cash and carry for arms came with the Act of 4 November 1939.
  16. c16 - The Atlantic Charter did not explicitly oppose colonialism; Churchill (9 September 1941) said point 3 did not alter British policy on India and Burma.
  17. c17 - Japan occupied (not conquered) Indochina: north in September 1940, south in July 1941, under agreements forced on Vichy.
  18. c18 - The oil embargo angered Japan, not the US: Japan's move into southern Indochina led to the US asset freeze and embargo.
  19. c19 - At Pearl Harbor 19 ships were damaged or destroyed (most repaired); 2,403 Americans were killed, including 2,008 Navy personnel and 68 civilians.
  20. c20 - Japanese aircraft losses at Midway are given as about 250-320 (Britannica 320); "332" is unsupported; the four carriers are correct.
  21. c21 - MacArthur and Nimitz were the US Pacific commanders; Mountbatten was British, Supreme Allied Commander of South-East Asia Command.
  22. c22 - In August 1945 Japan still held Korea, Manchuria, much of China, Indochina, Malaya, Singapore and most of the Dutch East Indies, not only Korea and Manchuria.
  23. c23 - Egypt was the British base; El Alamein (Oct-Nov 1942) drove the invading Axis out of Egypt rather than "conquering Egypt from the Axis".
  24. c24 - Operation Torch was Anglo-American and landed in Vichy-held Morocco and Algeria; Axis forces in Africa surrendered in Tunisia on 13 May 1943.
  25. c25 - Operation Husky was the invasion of Sicily (July 1943); the mainland landings came in September 1943.
  26. c26 - Mussolini was arrested (25 July 1943) before Italy's armistice (8 September 1943), not after it.
  27. c27 - Mussolini was shot on 28 April 1945 by Communist-led partisans of the CLN, not by "socialists".
  28. c28 - Revenge was not the official reason for the atomic bombings; the stated aim was a quick surrender without invasion; other motives are historians' interpretations.
  29. c29 - The USSR had denounced the Neutrality Pact on 5 April 1945 (valid to April 1946) and attacked on 8-9 August under its Yalta promise.
  30. c30 - The handout's line on borders is Tony Judt's (Postwar, 2005) and omits his "one major exception" (Poland moved west).
  31. c31 - The IMF and World Bank were agreed in July 1944 but came into existence on 27 December 1945; GATT was signed 30 October 1947.
  32. c32 - None of the 22 tried had committed suicide before trial (Hitler, Himmler, Goebbels were never tried); 19 convicted, 12 sentenced to death, 10 hanged.
  33. c33 - Orwell first applied "cold war" to the post-war world (19 Oct 1945); Baruch (16 Apr 1947, written by Swope) was the first statesman to use it; Lippmann popularised it.
  34. c34 - "Churchill's delay in Op. Barbarossa" means the delay in opening a second front (until June 1944) during the German invasion of the USSR.
