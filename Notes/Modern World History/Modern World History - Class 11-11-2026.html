7. The American Revolution (continued) - from the First Continental Congress to the Bill of Rights (1774-1791)

Continues from 7.6.11 Coercive Acts in the 10 November note. Sections 7.7 onwards below replace the short "looking ahead" preview (7.7) of that note.

7.7 First Continental Congress, 1774

  • Background: the Coercive Acts suspended Massachusetts' elected government. The colonists formed provincial congresses, which were extra-legal assemblies that took over government in place of the royal one. Massachusetts set up its Provincial Congress on 7 October 1774 (its members first met at Salem on 5 October) [Additional].
  • Who / where / when: 56 delegates from 12 colonies met at Carpenters' Hall, Philadelphia, from 5 September to 26 October 1774 (see Map 1).
  • Absent: Georgia. It faced a threat from Native peoples on its frontier and needed British military protection, and its royal governor blocked the choice of delegates (Britannica) [Additional]. The class note called this an "internal conflict"; these are the reasons sources give.
  • Declaration and Resolves (14 October 1774):
    • declared the Coercive Acts violations of the colonists' rights;
    • denounced taxation without representation;
    • questioned Parliament's authority to legislate for the colonies' internal affairs.
    It still professed allegiance to the King (Avalon Project).
  • Continental Association (20 October 1774) [Additional]: a colony-wide boycott enforced by local committees.
    • Non-importation of British goods from 1 December 1774.
    • Non-exportation to Britain from 10 September 1775, if grievances were not redressed. This was the colonists' "threat to stop exports".
  • Result: the boycott worked. In the first six months of 1775, British exports to the colonies fell from about £3 million to £220,000 (US National Archives) (c01).
  • Congress agreed to meet again in May 1775 if grievances remained.

[Correction] The rough notes say imports fell by "97%". The US National Archives figure (£3 million down to £220,000 in the first half of 1775) is a fall of about 93%. No reliable source for "97%" was found, so that number is [Verification Required]. Either way, the point stands: the boycott sharply cut British trade.

Exam point: the First Continental Congress was the first united action of almost all the colonies. It was a protest body: it asked for redress within the Empire, not independence. The Coercive Acts were meant to divide the colonies and isolate Massachusetts; they united them instead.

7.8 Outbreak of war - Lexington and Concord, 19 April 1775

  • Why: with their assembly suspended, Massachusetts colonists (through the Provincial Congress) stored arms and gunpowder at the village of Concord and drilled the militia, including the "minutemen", who were to be ready at a minute's notice [Additional].
  • Who: General Thomas Gage, military governor of Massachusetts, sent British regulars from Boston to seize the military stores at Concord.
  • What / when: shots were exchanged first at Lexington and then at North Bridge, Concord, both on the same day, 19 April 1775. Militia then harassed the British column all the way back to Boston.
  • Losses (US National Park Service):
    • British: 273 casualties (73 killed, 174 wounded, 26 missing).
    • Colonial: 95 (49 killed, 41 wounded, 5 missing). Some sources give 39 wounded.
  • Consequence: the American War of Independence began. Militia then besieged Boston (c02, c03).

[Correction] The rough notes put the outbreak of war "during" the second meeting of Congress. In fact the war began on 19 April 1775, about three weeks before the Second Continental Congress first met on 10 May 1775. Congress then met while the war was already under way.

[Correction] "Battle of Lexington, 1775" and "Battle of Concord, 1775" were one day's fighting (19 April 1775), usually treated as a single engagement. The figure 273 is the total British casualties (killed, wounded and missing), not the number killed. Only 73 British soldiers were killed (NPS; Britannica).

7.9 Second Continental Congress (10 May 1775 - 1 March 1781) and the Olive Branch Petition

  • Where / when: the Pennsylvania State House (now Independence Hall), Philadelphia, from 10 May 1775.
    • It served as the common (de facto) government of the colonies during the war.
    • It remained in session until the Articles of Confederation came into force on 1 March 1781. After that it continued as the "Congress of the Confederation" [Additional].
  • Delegates: leaders included John Adams, Samuel Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson (who joined in June 1775) and George Washington (c04).
  • Army: Congress created the Continental Army on 14 June 1775 and appointed George Washington commander-in-chief on 15 June 1775.
  • Olive Branch Petition (adopted 5 July 1775, signed 8 July 1775; drafted mainly by John Dickinson †):
    • professed loyalty to George III;
    • asked for a "happy and permanent reconciliation";
    • asked the King to prevent further bloodshed;
    • asked for repeal of the statutes that distressed the colonies.
  • Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms (6 July 1775) [Additional]: adopted alongside the petition. It justified armed resistance while denying any aim of independence.
  • British response:
    • George III refused to receive the petition.
    • His Proclamation for Suppressing Rebellion and Sedition (23 August 1775) declared the colonies in "open and avowed rebellion", so the colonists were now treated as rebels or traitors.
    • The Prohibitory Act (22 December 1775) banned all trade with the colonies [Additional].
    The result was full-scale war (c05).
[Correction] The rough notes say "65 representatives of 13 colonies". No reliable source for 65 was found [Verification Required]. Membership changed over time; sources speak of about 56-60 delegates sitting at any one time.
Georgia was not fully represented at the start:
  • Lyman Hall attended from 13 May 1775 for one Georgia parish only.
  • Georgia joined the Association in July 1775.
  • Its full delegation arrived around August-September 1775 (Britannica).
So all 13 colonies were represented only from later in 1775.

[Correction] The Olive Branch Petition did not demand "political autonomy and freedom of trade". Its text asks only for reconciliation, an end to bloodshed and the repeal of distressing statutes (Avalon Project, Journals of Congress, 8 July 1775). Demands about trade and self-government appear in the Declaration and Resolves (1774). Also, George III did not merely decline to read it: he refused to receive it formally and proclaimed the colonies in rebellion.

[Correction] The rough notes say "France got tobacco and Senegal". The island is Tobago, not tobacco. Also, France's and Spain's gains came from the separate Treaties of Versailles signed the same day, not from the Anglo-American Treaty of Paris. Spain "was given Florida" in the sense that it kept the East and West Florida it had already conquered (Britannica, Peace of Paris 1783).

[Correction] The treaty did not "name" the colonies the United States of America. The name was already in use:
  • the Declaration of Independence (1776) speaks of the "united States of America";
  • Article I of the Articles of Confederation (adopted 15 November 1777, in force 1 March 1781) makes "The United States of America" the official style †.
In 1783 Britain recognised the United States by that name.

[Correction] The rough notes leave the drafting date blank and say the Constitution "was adopted in 1789". It was drafted and signed in 1787 (Philadelphia Convention, 25 May - 17 September 1787) and ratified in 1788. It took effect on 4 March 1789 (Mount Vernon; US National Archives).

[Correction] Separation of powers is not achieved "through a bicameral legislature".
  • Separation of powers divides authority among the three branches: legislature, executive and judiciary.
  • Bicameralism is a separate feature: Congress has two houses.
Both are features of the US Constitution, but they are distinct ideas.
[Correction] The rough note "the first 10 amendments (1791) made to first written constitution for the modern world came to be known as Bull of rights" mixes two points.
  • The first ten amendments, ratified on 15 December 1791, are the Bill of Rights.
  • The US Constitution is best described as the oldest written national constitution still in use (Britannica). Calling it simply "the first written constitution of the modern world" is debatable: written frameworks of government existed earlier, including the American state constitutions of 1776 and the Articles of Confederation †.

Balanced view [Additional]:
  • Supports the thesis: the economic grievances are real, and the revolutionary leadership was largely merchant, planter and professional. Progressive historians such as Charles Beard † stressed economic interests.
  • Qualifications:
    • Mercantilism had operated for a century without revolt, cushioned by salutary neglect and smuggling.
    • Many colonists, such as Southern planters with protected markets, gained from the system.
    • The language of the revolt was constitutional and ideological: rights of Englishmen, Locke and consent. Constitutional and ideological historians such as Bernard Bailyn † stress this.
  • Conclusion: economic conflict was a major underlying cause, turned into revolution by political and ideological factors. This is an interpretation, not settled fact.

8. The French Revolution and Napoleon (1789-1799-1815)

8.0 What it was - overview

  • What: a period of far-reaching social and political upheaval in late-18th-century France, inspired by liberal and radical Enlightenment ideas.
  • Dates: 1789 (Estates-General, Bastille) to 1799 (Napoleon's coup of 18 Brumaire, 9 November 1799). Napoleon then ruled until 1815 (c15).
  • Achievements:
    • Abolished feudalism and class privileges.
    • Overthrew absolute monarchy in France (Republic from 1792 †).
    • Spread the ideas of liberty, equality, fraternity, popular sovereignty and nationalism across Europe.
    • Unleashed the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (1792-1815) †, a wave of European and global conflict.
    It permanently changed the course of history (c14).
  • Popular summaries: the class note says historians call it a "living legend" and sum it up in four words, Revolution, War, Tyranny and Empire. Both phrases are a useful mnemonic for the four phases (constitutional revolution → war → Terror → Napoleonic Empire). Neither could be traced to a named historian [Verification Required].
  • Napoleon is popularly called "the child of the Revolution". The phrase is used, for example, by A. J. Grant in Europe in the Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries, and Napoleon himself said "I am the Revolution" †. He is also called the product of the reaction against it, because his rule ended the Republic.

[Correction] The rough note says the Revolution "replaced theocracies and monarchies with democracies and republics". That is an overgeneralisation.
  • France was not a theocracy: it was an absolute monarchy with a privileged Catholic Church.
  • The French Republic itself gave way to Napoleon's Empire (1804) and to the Bourbon Restoration (1814/15) †.
The accurate claim is about long-term influence: the Revolution weakened absolutism, feudalism and Church privilege, and spread republican and democratic ideals that later reshaped Europe.
[Correction] "Napoleon's Rule (1799-1804-1819)" should read 1799-1804-1815:
  • First Consul after 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799).
  • Proclaimed Emperor 18 May 1804, crowned 2 December 1804.
  • Final defeat at Waterloo, 18 June 1815 †, and abdication on 22 June 1815.
  • Died in exile on St Helena on 5 May 1821 (Britannica).
Nothing ends in 1819.

8.1 Conditions in France before 1789 (the Old Regime)

8.1.1 Polity - absolute monarchy under weak kings

  • Dynasty: the Bourbons ruled France from 1589 (Henry IV, the first Bourbon king).
  • Kings: Louis XIV (1643-1715 †; "I am the State" is attributed to him †), Louis XV (1715-74 †) and Louis XVI (1774-92). They were autocrats who claimed the divine right of kings: power came from God, so the king was answerable to no one.
  • Versailles: Louis XIV moved the court and government from the old capital Paris to Versailles (officially on 6 May 1682), about 20 km south-west of Paris. Its extravagant palace and lavish court symbolised royal excess.
  • Weak rulers: Louis XV, and especially Louis XVI, lacked the qualities of an effective autocrat. Louis XVI was strongly influenced by his wife, Marie Antoinette:
    • an Austrian archduchess of the Habsburg(-Lorraine) dynasty and daughter of Empress Maria Theresa;
    • married to Louis on 16 May 1770;
    • a supporter of the nobility at court;
    • unpopular, nicknamed "Madame Deficit" †.
  • Administration: under weak monarchs it became inefficient and corrupt. Autocracy under a weak monarch was bound to fail, the key point of the class.

8.1.2 Society - the three estates

France had a feudal, mostly Catholic society full of inequalities. It was divided into three "orders" or estates (Figure 1).

[Correction] The rough shares (clergy 1%, nobility 2%, Third Estate 97%; Church land 10%; noble land 25-30%) are slightly high for the privileged orders. Standard estimates (Britannica; NCERT Class IX) are:
  • population: clergy about 0.5%, nobility about 1-1.5%;
  • land: Church about 6-10%, nobles about 20-25%.
All such figures are estimates.

[Correction] The rough notes say the Third Estate had no "own agriculture land". That holds for most peasants but not the whole Third Estate. NCERT notes that about 60% of the land was owned by nobles, the Church and the richer members of the Third Estate, and that only a small group of peasants owned the land they cultivated.

[Correction] Two errors about the Estates-General:
  1. Its last meeting before 1789 was in 1614, not 1613.
  2. Its decisions were not binding on the King. It was a consultative body, and the King decided when, and whether, to call and dismiss it. Britannica: it was not required to approve royal taxation or legislation. In practice kings called it to win consent for taxes (NCERT).
Kings avoided it because it could become a platform for grievances, not because it could bind them.

8.1.3 Economy - fiscal crisis

  • Feudal, agrarian and decentralised economy. Revenue administration was decentralised, inefficient and corrupt, which produced a chronic fiscal deficit.
  • Unjust tax system:
    • The First and Second Estates were largely exempt, so the whole burden fell on the Third Estate.
    • Tax farming: the government sold the right to collect indirect taxes to private financiers, the tax farmers (the Ferme generale †), who extracted the maximum.
    • Some historians say peasants paid about 60% of their income in taxes, tithe and dues [Verification Required]. No reliable source was found for this figure.
  • Waste: revenue was spent on the luxurious royal court, Versailles and frequent wars:
    • War of the Austrian Succession (1740-48): costly and gained nothing (c19);
    • Seven Years' War (1756-63): a heavy defeat, with Canada and Indian power lost (see the 10 November note).
    These wars forced heavy borrowing.
  • Debt service: by the late 1780s, interest on the debt took roughly half of royal revenue. Estimates range from about 42% (1787) to 62% of tax receipts (1788) [Verification Required for an exact figure]. Lenders charged up to 10% interest (NCERT). France faced bankruptcy.
  • Reforming finance ministers (Controllers-General):
    • Turgot (1774-76);
    • Necker (1777-81, 1788-90);
    • Calonne (1783-87);
    • Brienne (1787-88) [Additional].
    They proposed:
    1. taxing the nobility: blocked by privileged opposition. Calonne's universal land tax was rejected by the Assembly of Notables (February 1787) [Additional];
    2. cutting court expenditure: resisted at court;
    3. avoiding unnecessary wars.
  • American war: France nevertheless joined the American War of Independence (1778-83) (7.10). It added more than a billion livres to a debt that had already risen above 2 billion livres (NCERT) (c20).
  • Option left: to levy new taxes, which needed the consent of the nation. Hence the Estates-General (8.4.1).
[Correction] France did not clearly "lose" the War of the Austrian Succession.
  • The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) † largely restored pre-war positions. France handed back its conquests, including Madras to Britain †, and so gained nothing for its expense.
  • The Seven Years' War (1756-63) was a clear defeat.

[Correction] The rough notes give the American war's cost as "1.3 billion livres" and say Louis XVI's debt increased by "250%". The best-supported figure (NCERT; most scholarly estimates) is about 1 billion livres, added to a debt of over 2 billion. The 1.3 billion estimate comes from weaker sources, and no reliable source was found for "250%" [Verification Required].

8.2 Causes - American inspiration and the immediate crisis

  • Practical inspiration from the American Revolution: French soldiers and officers who fought in America, such as Lafayette and the troops under Rochambeau (7.10), returned home and shared their experience. The American example spread these ideas:
    1. liberty, equality, fraternity (the slogan itself became popular during the Revolution †);
    2. no taxation without representation;
    3. a republic is better than a monarchy;
    4. the right to revolt against tyrannical government.
    The war also deepened France's debt.
  • Immediate causes:
    • Bankruptcy: after the Notables and the parlements refused new taxes, the King announced in August 1788 that the Estates-General would meet in May 1789 †, to discuss taxation (c22).
    • Subsistence crisis: the 1788 harvest failed. In Paris the price of a 4-lb loaf rose from 8 to 14 sous by January 1789, nearly doubling (Britannica). This made the urban and rural poor hungry and angry.

8.4 Stage 1 - the National Assembly and the fall of the Bastille (1789-1791)

8.4.1 The Estates-General at Versailles (May 1789)

  • When / where: opened on 5 May 1789 at Versailles (c22).
  • Third Estate demands:
    1. double representation, about 600 deputies against about 300 each for the clergy and nobles;
    2. a joint sitting of all three estates;
    3. voting by head (one deputy, one vote).
    The Third Estate deputies were educated and wealthy men, mostly lawyers and officials †.
  • Outcome:
    • The King granted the first demand: the royal council decided on 27 December 1788 that the Third Estate would have as many deputies as the other two combined.
    • Hoping for the rest, the Third Estate took part.
    • The privileged orders opposed the joint sitting and voting by head. The King sided with them, fearing government would fall under popular control, and rejected the other demands.

[Correction] The rough notes date the Estates-General to "18 May 1789". It opened on 5 May 1789 (Britannica). The King had announced the meeting in August 1788, and the doubling of the Third Estate was decided on 27 December 1788, before the meeting.

Interactive map of Paris and Versailles: open a larger map. Map data (c) OpenStreetMap contributors.

8.4.2 National Assembly and the Tennis Court Oath (June 1789)

  • Disappointed with the King, the Third Estate deputies declared themselves the National Assembly on 17 June 1789.
  • Locked out of their hall, they met in a nearby indoor tennis court (the Jeu de Paume) at Versailles. On 20 June 1789 they swore not to separate until they had given France a constitution: the Tennis Court Oath.
  • The class takes the National Assembly and the Tennis Court Oath as examples of fraternity.
  • King's reaction:
    • At a royal session on 23 June he declared the Third Estate's actions illegal †.
    • Faced with defiance and Parisian unrest, on 27 June 1789 he ordered the clergy and nobles to join the National Assembly. Constitution-making began.
    • Many clergy and some liberal nobles had already joined † [Additional].

8.4.3 Fall of the Bastille, 14 July 1789

  • At the same time the King massed troops around Paris and Versailles, and on 11 July dismissed the popular minister Necker.
  • Fearing a royal coup against the Assembly, Parisians stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress-prison and symbol of despotism, on 14 July 1789.
  • The fortress was then demolished (c23).
  • The fall of the Bastille symbolises liberty. 14 July is France's national day † [Additional].

[Correction] The crowd that stormed the Bastille was chiefly after gunpowder †. It had seized muskets earlier that day from the Invalides †. The Bastille was not "destroyed" on 14 July: it was demolished over the following months †. It held only seven prisoners at the time †.

8.4.4 The Great Fear and the abolition of feudalism (July-August 1789)

  • The Great Fear (about 20 July to early August 1789): rumours of an "aristocratic plot" spread. Peasants, especially in the countryside, attacked chateaux and burnt the records of feudal dues (c24).
  • Emigres: many nobles fled abroad. Most went to the Rhineland, especially Koblenz, and to Turin †, and they sought help from Austria and Prussia. They were called emigres (Map 3).
  • Night of 4 August 1789: in the National Assembly, liberal noble and clerical deputies renounced feudal privileges (the Vicomte de Noailles and the Duc d'Aiguillon made the first proposals †).
    • The August decrees abolished serfdom, the tithe and tax privileges †.
    • Some dues were to be redeemed (bought out) rather than abolished outright † [Additional].
    "Voluntary", but under heavy pressure from peasant revolt.

[Correction] The rough notes say "many of the nobles got killed and escaped to Austria and Prussia". The Great Fear mainly destroyed property and feudal records; killings of nobles were few †. The mass killings came later, in 1792-94. Emigres gathered mainly at Koblenz and Turin †, and looked to Austria and Prussia for help.

8.4.5 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, 26 August 1789

  • Who / when: adopted by the National Assembly on 26 August 1789. Lafayette had a hand in drafting it, with Jefferson's advice †.
  • Later status: it became the preamble to the Constitution of 1791 (8.4.8).
  • Content:
    • popular sovereignty: sovereignty resides in the nation;
    • natural and inalienable rights: liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression †;
    • representative government and rule of law: law is the expression of the general will;
    • equality before the law;
    • freedom of opinion and religion (religious tolerance) and freedom of the press †.
    Together with the August decrees it abolished feudal and birth privileges. It stands for the ideal of equality.
  • Limits: it did not provide absolute equality.
    • Women were excluded. Olympe de Gouges answered with a Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791) †.
    • Slavery in the colonies continued until 1794 †.
    • Voting was limited to "active citizens" (c25).
Fraternity
National Assembly, Tennis Court Oath (June 1789)→Liberty
Fall of the Bastille (14 July 1789)→Equality
4 August decrees + Declaration of Rights (26 August 1789)
[Correction] The rough notes say that "Man" means an active citizen and "Citizen" a passive citizen. That is wrong.
  • The title "Rights of Man and of the Citizen" separates the natural rights of every human being from the political rights of members of the nation.
  • The active/passive distinction (a Sieyes idea) came later, from the Assembly's decrees of late 1789 and the Constitution of 1791:
    • Active citizens were men aged 25 and over who paid direct taxes equal to at least three days' wages, and could vote. More than half of adult men qualified (Britannica).
    • Passive citizens, including all women, had civil rights but no vote.

8.4.6 Paris Commune, National Guard and the October Days (1789)

  • In July 1789 Parisians set up the Paris Commune, a new revolutionary municipal government for Paris. Its exact founding date was not confirmed for this note †.
  • They also formed the National Guard, a citizens' militia to protect the city and the National Assembly, under Lafayette (commander from 15 July 1789).
  • October Days (5-6 October 1789): a crowd, many of them market women, angry about bread prices, marched to Versailles [Additional].
    • The King and his family were forced to move to Paris, to the Tuileries palace †, and live among the people.
    • The King became, in effect, a prisoner of the people, and the Assembly also moved to Paris †.

8.4.7 Flight to Varennes (June 1791) and the Declaration of Pillnitz (August 1791)

  • Flight to Varennes (20-21 June 1791):
    • The royal family secretly fled Paris for the royalist stronghold of Montmedy near the frontier of the Austrian Netherlands, hoping for Austrian support.
    • They were recognised and arrested at Varennes and brought back to Paris (Map 3).
    • The people now saw the King as a traitor to the Revolution, which boosted republicanism †.
  • Declaration of Pillnitz (27 August 1791):
    • The emigres, led by the King's brother the Comte d'Artois †, appealed to Emperor Leopold II of Austria (Marie Antoinette's brother †) and King Frederick William II of Prussia.
    • Meeting at Pillnitz near Dresden, the two rulers declared the French royal family's situation a common concern of all European sovereigns. They threatened intervention, since the Revolution was a threat to the old order in Europe (c26).
    • The French saw the declaration as a threat to the Revolution. It helped lead to war with Austria in April 1792 † (next class).

[Correction] The Declaration of Pillnitz did not simply call on European monarchs to unite. Its threat was conditional: Austria and Prussia would act only if the other powers joined them. Since Britain would not, the threat was largely bluff (Britannica). In France, however, it was read as a real threat.

8.4.8 Constitution of 1791

  • The National Assembly completed the constitution on 3 September 1791, and the King swore to it on 14 September 1791.
  • It made France a constitutional monarchy with the Declaration of Rights as its preamble †. Power was separated:
    • a single-chamber Legislative Assembly, elected by active citizens through electors †;
    • the King, with a suspensive veto †;
    • an elected judiciary †.
  • Its details and its collapse in 1792 are expected in the next class.

Mains Points

  • From protest to independence: the colonies moved in stages: petition and boycott (1774) → armed resistance (1775) → a rejected Olive Branch → independence (1776). British intransigence (the Proclamation of Rebellion and the Prohibitory Act) radicalised moderates.
  • Why the colonies won: home ground and Washington's leadership; British logistics across the Atlantic; and decisively the French alliance after Saratoga, with French sea power at Yorktown (useful for Optional 2023 and 2018).
  • Significance of the American Revolution:
    • the first successful colonial revolt;
    • a written republican and federal constitution with separation of powers;
    • a Bill of Rights;
    • inspiration and debt for France in 1789;
    • the shift of Britain's imperial focus towards India and Asia †.
    The Revolution also had limits: slavery and the dispossession of Native Americans continued.
  • Mercantilism vs capitalism (7.13): an economic framework with political and ideological qualifiers.
  • Causes of the French Revolution: structural causes (absolutism, estates, taxation, Enlightenment ideas) plus immediate ones (bankruptcy, the 1788 harvest, the Estates-General voting dispute). Philosophers supplied ideas; social and economic crisis supplied the trigger (Optional 2023, 2020, 2016).
  • Declaration of the Rights of Man: the "death-warrant of privilege", yet limited by property-based citizenship and the exclusion of women and slaves (Optional 2014).
  • Weak monarch thesis: absolutism needs a strong ruler. Louis XVI's vacillation, from conceding double representation to calling troops and then fleeing to Varennes, destroyed trust in the monarchy.
  • International dimension: emigres and Pillnitz turned a domestic revolution into a European war (1792), and the war in turn radicalised the Revolution (next class).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Lexington and Concord were one day (19 Apr 1775), and 273 = British casualties, not British dead (73).
  • The war began before the Second Continental Congress met (19 Apr vs 10 May 1775).
  • Yorktown = 1781 (surrender 19 Oct). 1783 = Treaty of Paris; 1784 = its US ratification.
  • Cornwallis was not British commander-in-chief in America. He later became Governor-General of India.
  • Treaty of Paris (Britain-USA) vs Treaties of Versailles (Britain-France/Spain), all signed on 3 Sep 1783. France gained Tobago, not "tobacco".
  • Three treaties of Paris: 1763 (Seven Years' War), 1783 (American war), 1856 (Crimean War †).
  • Declaration of Independence (1776) vs Constitution (1787/1789) vs Bill of Rights (1791).
  • Separation of powers (three branches) is not the same as bicameralism (two houses).
  • Estates-General last met in 1614 and was consultative, not binding. It opened on 5 May 1789.
  • National Assembly (17 Jun) came before the Tennis Court Oath (20 Jun).
  • Active vs passive citizen does not mean "Man" vs "Citizen". It is a voting qualification under the 1791 system.
  • Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789, France) vs Bill of Rights (1791, USA) vs English Bill of Rights (1689) †.
  • Declaration of Pillnitz (1791) was a conditional threat, not a war declaration. The war began in 1792.
  • Napoleon's rule ends in 1815 (Waterloo); he died in 1821. "1819" is wrong.
  • Charter Act 1813 ended the EIC's India trade monopoly; its tea and China monopoly ended in 1833 †.

30-Second Revision

America: the Coercive Acts united the colonies.
  • First Continental Congress (1774): Declaration and Resolves, plus a boycott that cut British exports by about 93%.
  • War began at Lexington and Concord (19 Apr 1775).
  • The Second Congress (1775-81) made Washington commander and sent the Olive Branch Petition, which George III refused.
  • Independence was declared on 4 Jul 1776.
  • Saratoga (1777) brought in France (1778); Spain followed (1779).
  • Yorktown (19 Oct 1781) ended the fighting.
  • The Treaty of Paris (3 Sep 1783) set the Mississippi as the western boundary; France got Tobago and Senegal and Spain the Floridas and Minorca.
  • The Constitution (1787, in force 1789) created a federal, presidential, separated-powers republic; Washington was the first President, and the Bill of Rights followed in 1791.
France: weak Bourbon absolutism, a privileged First and Second Estate (about 2%) over a taxed Third Estate (about 98%), bankruptcy from wars (including America's) and Enlightenment ideas (Voltaire, Montesquieu, Rousseau) combined.
  • The Estates-General met on 5 May 1789.
  • The National Assembly (17 Jun) and the Tennis Court Oath (20 Jun) stand for fraternity.
  • The Bastille (14 Jul) stands for liberty.
  • The 4 August decrees and the Rights of Man (26 Aug) stand for equality.
  • The King was brought to Paris (Oct 1789), fled to Varennes (Jun 1791) and was caught.
  • Austria and Prussia threatened at Pillnitz (Aug 1791).
  • A constitutional monarchy was set up in Sep 1791.
