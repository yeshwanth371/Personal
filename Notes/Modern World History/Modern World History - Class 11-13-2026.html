9.1 The Congress of Vienna (September 1814 - June 1815)

  • Background: Napoleon had redrawn the map of Europe within a few years, and his empire and dependent states covered most of the continent. After his first abdication (April 1814), Louis XVIII became king and France became a constitutional monarchy under the Charter of 1814.
  • Aim: the victors wanted to erase Napoleon and the ideas of the French Revolution from Europe, and to restore peace, order and stability.
  • Who: the four victorious great powers, Austria, Russia, Prussia and Britain, with defeated France admitted later. The congress was hosted and dominated by Austria; the Austrian foreign minister Prince Klemens von Metternich chaired the main meetings.
  • Other leading figures [Additional]: Tsar Alexander I (Russia), Castlereagh (Britain), Hardenberg (Prussia) and Talleyrand (France).
  • When: it met from September 1814. The Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815, nine days before Waterloo.

[Correction] The rough notes list France among the great powers that convened the congress. In fact the four victors (Austria, Russia, Prussia, Britain) met first. Defeated Bourbon France, represented by Talleyrand, was admitted to the inner group later, after Castlereagh and Metternich insisted on it. The congress as a full body of all Europe never actually met: business was done by the great powers in committees. Britannica calls Metternich the congress's "host", so "chaired" is safer than "presided".

9.1.2 Assessment

  • The peacemakers were enemies of the new order: liberalism, nationalism and popular sovereignty. They strengthened the old order (dynastic monarchy, aristocracy, Church) against the hopes of the people. Peoples were transferred like property: Italians and Belgians, Poles and Norwegians. This produced a series of revolutions, because people wanted a new order (9.3).
  • Positive view [Additional]: there was no general European war for about 40 years, until the Crimean War of 1853-56. Many historians see the settlement as a working balance of power and a model of great-power diplomacy.
  • Negative view [Additional]: it ignored nationalism and liberalism, which later undid it: Belgium in 1830, Italy in 1859-70, Germany in 1866-71.
  • These are historians' interpretations, not settled fact.

9.2 The Concert of Europe (1815 - 1850s)

  • What: the powers that made the Vienna settlement agreed to hold regular meetings (congresses) to preserve the peace and order it had created. Hence the "Concert" (acting in concert) or the "Congress System".
  • Basis [Additional]:
    • Quadruple Alliance (20 November 1815 †) of Austria, Russia, Prussia and Britain, which became the Quintuple Alliance when France joined in 1818;
    • Holy Alliance (Paris, 26 September 1815) of Alexander I, Francis I of Austria and Frederick William III of Prussia: Christian monarchs pledged to rule on Christian principles. Britain did not join.
  • Why it weakened: after a few meetings the efforts failed because of the many revolutions in Europe and disagreements among the powers. Britain opposed intervening in other states' internal affairs. Austria, Russia and Prussia (the Troppau Protocol) supported it.
  • End: the Concert broke down when the European powers fought one another in the Crimean War (1853-1856) (9.4.9).

[Correction] The rough notes date the collapse of the Concert to the Crimean War. Historians date the end differently, so it is better to say "weakened in stages":
  • The congress system of regular meetings broke down by 1822-23, when Britain withdrew.
  • Britannica says the Concert in its original form was made obsolete by the revolutions of 1830 and 1848 and the unifications of Italy and Germany.
  • The Crimean War (1853-56) is often taken as the end of the Concert, because the great powers fought each other for the first time since 1815.
  • Ad hoc great-power conferences continued later: Paris in 1856, Berlin in 1878, Berlin in 1884-85.

9.4 Unification of Italy (1815-1870) - the Risorgimento

Risorgimento ("resurgence") is the name for the Italian national movement and unification. It was also the name of Cavour's newspaper, Il Risorgimento, founded in late 1847.

9.4.1 Italy before Napoleon

  • Italy was only a geographical expression: a peninsula divided into many states, with no political unity.
  • The rise of the Italian city-states, the prominence of the Italian language and Italy's prosperity go back to the Renaissance.
  • While nationalist feeling was rising elsewhere in Europe, Italians had strong regional identities, because the Italian states were built around cities: Venice, Genoa, Milan, Florence, Naples.
[Additional] "Italy is a geographical expression": Metternich wrote these words (in French, "L'Italie est une expression geographique") in a diplomatic circular of 2 and 6 August 1847, not in 1815 as exam answers often say. He meant that Italy had no political existence. In practice it was a geographical expression, under Austrian dominance, until 1859.

9.4.2 Italy and Napoleon

  • Napoleon conquered most of Italy and set up a series of small Italian republics and kingdoms that were puppet states of France (see 8.13.2 of the 12 November note).
  • Unintended legacy [Additional]: French rule simplified the map, introduced the Napoleonic Code, uniform administration and the idea of a "Kingdom of Italy" (1805-14), and created a shared resentment of foreign rule.
  • In the early 19th century secret revolutionary societies emerged against French domination. The most important was the Carbonari ("charcoal burners"), whose first lodges were formed in southern Italy in the early 1800s, during the rule of Joachim Murat in Naples (from 1808). Their goal was to end French control.

9.4.4 Italy and the Carbonari

  • After French control ended, the Carbonari turned against the new foreign rulers, with a strongly anti-Austrian tendency.
  • Goals: to free Italy from foreign rulers and to establish constitutional government.
  • Inspired by the Spanish Revolution of 1820 and the July Revolution of 1830, they organised a series of uprisings in Italy:
    • Naples, July 1820: forced Ferdinand I to promise a constitution;
    • Piedmont, March 1821;
    • central Italy, 1831: Modena, Parma, Bologna (Papal States).
  • All were militarily crushed by Austria; the Congress of Laibach, 1821, authorised Austria to intervene (9.2).
  • Why they failed [Additional]:
    • they were secret and elitist, with no mass base;
    • they had no clear common programme (Britannica says their origins and programme are "matters of conjecture");
    • the revolts were local and uncoordinated;
    • they relied on foreign help, which never came;
    • Austria was militarily strong.
  • Though the Carbonari failed, they became a great inspiration for the Risorgimento.

[Correction] The rough notes say the Carbonari were "inspired by Spanish rev of 1830". The Spanish Revolution was in 1820 (9.3), and it inspired the Naples (1820) and Piedmont (1821) risings. The July Revolution of 1830 in France inspired the central Italian risings of 1831.

9.4.5 Giuseppe Mazzini (1805-1872) - the "Soul" of Italian unification

  • Born in Genoa, 22 June 1805; died in Pisa, 10 March 1872.
  • Views: opposed absolute monarchy; a supporter of democracy and republican ideas. He became a revolutionary and joined the Carbonari after graduating in law (1827).
  • 1830: he was betrayed to the police, arrested, and interned at Savona for three months. There he saw the Carbonari's failures: secrecy, no clear aims, reliance on foreign help.
  • Young Italy (Giovine Italia), founded at Marseille in 1831 after his release:
    • goal: a free, unified, democratic Italian republic;
    • means: modern education and political awareness, especially among the youth (members had to be under 40 †), revolutionary ideas spread through the press, and in time a people's war (insurrection by the people themselves, not by foreign armies). His motto was "Thought and action" †.
  • He attempted a rising in Savoy in February 1834, which failed. He then founded Young Europe at Bern (15 April 1834).
  • Exile: Marseille (1831-33), then Switzerland (1833-37), then London from 1837. Though far away, he stayed close to Italians through letters and articles in newspapers.
  • 1848-49: he returned to Italy and became one of the three leaders (triumvirs) of the Roman Republic in 1849 (9.4.7).
  • He inspired the Risorgimento and nationalists abroad. He is called the "Soul" of Italian unification.
[Correction] Three points in the rough notes on Mazzini need fixing:
  • 1830 arrest: he did not take part in an "uprising of 1830" and get imprisoned after it failing. He was betrayed and arrested in 1830 and interned at Savona for three months, then went into exile in early 1831.
  • Savoy rising: this was in February 1834, not 1831. Savoy was itself part of the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia. The invading column from Switzerland broke up and was dispersed.
  • Exile: after Marseille (1831-33) he lived in Switzerland (1833-37) before moving to London (1837). Britain was home until 1848 and again after 1849.
India link [Additional]: Mazzini inspired Indian nationalists.
  • Lala Lajpat Rai wrote a biography of Mazzini in Urdu; a Hindi translation was published in Banaras (3rd edition, 1921). The often-quoted year 1896 for the original [Verification Required].
  • V. D. Savarkar translated Mazzini's writings (his autobiography and political essays) into Marathi with a long introduction, around 1906-07, during his India House years in London.
  • Surendranath Banerjea also lectured on Mazzini †.

[Correction] The rough notes name "VD Satheesan". The Indian revolutionary influenced by Mazzini was Vinayak Damodar (V. D.) Savarkar. V. D. Satheesan is a present-day Kerala politician with no link to Mazzini.

9.4.6 Giuseppe Garibaldi (1807-1882) - the "Sword" of Italian unification

  • Born in Nice (then in the French Empire), 4 July 1807; died on Caprera, 2 June 1882. He was a sailor by profession.
  • He hated absolute monarchy and believed in democratic and republican ideas. He was a revolutionary who joined Young Italy (1833), with the same goal as Mazzini.
  • 1834: while serving in the Piedmontese navy, he took part in a mutiny at Genoa linked to Mazzini's Savoy rising, meant to spark a republican revolution in Piedmont. It failed. He escaped to France and was condemned to death in absentia by a Genoese court.
  • South America (1836-1848): he fought for the rebel republic of Rio Grande do Sul against the Empire of Brazil, and for Uruguay, where in 1843 he commanded the Italian Legion at Montevideo (the "Red Shirts"). There he learnt guerrilla warfare. He became famous as the "Hero of Two Worlds" †.
  • He made the military contribution to the Risorgimento, so he is called the "Sword" of Italian unification.
  • Mazzini and Garibaldi returned to Italy in 1848; Garibaldi in April 1848.

[Correction] The rough notes say Garibaldi first joined the Carbonari, then Young Italy, and revolted "in Piedmont in 1834" before staying in France till 1836. No source opened for these notes says Garibaldi was ever a Carbonaro. His entry into politics was Young Italy (1833), under Mazzini's influence. The 1834 attempt was a naval mutiny at Genoa (part of Piedmont-Sardinia). He sailed for South America in 1835 † and lived there from 1836 to 1848.

9.4.7 1848-49: the First War of Italian Independence and the Roman Republic

  • The February Revolution of 1848 in France set off a revolutionary wave in Europe. Revolts broke out in Sicily (January 1848), Milan (the Five Days, 18-22 March 1848) and Venice (the Republic of San Marco).
  • Charles Albert, King of Piedmont-Sardinia, granted a constitution, the Statuto Albertino (4 March 1848), making Piedmont a constitutional monarchy. It later became the constitution of the Kingdom of Italy, until 1946/48. People began to support him.
  • First War of Italian Independence (1848-49): Piedmont-Sardinia v. Austria. Charles Albert declared war on 23 March 1848.
  • Italians hoped Pope Pius IX, thought to be a liberal, would join. In his Allocution of 29 April 1848 he refused to make war on Catholic Austria.
  • Rome:
    • After the murder of his minister Pellegrino Rossi (November 1848 †), Pius IX fled Rome to Gaeta, in the Bourbon Kingdom of Naples (24-25 November 1848).
    • A constituent assembly proclaimed the Roman Republic (9 February 1849), led by Mazzini (triumvir) and defended by Garibaldi.
    • Louis-Napoleon, president of the French Republic, sent French troops under General Oudinot. They took Rome (July 1849; 3 July †) and restored the Pope.
    • A French garrison stayed in Rome until 1870, apart from a brief withdrawal in 1866-67.
  • Defeat of Piedmont: Charles Albert was beaten at Custoza (25 July 1848 †) and, when the war resumed, decisively at Novara (23 March 1849). He abdicated the same day and died in exile.
  • His son Victor Emmanuel II (King of Sardinia 1849-61; King of Italy 1861-78) became king. He kept the Statuto, so Piedmont was the only Italian state to remain constitutional after 1849 [Additional]. This made it the natural leader of Italian liberals.
[Correction] The rough notes say Charles Albert "requested help of Pope Pius 9", that the Romans then revolted, and that the Pope "escaped to France (ruler Napoleon 3)". The corrected sequence:
  • The Pope refused to join the war against Austria (29 April 1848).
  • He then fled to Gaeta in the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (24-25 November 1848), not to France.
  • The French troops that restored him in 1849 were sent by Louis-Napoleon as President of the Second Republic. He became Emperor Napoleon III only in December 1852.

9.4.8 Count Camillo Benso di Cavour (1810-1861) - the "Brain"

  • Born in Turin, Piedmont, 10 August 1810; died in Turin, 6 June 1861. An intelligent and ambitious liberal politician; he founded Il Risorgimento in 1847.
  • Prime Minister of Piedmont-Sardinia: 4 November 1852 - July 1859 (he resigned in protest at Villafranca), then January 1860 - June 1861.
  • Goal: a free, united Italy under Piedmont-Sardinia, as a constitutional monarchy under Victor Emmanuel II. Unlike Mazzini, he did not want a republic, and he relied on diplomacy and foreign alliances, not a people's war.
  • Domestic preparation:
    • strengthened the economy by encouraging agriculture, modern industry, foreign trade (free-trade treaties), railways and banks [Additional];
    • modernised the army;
    • spread anti-Austrian sentiment;
    • curbed the Church's privileges [Additional].

[Correction] The rough notes give Cavour's premiership as 1852-1861 without a break. He resigned in July 1859 in protest at the armistice of Villafranca (9.4.10) and was returned to power in January 1860. He then held office until his death in June 1861.

9.4.9 The Crimean War (1853-1856) and Piedmont

  • Who: the Ottoman Empire (Turkey) against the Russian Empire of the Romanovs.
  • Allies of the Ottomans: Britain and France (war declared on Russia 28 March 1854). Piedmont-Sardinia joined in January 1855 (alliance of 10 January; accession 26 January 1855).
  • Why: religious and territorial conflicts. Russia claimed to protect the Sultan's Orthodox Christian subjects, and Russia and France disputed the holy places in Palestine. Behind this lay Russia's expansion toward the Straits (9.6.1).
  • When: the Ottomans declared war on 4 October 1853. The main event was the siege of Sevastopol (17 October 1854 - 11 September 1855).
  • Result: Russia was defeated. The Treaty of Paris (30 March 1856) neutralised the Black Sea and opened the Danube to all shipping. Florence Nightingale reformed nursing at the Scutari (Uskudar) hospital [Additional].
  • Gain for Piedmont: Cavour sat at the Congress of Paris (1856) as an equal of the great powers and raised the "Italian question". Piedmont won the friendship of Napoleon III, and the two shared an enemy, Austria.

9.4.10 The Second War of Independence (1859) and the annexation of central Italy (1860)

  • The bargain with France:
    • At a secret meeting at Plombieres (July 1858; 20-21 July †), Napoleon III promised military help if Austria could be made to attack first.
    • Cavour promised Savoy and Nice to France in return. A treaty of alliance followed in January 1859 (26 January †).
    • Cavour then provoked Austria into declaring war (April 1859 †).
  • War of 1859 (Franco-Piedmontese v. Austria): the allies won at Magenta (4 June 1859) and Solferino (24 June 1859). The carnage at Solferino moved Henri Dunant to found the Red Cross (1863) [Additional] †.
  • Napoleon III quit the war: he signed an armistice with Emperor Francis Joseph at Villafranca (11 July 1859) without consulting Cavour. His reasons:
    • heavy losses for little benefit;
    • French Catholics opposed a war that threatened the Pope;
    • fear of a Prussian attack on the Rhine (Prussia was mobilising) [Additional];
    • he did not want too strong an Italy on France's border [Additional].
  • Result: Piedmont got Lombardy (confirmed by the Treaty of Zurich, 10 November 1859). Austria kept Venetia.
  • Central Italy: the people of Tuscany, Parma, Modena and the Romagna (the northern Papal Legations) drove out their rulers and voted by plebiscites (March 1860) to join Piedmont.
  • The price: France agreed only when Cavour ceded Savoy and Nice by the Treaty of Turin (24 March 1860). Garibaldi, a native of Nice, never forgave this.
  • By 1860 Cavour controlled most of northern and central Italy, except Venetia (and Rome with Lazio).
[Correction] The rough notes date the second war 1859-61 and say Cavour "conquered Lombardy, established control over Parma, Modena, Tuscany, Papal States". The corrected picture:
  • The war lasted April-July 1859.
  • Lombardy was won by the French and Piedmontese armies.
  • Parma, Modena, Tuscany and only the Romagna part of the Papal States joined through popular revolts and plebiscites (March 1860), not conquest.
  • The rest of the Papal States (the Marche and Umbria) was taken in September 1860 (9.4.11). Rome itself was taken in 1870.
  • Savoy and Nice were promised at Plombieres (1858) but actually ceded in March 1860, as the price of central Italy.

9.4.11 Garibaldi and the Expedition of the Thousand (1860)

  • Who and why: a revolt in Sicily (April 1860) gave Garibaldi his chance. Mazzinian democrats pressed for action, and Garibaldi agreed to fight under the slogan "Italy and Victor Emmanuel" †, accepting the Savoy monarchy's leadership.
  • The Thousand (the "Red Shirts", just over 1,000 volunteers):
    • sailed from Quarto near Genoa (night of 5-6 May 1860);
    • landed at Marsala (11 May);
    • won at Calatafimi (15 May);
    • took Palermo (6 June) and the rest of Sicily;
    • crossed to the mainland and entered Naples (7 September 1860);
    • beat the Bourbon army on the Volturno (1-2 October).
    The Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies collapsed. Plebiscites (October 1860) joined it to Piedmont.
  • Cavour's response: he feared Garibaldi would march on Rome, provoke France and set up a republic. So he sent the Piedmontese army through the Papal Marche and Umbria; it won at Castelfidardo (18 September 1860 †). Victor Emmanuel met Garibaldi at Teano (26 October 1860), and Garibaldi handed over the south and retired to Caprera.
  • So Cavour unified most of Italy through diplomacy and warfare, using others' energies. He is called the "Brain" behind unification.

[Correction] The rough notes say Cavour "convinced Garibaldi to accept Piedmont-Sardinia's leadership" and that "they launched" the expedition together. In fact Garibaldi acted on his own initiative. Cavour was ambivalent: he did not stop the expedition, but he did not launch it, and he feared it. Garibaldi accepted the king's leadership in his slogan and finally at Teano. Cavour's own move was the invasion of the Marche and Umbria, to head off Garibaldi.

9.4.12 The Kingdom of Italy (1861)

  • Cavour held elections, and the first Italian Parliament met in Turin on 18 February 1861.
  • A law of 17 March 1861 proclaimed Victor Emmanuel II King of Italy.
  • Capitals: Turin (1861), then Florence (1865), then Rome (1871).
  • Cavour died on 6 June 1861, of illness, a few months later.

[Correction] The rough notes say the Italian Parliament was established in Florence (Tuscany) in 1861. It met in Turin, the Piedmontese capital, on 18 February 1861. The capital moved to Florence only in 1865 (approved 1864; the king moved there on 3 February 1865), and to Rome in 1871.

9.4.13 Venetia (1866) and Rome (1870)

  • 1866, Venetia:
    • Victor Emmanuel II and Bismarck of Prussia, who was leading German unification, made an alliance (8 April 1866) to fight Austria on two fronts.
    • Austro-Prussian War (Seven Weeks' War), 1866: Italy declared war on 20 June. Italy itself lost at Custoza (24 June) on land and Lissa (20 July) at sea. Prussia crushed Austria at Koniggratz (Sadowa), 3 July 1866.
    • Austria and Prussia made peace at Prague (23 August 1866). By the Peace of Vienna (3 October 1866) Austria ceded Venetia to Napoleon III, who handed it to Italy. A plebiscite followed (21 October 1866).
  • 1870, Rome:
    • Garibaldi's attempts on Rome failed in 1862 (Aspromonte †) and 1867 (Mentana †); the French garrison came back.
    • When the Franco-Prussian War (1870) broke out, Napoleon III withdrew the French troops from Rome (August 1870).
    • Italian troops entered Rome through the Porta Pia on 20 September 1870. A plebiscite (2 October 1870) joined Rome and Lazio to Italy, and Rome became the capital in 1871.
    • The Pope refused to recognise this and called himself the "prisoner of the Vatican" †. The Roman Question was settled only by the Lateran Treaty, 1929 [Additional] †.
  • Thus the unification of Italy was completed in 1870, as a constitutional monarchy under Victor Emmanuel II of the House of Savoy. It came through the inspiration, efforts and strategies of Mazzini, Garibaldi and Cavour: the Soul, the Sword and the Brain.

[Correction] The rough notes say Austria "signed Treaty of Prague, 1866. With this Venetia given to Italy". The Peace of Prague (23 August 1866) was between Austria and Prussia. Venetia came through the separate Peace of Vienna (3 October 1866) between Austria and Italy, via Napoleon III, because Austria refused to cede it directly to Italy, which had lost the battles. Note also that "completed in 1870" leaves out Trentino and Trieste ("Italia irredenta", unredeemed Italy). They stayed Austrian until 1918 and were formally annexed by the Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919).

9.5 Unification of Germany (1815-1871) - part 1, to 1849

The Bismarck phase (1862-1871: "blood and iron", the wars with Denmark, Austria and France, and the German Empire of 18 January 1871) was not taught today. It is expected in the next class.

9.5.1 Germany before Napoleon

  • Until the early 19th century central Europe was politically fragmented: over 300 separate territories, bound together loosely in the Holy Roman Empire. These were kingdoms, electorates, duchies, ecclesiastical states and free cities.
  • Habsburg Austria was the most powerful state and the leader of the Empire; the Habsburgs were almost always elected emperor. Prussia (the Hohenzollerns) was the second most powerful and Austria's rival.
  • Because of this fragmentation, the people of the Empire lacked nationalist feeling; loyalty was to the local ruler. German cultural unity (language, literature) existed among the educated [Additional].

[Correction] The rough notes speak of "approximately 300 independent, sovereign kingdoms". The territories were not all kingdoms, and not fully sovereign: they were principalities, ecclesiastical states, free cities and imperial knights under the Emperor. The count of about 300 or more held only before 1803. In 1803 (the Imperial Recess, under French pressure) 112 smaller territories were absorbed into larger states, and the count fell sharply.

9.5.2 Germany and Napoleon

  • After his victories at Ulm and Austerlitz (1805), Napoleon took most of the Holy Roman Empire's territory and reorganised it into the Confederation of the Rhine (July 1806; 12 July †). Francis II abdicated and dissolved the Holy Roman Empire on 6 August 1806.
  • French domination provoked German nationalism and a common German identity:
    • Johann Gottlieb Fichte's Addresses to the German Nation (Berlin, 1807-08);
    • the Prussian reforms (Stein, Hardenberg, Scharnhorst) [Additional];
    • the War of Liberation (1813).
  • After Napoleon's disastrous Russian campaign (1812) and his defeat at Leipzig (October 1813), the Confederation of the Rhine collapsed.
  • Legacy [Additional]: Napoleon reduced hundreds of states to a few dozen, which made later unification easier.

[Correction] The rough notes say "German rulers encouraged nationalistic sentiments against the French control". In fact the princes of the Confederation of the Rhine were mostly Napoleon's allies: Bavaria, Wurttemberg and Saxony owed their royal titles to him. The new national feeling came mainly from intellectuals, students and Prussian reformers (Fichte, Arndt, Jahn †) and from the War of Liberation of 1813. Prussia's king joined the war only under pressure.

9.5.3 Germany and the Congress of Vienna: the German Confederation (1815)

  • The congress did not restore the Holy Roman Empire. It replaced the Confederation of the Rhine with a new German Confederation (Deutscher Bund), created on 8 June 1815.
  • Membership: 39 German states, including Austria and Prussia, but only for their lands that had been in the old Empire. Hungary, Galicia, East Prussia and Posen were outside it. The number varied over time, so 38 or 41 also appear.
  • Some foreign kings were members through German lands [Additional] †: the King of Britain for Hanover (until 1837), the King of Denmark for Holstein, and the King of the Netherlands for Luxembourg.
  • Austria was the political leader. It presided over the Federal Diet at Frankfurt, through its envoy. Metternich, Austria's chancellor, directed this policy.
  • Austria preferred a divided Germany, which was easier to control.
  • Federal Diet (Bundestag), Frankfurt: not a legislature, but a permanent congress of envoys of the governments. It handled common defence and foreign affairs, so that the German states could speak with one voice in European policy.
  • Under Metternich it became an instrument of repression [Additional]. The Carlsbad Decrees (20 September 1819) censored the press, supervised universities and banned the student societies (Burschenschaften).

[Correction] The rough notes say "Prince Metternich was its President". The presidency of the Federal Diet belonged to Austria as a state and was held by the Austrian envoy at Frankfurt. Metternich, as Austrian foreign minister and later chancellor, directed the Confederation's policy from Vienna. He was not personally its president.

9.5.4 Prussia, the Zollverein and economic unification

  • The Congress of Vienna gave Prussia new territories in the west: the Rhineland, with the Moselle valley (Trier, the Saar), and Westphalia, with the Ruhr. These were not contiguous with the old Prussian lands in the east (Map 4).
  • The problem: political fragmentation, different policies and many customs barriers discouraged trade between the German states.
  • Prussian Tariff Law (1818; 26 May †) under Frederick William III:
    • it abolished internal tolls and created one customs area for all Prussian territories, east and west, with a moderate external tariff;
    • neighbouring states were invited to join and share the economic benefits, one treaty at a time.
  • Result: the Zollverein (German Customs Union), in force from 1834 (1 January †), with 18 states.
    • It encouraged trade and the growth of modern industries such as railways. The first German line, Nuremberg to Furth, opened on 7 December 1835.
    • It brought great prosperity to its members.
    • The economist Friedrich List championed it and a national railway system [Additional].
  • Effects:
    • Prussia became the economic leader of the German Confederation. Austria was deliberately excluded, and its protectionist economy could not easily join.
    • Economic cooperation succeeded and membership grew. An economically united Germany created the hope of a politically united Germany.
    • So the Zollverein is the first milestone in German unification: J. M. Keynes later wrote (1919) that the German Empire was built "more truly on coal and iron than on blood and iron" †.
[Correction] The rough notes say Frederick William III "announced Custom free zone" and that this "resulted in the Zollverein 1834". More precisely:
  • The 1818 Prussian Tariff Law made Prussia's own territories one customs area. It was free of internal duties but had a common external tariff, so it was not a "customs-free zone" in the modern sense.
  • The Zollverein grew from separate treaties between Prussia and other states, including rival unions in the south and centre that merged.
  • It came into force in 1834 with 18 states.

9.5.5 1848 and the Frankfurt Parliament (1848-49)

  • The February Revolution of 1848 in France set off a wave of revolutions in Europe. In Germany there were the March revolutions in Vienna, Berlin and the smaller states, and many German rulers adopted constitutions under popular pressure.
  • Prussia: after street fighting in Berlin (18 March 1848), Frederick William IV promised a constitution. In his proclamation of 21 March 1848 he declared that "Prussia henceforth merges into Germany".
  • Prussia became a constitutional monarchy through a constitution the king imposed in December 1848 (5 December †), revised in January 1850. It kept a weighted three-class franchise [Additional] †.
  • The parliament: a liberal Pre-Parliament (Vorparlament) and the Federal Diet called elections across the German states. The elected representatives formed the Frankfurt Parliament (Frankfurt National Assembly).
    • It opened on 18 May 1848 in St Paul's Church (Paulskirche).
    • Most members were professors, lawyers and officials: the "parliament of professors" [Additional].
  • Its work: it debated a "Greater Germany" (including Austria) against a "Smaller Germany" (excluding Austria, led by Prussia) [Additional]. It chose the smaller solution and adopted the Imperial Constitution (28 March 1849):
    • a federal united Germany;
    • a hereditary emperor with limited powers;
    • an elected parliament;
    • basic rights.
  • The crown was offered to Frederick William IV (3 April 1849), and he refused it. His reasons:
    1. it was offered by the representatives of the people, not by the German princes. He reportedly called it a crown "from the gutter" in a private letter [Verification Required];
    2. he believed in rule by divine right and wanted to remain an autocrat;
    3. [Additional] accepting would have meant war with Austria (and possibly Russia), which Prussia was not ready for.
  • Failure: the parliament collapsed. Austria and Prussia recalled their deputies, and the rump that moved to Stuttgart was dispersed on 18 June 1849.
  • Lesson [Additional]: German unity would not come from liberals and speeches but from Prussian power. That is Bismarck's "blood and iron" (1862), the topic for the next class.

[Correction] The rough notes say Frederick William IV "suggested people to create a constitution, assemble, draft a constitution and unify Germany", and that elections followed. The elections to the Frankfurt Parliament were called by the liberal Pre-Parliament and the Federal Diet, not by the Prussian king. Frederick William IV made vague promises to lead Germany (21 March 1848), but he did not initiate the Frankfurt Parliament.

9.6 Topics requested in class [Additional]

The rough notes asked for the Eastern Question, the Far Eastern Question and the Scramble for Africa to be added. These are summary treatments for revision. Each will probably be taught in detail later, with the World War I, imperialism and China/Japan classes.

9.6.1 The Eastern Question (c. 1774 - 1923)

  • What: the diplomatic problem created by the decline of the Ottoman Empire. The questions were:
    • who would gain from its weakness in the Balkans, the Black Sea Straits (the Bosporus and Dardanelles) and the Near East;
    • how the great powers would stop one another gaining too much.
  • Interests of the powers:
    • Russia wanted warm-water access through the Straits and posed as protector of the Orthodox Christians and the Slavs (Pan-Slavism).
    • Austria(-Hungary) feared Slav nationalism and wanted the western Balkans.
    • Britain wanted to keep Russia away from the Mediterranean and the route to India, especially after the Suez Canal (1869). So Britain usually propped up the Ottomans.
    • France had interests in the Levant and the holy places.
    • The Balkan nationalities (Greeks, Serbs, Romanians, Bulgarians) wanted independence.
  • "Sick man": in January 1853 Tsar Nicholas I told the British ambassador Seymour that the Ottoman Empire was "a sick man - a very sick man". The words "of Europe" were added later.

9.6.2 The Far Eastern Question (c. 1839 - 1910)

  • What: the rivalry of the Western powers, and later Japan, over a weak Qing China, Korea and Manchuria, and more broadly over interests in "farther Asia and the Pacific". It covered markets, treaty ports, concessions, leased bases, railways and spheres of influence.
  • Stages:
    • Opium Wars:
      • First Opium War (1839-42), Britain against China, ended by the Treaty of Nanking (29 August 1842). China ceded Hong Kong Island and opened five treaty ports (Canton, Amoy, Foochow, Ningpo, Shanghai) instead of Canton alone.
      • Second Opium War (1856-60), Britain and France against China, ended by the Treaties of Tientsin (June 1858) and the Convention of Peking (October 1860). These opened more ports and ceded Kowloon.
      • These were the first "unequal treaties".
    • Japan rises:
      • The First Sino-Japanese War (war declared 1 August 1894) ended with the Treaty of Shimonoseki (17 April 1895). China recognised Korea's independence and ceded Taiwan, the Pescadores and the Liaodong Peninsula.
      • The Triple Intervention of Russia, Germany and France (23 April 1895) forced Japan to give back Liaodong.
    • "Scramble for concessions" (1897-98):
      • Germany leased Kiaochow (Jiaozhou) with Tsingtao, occupied in November 1897 (99 years);
      • Russia leased Port Arthur (Lushun) and Dalian (25 years);
      • Britain leased Weihaiwei and the New Territories of Hong Kong (99 years);
      • France leased Guangzhouwan (99 years) †.
    • Open Door policy (USA): Secretary of State John Hay's notes of 6 September 1899 (equal trade for all) and 3 July 1900 (China's "territorial and administrative integrity").
    • Boxer Rebellion (1899-1901): an anti-foreign rising, crushed by an eight-nation force. The Boxer Protocol (7 September 1901) imposed an indemnity.
    • Japan becomes a great power:
      • Anglo-Japanese Alliance (30 January 1902);
      • Russo-Japanese War (8 February 1904 - 5 September 1905), ended by the Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905; 23 August Old Style);
      • Japan annexed Korea (treaty signed 22 August 1910, published 29 August 1910).
  • Significance: Japan's defeat of a European power in 1905 electrified Asian nationalists. China's humiliation led to the Revolution of 1911 †.
India link [Additional]:
  • The opium that caused the Opium Wars was Indian. The East India Company held a monopoly of opium cultivation in Bengal, and Malwa opium (western Madhya Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan) was exported through Bombay.
  • Opium revenue paid for Britain's tea imports from China. This was the "triangular trade" of Britain, India and China †.
  • Indian sepoys fought in the Opium Wars and against the Boxers †.
  • Japan's victory over Russia in 1905 inspired Indian nationalists during the Swadeshi Movement †.

9.6.3 The Scramble for Africa (c. 1881 - 1914)

  • What: the rapid partition of Africa by European powers. In the 1870s only about 10% of Africa was under European control. By 1914 about 90% was, and only Ethiopia and Liberia stayed independent.
  • Causes (New Imperialism) [Additional]:
    • Economic: raw materials (rubber, cotton, palm oil, minerals, the gold and diamonds of South Africa), markets and investment outlets. This is the explanation of J. A. Hobson (Imperialism: A Study, 1902) and Lenin (Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism, written 1916, published 1917).
    • Political and strategic: national prestige (Germany and Italy after unification; France after 1871), naval bases, and the route to India.
    • Ideological: Social Darwinism, the "civilising mission" and "white man's burden", and missionaries.
    • Enabling factors: quinine, steamships, railways, the telegraph and the Maxim gun.
    Historians disagree on which cause was decisive; this is an interpretive debate.
  • Berlin (West Africa) Conference, 15 November 1884 - 26 February 1885: Bismarck issued the invitations, prompted especially by Portugal's claim to the Congo estuary.
    • It recognised Leopold II's Congo Free State, a personal possession, which required free trade.
    • It declared the Congo and Niger rivers free for navigation.
    • It laid down the principle of "effective occupation" for claims to the African coast.
    • These terms were set out in the General Act (26 February 1885).
    • No African ruler was represented.
  • Flashpoints:
    • Ethiopia under Menelik II defeated Italy at Adwa (1 March 1896);
    • the Fashoda incident (1898) between Britain and France on the Nile ended in a French climb-down. It set Britain's Cape-to-Cairo plan against France's west-to-east plan, and was followed by the Entente of 1904;
    • the South African (Second Boer) War (1899-1902);
    • the Moroccan crises of 1905-06 (Tangier) and 1911 (Agadir), steps toward World War I.
  • Consequences: artificial borders cut across ethnic groups, economies were built on exporting a few products, and atrocities followed, such as Leopold's Congo and the Herero genocide in German South West Africa (1904-08) †. These shaped Africa's post-colonial problems.
India link [Additional]:
  • M. K. Gandhi went to South Africa in 1893 as a lawyer for an Indian firm. He was thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg †, developed satyagraha there, ran the Phoenix Settlement near Durban, and returned to India in 1914-15.
  • Indian indentured labour and traders went to Natal and East Africa, where Indian labour built the Uganda Railway †.
  • Indian troops served in Britain's African wars †.

Mains Points

  • Vienna - assessment: it kept a balance of power and a long peace (1815-1853), but it was reactionary. It ignored nationalism and liberalism, and these destroyed it in 1830, 1848 and 1859-71. It "turned the clock back", but not completely: Napoleon's simplified map of Germany was largely kept.
  • Concert of Europe: an early experiment in great-power consultation, a precursor of the League and the UN. It was divided between intervention (Austria, Russia, Prussia) and non-intervention (Britain).
  • Why Italy unified under Piedmont:
    • the only native dynasty;
    • a liberal constitution kept after 1849;
    • economic and military modernisation;
    • Cavour's diplomacy (Crimea, Plombieres);
    • French help in 1859 and Prussian victories in 1866 and 1870;
    • Garibaldi's conquest of the South;
    • plebiscites gave legitimacy.
    Mazzini's republican and popular route failed in 1848-49.
  • Role of external factors in Italy: Italy was made as much by France (1859) and Prussia (1866, 1870) as by Italians. "Italy was made by others' wars" is a useful thesis to evaluate critically.
  • Limits of Italian unification: Piedmontisation of the South, which brought brigandage and the North-South divide; the Roman Question until 1929; Italia irredenta (Trentino and Trieste); a narrow franchise. "We have made Italy; now we must make Italians" (attributed to d'Azeglio †).
  • Germany to 1849: cultural nationalism (Fichte), Napoleon's simplification, the Zollverein's economic unity and Prussian leadership prepared the ground. 1848 showed that liberal idealism without power could not overcome the princes or Austria. That was the lesson Bismarck drew.
  • Italy and Germany compared:
    • both had a leading state: Piedmont and Prussia;
    • both had a statesman: Cavour and Bismarck;
    • both fought Austria;
    • both used diplomacy plus war.
    But Germany had the Zollverein's economic base, while Italy depended more on foreign help.
  • Eastern Question: Ottoman decline combined with Balkan nationalism and great-power rivalry (Russia, Austria-Hungary, Britain) to make the Balkans the "powder keg" of Europe. The line runs Berlin 1878, Bosnia 1908, the Balkan Wars, Sarajevo 1914.
  • Far Eastern Question: China's semi-colonisation through unequal treaties, against Japan's rapid rise. Indian opium was the starting point of the Opium Wars.
  • Africa: borders were drawn by "accidents of European competition" (UPSC GS-I 2013). The economic, strategic and ideological causes, and their weight, are a historians' debate (Hobson and Lenin against strategic and "official mind" explanations).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • France at Vienna was admitted later; it was not a convener. Borders of 1792 against 1790: the first Treaty of Paris gave 1792, the second gave 1790.
  • Holy Alliance against Quadruple Alliance: the Holy Alliance was ideological (Britain stayed out); the Quadruple Alliance was a military guarantee (Britain was in).
  • Metternich's "geographical expression" dates from 1847, not 1815.
  • Kingdom of Sardinia = Piedmont-Sardinia, ruled from Turin. The island merely gave the royal title.
  • The Pope fled to Gaeta (Naples), not France. Louis-Napoleon restored him as president (1849), not as emperor.
  • Peace of Prague (Austria-Prussia) against the Peace of Vienna (Austria-Italy, Venetia), both 1866. Don't confuse either with the Congress of Vienna (1815).
  • Treaty of Paris 1856 (Crimean War) against the Treaties of Paris 1814/1815 (Napoleon).
  • Italy's first parliament: Turin, not Florence. Florence became the capital in 1865.
  • Savoy and Nice were promised in 1858 but ceded in 1860 (Treaty of Turin). Garibaldi was born in Nice, Mazzini in Genoa, Cavour in Turin.
  • Garibaldi was not a Carbonaro (on available evidence). Mazzini was.
  • "Completed in 1870": Trentino and Trieste were added only after World War I.
  • Zollverein against German Confederation: the Zollverein was economic, led by Prussia, without Austria. The Confederation was political, led by Austria.
  • The Frankfurt Parliament was called by the Pre-Parliament, not by the Prussian king. The king refused the crown.
  • Congress of Berlin (1878) was about the Balkans. The Berlin Conference (1884-85) was about Africa. Bismarck hosted both.
  • Treaty of Nanking (1842) was Britain and China, ending the First Opium War. The Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895) was Japan and China. The Treaty of Portsmouth (1905) was Japan and Russia, mediated by the USA.
  • Cyprus went to Britain by a separate convention in 1878, not by the Berlin treaty.
  • V. D. Savarkar, not V. D. Satheesan, translated Mazzini.

30-Second Revision

  • Vienna 1815: the victors redrew Europe by balance, legitimacy and compensation. The Concert policed it, and the revolutions of 1820, 1830 and 1848 challenged it.
  • Italy: divided in 1815 under Austria. The Carbonari failed; Mazzini (Young Italy) inspired; 1848-49 failed but left Piedmont's Statuto. Cavour went from Crimea to Plombieres to 1859 (Lombardy) to 1860 (the centre). Garibaldi took the South. The Kingdom came in 1861, Venetia in 1866, Rome in 1870.
  • Germany to 1849: the HRE (300+ territories) became the Confederation of the Rhine (1806), then the German Confederation (1815). The Zollverein (1834) was the first milestone. The Frankfurt Parliament (1848-49) failed when the crown was refused. Next: Bismarck.
  • Requested: the Eastern Question (the Ottoman "sick man": Greece, Crimea, Berlin 1878, Bosnia 1908); the Far Eastern Question (Opium Wars, Shimonoseki, concessions, Open Door, Japan rises); the Scramble for Africa (Berlin 1884-85; 90% partitioned by 1914; Ethiopia and Liberia free).

1. UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - GS Paper I, Q12 (10 marks, 200 words)

"Africa was chopped into States artificially created by accidents of European competition." Analyze.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • The Scramble for Africa (1881-1914): from 10% to 90% under European control. The Berlin Conference rules (effective occupation, no African voice) (9.6.3; Map 7).
  • Borders were drawn on maps along rivers, lines of latitude and bargains (for example Fashoda): they split peoples (the Somali, Ewe, Maasai) and joined rivals.
  • Balance: some borders followed earlier African states or the colonial administration on the ground. The OAU kept the colonial borders (1964) †.
  • Consequences: secession and civil wars, irredentism.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2016 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(b) (10 marks)

What is Metternich system? Assess its impact on Europe.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Define: the conservative order of 1815-48. It rested on the Vienna principles, the Concert and intervention (Troppau), and repression through the Carlsbad Decrees and censorship (9.1, 9.2, 9.5.3).
  • Impact: peace among the powers; suppression of the Carbonari and the German students; Austrian dominance over Italy and Germany.
  • Collapse: 1830 and 1848, when Metternich fled (9.3).

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2019 - History Optional, Paper II, Q8(a) (20 marks)

Do you subscribe to the view that the Greek War of Independence was mired in contrasts of the best and the worst episodes? How did it affect the Concert of Europe?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:

  • Worst: massacres on both sides (Chios, Tripolitsa †).
  • Concert: the powers first opposed rebellion, then intervened (Navarino 1827). This showed national interest overriding legitimacy, and Britain and Russia diverging from Austria (9.6.1, 9.2).

Corrections Made

  1. c01 - Vienna was convened by the four victors; France was admitted later; Metternich hosted rather than presided.
  2. c02 - France was cut to its 1790 borders by the second Treaty of Paris (1815), not 1792.
  3. c03 - The Concert weakened in stages (1822-23, 1830, 1848); the Crimean War is one dating of its end.
  4. c04 - The July Revolution did not cause a Portuguese revolt; it inspired Belgium, Poland, Italy and Germany.
  5. c05 - Piedmont was the core of the Kingdom of Sardinia; Genoa (Liguria) was added.
  6. c06 - The Spanish Revolution was in 1820, not 1830.
  7. c07 - Mazzini was arrested and interned in 1830; the Savoy rising was in 1834; Switzerland 1833-37, then London.
  8. c08 - V. D. Savarkar, not V. D. Satheesan.
  9. c09 - Garibaldi was not a Carbonaro; the 1834 attempt was a mutiny at Genoa; South America 1836-48.
  10. c10 - The Pope refused the war, fled to Gaeta (not France), and was restored by President Louis-Napoleon.
  11. c11 - Cavour resigned in July 1859 and returned in January 1860.
  12. c12 - The 1859 war won Lombardy; the centre joined by plebiscites in 1860; Savoy and Nice were ceded in 1860.
  13. c13 - Garibaldi launched the Thousand himself; Cavour was ambivalent and invaded the Marche and Umbria.
  14. c14 - The first Italian Parliament met in Turin; Florence became the capital only in 1865.
  15. c15 - Venetia came by the Peace of Vienna (3 October 1866), not Prague; unification was not complete until 1918-19.
  16. c16 - The Holy Roman Empire had 300+ territories (not all kingdoms, not sovereign), and only until 1803.
  17. c17 - German nationalism came from intellectuals and reformers, not the princes allied to Napoleon.
  18. c18 - Austria (through its envoy) presided over the Diet; Metternich was not personally president.
  19. c19 - The 1818 Prussian Tariff Law created a single customs area, not a "customs-free zone"; the Zollverein grew by treaties.
  20. c20 - The Frankfurt Parliament was called by the Pre-Parliament and the Diet, not by Frederick William IV.
