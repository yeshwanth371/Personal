12. The Russian Revolutions (1905-1922) - continued

12.1 Russia under the Romanovs (to c. 1900)

The Romanov dynasty ruled Russia from 1613 (Michael Romanov) until the abdication of Nicholas II in March 1917, a little over 300 years. Its rulers took the title Tsar (from the Latin "Caesar"); from Peter the Great (1721) the formal title was Emperor.

12.1.1 Polity

  • The Tsars were autocrats who claimed to rule by the divine right of kings. There was no parliament, no constitution and no legal political party until 1905-06.
  • The Tsars of the period: Alexander I (1801-1825), Nicholas I (1825-1855), Alexander II (1855-1881), Alexander III (1881-1894) and Nicholas II (1894-1917) (Figure 1).
  • Capital: the old capital Moscow was replaced by St Petersburg, the city founded by Peter the Great in 1703; the court and government moved there in 1712 †. St Petersburg was renamed Petrograd in 1914 (to sound less German) and Leningrad in 1924. The Bolsheviks moved the capital back to Moscow in March 1918.
  • Territorial expansion was a constant aim of the Tsars (the notes' "first duty"): westward into Poland and the Baltic, south towards the Black Sea, the Caucasus and the Ottoman Straits, and east across Siberia to the Pacific and into Central Asia. A recurring motive was the search for a warm-water port free of ice in winter. [Additional]

[Correction] The notes give Nicholas II's dates as "1814-1917". Nicholas II was born in 1868 and reigned 1894-1917; he abdicated on 2 / 15 March 1917 and was shot with his family on 16-17 July 1918.

12.1.2 Society

  • Russia had a feudal society, but Russian serfdom was unusually harsh, close to bonded or forced labour. Serfs belonged to the landlord or the state, could be sold or flogged, and had no land of their own, no political power and no basic rights. Serfdom lasted until 1861, long after it had ended in western Europe.
  • Russia was a multi-ethnic empire. The Slavs formed the majority (Great Russians, Ukrainians or "Little Russians", Belarusians, Poles). The minorities included Poles, Tatars, Mongol peoples (Buryats, Kalmyks), Finns, Balts, Jews, Georgians, Armenians and the Muslim peoples of the Caucasus and Central Asia.
  • The state religion was Orthodox Christianity, and the Tsar was its protector. Russia's role as the protector of Orthodox Christians in the Ottoman Empire led to repeated religious and territorial conflicts with the Ottomans.
  • Western Europe regarded Russia as a backward country with an under-developed society.

[Correction] The notes say Russians "practised Greek Catholic Christianity" in a church "headed by the Patriarch of Constantinople". This is not correct for the period. The Russian church was the Russian Orthodox Church, part of Eastern Orthodoxy. It had been self-governing (autocephalous) since 1448, and its head became the Patriarch of Moscow in 1589. In 1721 Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate and put the church under a Holy Synod supervised by a lay official, the chief procurator, making it in effect a department of the state. The patriarchate was restored only in 1917. "Greek Catholic" means the Eastern-rite churches in union with Rome (for example, in Ukraine), a different body.

[Correction] The notes say "minorities happened to be the majority in the Russian provinces". The clearer statement is this: in the first census of the empire (1897, population 125.6 million), the Great Russians were only about 44% of the population †, so non-Russians together were a majority of the empire, and in many border provinces (Poland, Finland, the Baltic, the Caucasus, Central Asia) the local people were the majority. This is why Russification under Alexander III and nationalist unrest later mattered. The "Ruthenians" of the notes are the Ukrainians (and Rusyns).

12.1.3 Economy

  • An agrarian economy with medieval industries; the great majority were peasants and serfs (about 85% of the people were peasants around 1900 †).
  • A harsh climate, especially long winters: the farming season lasted only about 4-6 months, so yields were low and peasants were poor and often not self-sufficient. Famines recurred (for example, 1891-92).
  • The Industrial Revolution came late, from the 1860s and especially the 1890s, so Russia historically lacked both a strong class of industrialists (a bourgeoisie) and a large working class.

12.2 The Tsars, 1801-1917: reform and reaction

12.2.1 Alexander I (1801-1825)

  • Tsar during the Napoleonic era. He played an important part in Napoleon's fall: the French invasion of Russia in 1812 failed, and Russian armies advanced to Paris in 1814.
  • He took part in the Congress of Vienna (1814-15), gained most of Poland (the "Congress Kingdom", Map 1) and proposed the Holy Alliance (1815) of Christian monarchs. [Additional]
  • His early reign promised reform; his later years were reactionary. His sudden death in December 1825 set off the Decembrist revolt of liberal army officers. [Additional]

12.2.2 Nicholas I (1825-1855) and the Crimean War

  • He came to the throne by crushing the Decembrists (December 1825) and ruled in a turbulent time of revolutions in Europe (1830, 1848). He was determined to remain an autocrat; his motto was "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality" †.
  • To protect autocracy he controlled the press (censorship) and education (restrictions on universities and students), and created a secret political police with very wide powers, the Third Section of His Imperial Majesty's Own Chancellery (1826) †.
  • He aimed to dominate the Black Sea and the Ottoman Straits. In the process Russia helped the Greek War of Independence: with Britain and France it destroyed the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at Navarino (1827), and after the Russo-Turkish War of 1828-29 the Treaty of Adrianople (1829) forced the Ottomans to accept Greek autonomy; Greece became independent in 1830 (London Protocol) †. Russian influence in the Balkans grew.
  • Crimean War (1853-1856): a dispute over the protection of Orthodox Christians and the Holy Places, and Russian occupation of the Danubian Principalities (July 1853), led to war with the Ottoman Empire, Britain, France and (from 1855) Sardinia-Piedmont. The Allies besieged Sevastopol in the Crimea (1854-55) (Map 2).
  • Nicholas I died of illness (pneumonia) in March 1855, during the war.

[Correction] The notes say "Russia invaded Romania". In 1853 there was no Romania yet: Russia occupied the Danubian Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia, autonomous provinces under Ottoman suzerainty. They united as Romania in 1859-62 and became independent only in 1878.

12.2.3 Alexander II (1855-1881), the "Tsar Liberator"

  • His reign began with disaster: defeat in the Crimean War. By the Treaty of Paris (30 March 1856) the Black Sea was neutralised: Russia could keep no warships or naval arsenals there, so it lost control of the Black Sea. [Additional: date and term †]
  • He decided to end Russia's backwardness and modernise it (the "Great Reforms"):
    1. Abolition of serfdom: the Emancipation Manifesto of 19 February / 3 March 1861 freed about 23 million privately owned serfs †. Peasants received land but had to pay redemption payments for 49 years, and the land usually went to the village commune (mir), not to individuals.
    2. Local self-government: elected zemstvos (district and provincial councils, 1864) and town councils (1870).
    3. Judicial reform (1864): independent courts, trial by jury, public trials.
    4. Military reform: universal military service and a shorter term of service (1874, War Minister Milyutin) †.
    5. Encouragement of modern education (universities given more autonomy, 1863) and relaxed censorship (1865) †.
    6. Railway building: the railway network grew rapidly in the 1860s-70s, which led to the beginnings of industrialisation and of an industrial working class.
  • Regaining the Black Sea: in October 1870, while France was at war with Prussia, Russia repudiated the Black Sea clauses of 1856, and the London Conference of 1871 accepted this †.
  • Supporter of Balkan nationalism (Pan-Slavism): after the Russo-Turkish War of 1877-78, the Treaty of San Stefano (3 March 1878) created a "Big Bulgaria". The other powers objected, and the Congress of Berlin (June-July 1878) revised it.
  • Sold Alaska to the USA in 1867 for about US$7.2 million †.
  • Socialist ideas spread among students and the intelligentsia. The Narodniks (populists) "went to the people" in 1874 but the peasants did not respond. Their organisation Land and Liberty (1876) split in 1879; its terrorist wing, Narodnaya Volya ("People's Will"), assassinated Alexander II with a bomb in St Petersburg on 1 / 13 March 1881 (Figure 2).

[Correction] The notes say local self-governments were introduced "at village level". Alexander II's zemstvos (1864) were elected assemblies at the district (uezd) and provincial (guberniya) levels, and town dumas followed in 1870. The village commune (mir) already existed; it was given administrative duties under the emancipation of 1861.

[Correction] The notes date the "construction of railways" to 1879. Russia's railways began much earlier: the first line (St Petersburg - Tsarskoye Selo) opened in 1837 † and the Moscow - St Petersburg line was begun in 1842 as a state project and completed in the early 1850s. Under Alexander II the network expanded fast in the 1860s-70s. The great project of the next reign was the Trans-Siberian Railway (from 1891).

[Correction] The notes say Alexander II "helped in the independence of Romania, Montenegro, Bulgaria and Serbia" to regain the Black Sea. The Congress of Berlin (1878) recognised the independence of Romania, Serbia and Montenegro, but Bulgaria became only an autonomous principality under Ottoman suzerainty (fully independent in 1908). Russia gained southern Bessarabia, Kars, Ardahan and Batum. Russia had already won back its Black Sea rights in 1870-71 (above); Balkan nationalism served Pan-Slav aims and access to the Straits.

12.2.4 Alexander III (1881-1894): reaction

  • He stopped the reforms; his aim was to suppress the revolutionaries, above all Narodnaya Volya. His adviser was Konstantin Pobedonostsev, chief procurator of the Holy Synod †.
  • Very strict censorship (even private letters were opened) and a new secret police, the Okhrana (1881), which watched universities and other institutions. Hundreds of revolutionaries were imprisoned, exiled to Siberia or executed. He also pursued Russification of the minorities and anti-Jewish laws after the pogroms of 1881-82 †.
  • Supporters of Narodnaya Volya, including Alexander Ulyanov, plotted to kill the Tsar on 1 March 1887. The plot failed and Alexander Ulyanov was hanged in May 1887. His younger brother was Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov (1870-1924), later known as Lenin.
  • Foreign policy: the Franco-Russian Alliance (1891-94) ended Russia's isolation after Germany let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse (1890) (see the alliance system in the 11-14 note).
  • Economy: under finance ministers Vyshnegradsky and Sergei Witte, the Trans-Siberian Railway was begun in 1891 (Map 1).
  • Alexander III died suddenly in 1894 at the age of 49.

[Correction] Two names: Lenin's elder brother was Alexander Ilyich Ulyanov (not "Ulyana"), and Lenin himself was Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov ("Ilyich" is his patronymic, not his first name). The plot of 1887 targeted Alexander III.

[Correction] "Franco-Entente" in the notes should read the Franco-Russian Alliance (also called the Dual Alliance): a political agreement in 1891 and a secret military convention in 1892, ratified in 1893-94 †. The "Entente Cordiale" (1904) was between Britain and France, and the Triple Entente (1907) added Russia.

[Correction] The notes say the Trans-Siberian Railway was started in 1871. Construction began in 1891, under Alexander III and his finance ministers, with the future Nicholas II turning the first earth at Vladivostok. A through route to the Pacific via Manchuria (the Chinese Eastern Railway) worked by about 1904, and the all-Russian route along the Amur was completed only in 1916.

12.2.5 Nicholas II (1894-1917): the last Tsar

  • He became Tsar at 26. He lacked political experience because of the untimely death of his father, and he lacked the qualities of an autocrat; historians often call him indecisive.
  • He was strongly influenced by his wife Alexandra (Alix of Hesse, a German princess and granddaughter of Queen Victoria). Alexandra in turn fell under the influence of the Siberian peasant "holy man" Grigori Rasputin, who seemed able to ease the haemophilia of the heir, Alexei. The Tsar came to distrust his own ministers.
  • Industrialisation: Nicholas II encouraged rapid industrialisation, led by finance minister Sergei Witte (1892-1903): help from France (loans), foreign investment, higher taxes and the gold standard (1897) †. Railway building speeded up, which boosted mining and the iron and steel industries. Hundreds of modern factories were set up and oil was developed (Baku).
  • As a result the Industrial Revolution became significant and a working class emerged, roughly 3% of the population [Verification Required: estimates of the factory and mine workforce around 1900-1914 range from about 2 to 3 million, i.e. 2-3% of some 125-170 million people].
  • Workers' conditions: long hours (11-12 hours or more), no holidays, low wages, child labour, unsafe factories, overcrowded industrial cities, a low standard of living, and no legal trade unions (they were illegal until 1906).

12.3 The spread of socialism: the RSDLP, Lenin, Bolsheviks and Mensheviks

  • Harsh working conditions spread socialist (Marxist) ideas among workers and students; revolutionary circles of workers were set up in many cities.
  • The Russian Marxist Georgy Plekhanov (the "father of Russian Marxism"), in exile in Geneva, founded the first Russian Marxist group, the Emancipation of Labour (also called Liberation of Labour), in 1883, with Pavel Axelrod.
  • The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party (RSDLP) was founded at a small congress in Minsk in March 1898. Being illegal, its leaders were arrested or lived abroad.
  • The Tsarist government imprisoned and exiled revolutionaries. Lenin was arrested in December 1895 (League of Struggle for the Emancipation of the Working Class) and exiled to Shushenskoye in Siberia (1897-1900) † (Map 1). The state used force to control the masses.
  • Lenin's exile ended in 1900; he left Russia for western Europe (Munich, later London and Geneva) and, with Plekhanov's group and Martov, began the socialist newspaper Iskra ("The Spark"), first issue December 1900 (printed in Leipzig) †. In What Is to Be Done? (1902) he argued for a party of disciplined professional revolutionaries.
  • Second Congress of the RSDLP (July-August 1903, Brussels and London): a difference of opinion arose over Marxist strategy and party membership, and the party split into two factions:
    1. Mensheviks ("minority"), led by Julius Martov; Plekhanov soon joined them.
    2. Bolsheviks ("majority"), led by Lenin.
  • The differences deepened and the RSDLP broke into two parties in 1912: at the Prague Conference (January 1912) the Bolsheviks set up their own party, the RSDLP (Bolsheviks), and began the daily Pravda ("Truth") in May 1912. Iskra had passed to the Mensheviks in late 1903.
  • A second socialist current, the Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs, 1901-02), heirs of the Narodniks, worked for the peasants and demanded land for those who till it. [Additional]

[Correction] The notes say Plekhanov "established a secret socialist party RSDP in 1883", which was "reorganised into the RSDLP in 1898". In 1883 Plekhanov founded the Emancipation of Labour group (in Geneva), not a party. The RSDLP was founded separately at the Minsk congress of March 1898 by several Marxist groups (including the Jewish Bund); Plekhanov's group helped organise the 1903 congress and then dissolved itself into the party.

[Correction] The notes say Lenin "joined the RSDLP in 1903" and "gave control of Iskra to the RSDLP". Lenin was already a Social Democrat. Iskra (from December 1900) was the organ of the Iskra group within the RSDLP, edited by Lenin, Plekhanov, Martov and others; the Second Congress (1903), which Iskra had organised, recognised it as the party's central organ. After the split, Iskra came under Menshevik control (Lenin left its board in late 1903).

[Correction] The notes list Leon Trotsky with Lenin among the Bolsheviks in 1903. In 1903 Trotsky sided with the Mensheviks, then stood apart from both factions; he joined the Bolsheviks only in July-August 1917. Plekhanov at first backed Lenin in 1903 but soon went over to the Mensheviks.

[Correction] The notes say "RCP dominated by Bolsheviks". The name Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), RCP(b), was adopted only in March 1918. In 1912 the Bolshevik party was still called the RSDLP (Bolsheviks). (Later names: All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) from 1925; CPSU from 1952 †.)

12.4 Russia in the early 20th century: conditions before 1905

  • Political: Tsarist autocracy; the people had no political power or rights.
  • Peasants: did not own enough land (much land remained with nobles, the crown and the church), paid heavy taxes and redemption payments.
  • Spread of socialism and the rise of revolutionary groups (RSDLP 1898, SRs 1901-02); liberals also organised (the Union of Liberation, 1903-04) †.
  • Rapid population growth (from about 125 million in 1897), while farm output was poor in a harsh climate: land hunger and periodic famine.
  • A prominent but badly treated working class; a recession after 1900 †.
  • Humiliating defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (8 February 1904 - 5 September 1905) over Manchuria and Korea: Japan's surprise attack on Port Arthur (February 1904), the fall of Port Arthur (January 1905), defeat at Mukden (March 1905) and the destruction of the Baltic Fleet at Tsushima (27-28 May 1905). The Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905), mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, gave Japan Russia's lease of the Liaodong peninsula (Port Arthur), the South Manchurian railway and southern Sakhalin (Map 1).
  • The war worsened the existing food shortages, and the Tsar's autocracy became deeply unpopular.

[Correction] The notes say "1 lakh Russians got killed" in the Russo-Japanese War. Estimates of Russian military deaths are about 43,000-71,000 (killed, died of wounds and of disease), with some 146,000 wounded and 74,000 captured. Japan's dead were higher, about 80,000-86,000. "One lakh" fits Russian killed and captured together, not deaths.

[Additional] India link: Japan's victory was the first defeat of a European great power by an Asian nation in modern times. It inspired Asian nationalists, including Indian leaders during the Swadeshi movement (1905). The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, which divided Persia into spheres and settled Afghanistan and Tibet, ended the "Great Game" between Britain and Russia on India's north-west frontier (Map 1) †.

12.5 The Revolution of 1905

  • Bloody Sunday, 9 / 22 January 1905: a large, peaceful procession of industrial workers (often put at about 1-2 lakh; estimates vary †), led by the priest Father Georgy Gapon, marched to the Tsar's Winter Palace in St Petersburg to submit a petition. They asked for workers' welfare (an eight-hour day, better wages), basic rights and democratic reform, including a constituent assembly. When they came near, troops opened fire and killed hundreds (the official figure was about 130; other estimates are much higher †).
  • Bloody Sunday destroyed the image of the Tsar as the "little father" of his people and led to nation-wide protests: strikes, peasant risings, nationalist unrest in Poland and the Baltic, the mutiny on the battleship Potemkin (June 1905), and a general strike in October. People demanded a constituent assembly.
  • Workers (and later soldiers) formed soviets ("councils") to direct the strikes. The best known was the St Petersburg Soviet (October-December 1905), whose leading figure was Leon Trotsky.
  • Under this pressure the Tsar issued the October Manifesto (17 / 30 October 1905), drafted by Witte. It promised civil liberties (freedom of speech, press, assembly and conscience), an elected State Duma whose consent would be needed for laws, and a wider franchise. Liberals were satisfied, most strikes were called off and the opposition split; the Moscow rising of December 1905 was crushed.
  • Fundamental Laws (23 April / 6 May 1906), issued by the Tsar himself (the notes' "constitution of 1906"):
    • Russia became, on paper, a constitutional monarchy; legal political activity, parties and elections were allowed.
    • A national parliament: the State Duma (elected lower house) and the State Council (half appointed upper house), with legislative powers.
    • But the Tsar kept the title of "autocrat"; he alone appointed and dismissed the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, who were responsible to him, not to the Duma; he had an absolute veto over laws, could dissolve the Duma, could rule by emergency decree between sessions (Article 87), and controlled the army and foreign policy.
  • So the Revolution of 1905 made Russia only a semi-constitutional monarchy. It could not end Tsarist autocracy, because the constitution was granted by the Tsar and left him his autocratic powers (see the correction in the 11-16 note, c20). Historians often call 1905 the "dress rehearsal" for 1917.

12.6 The Dumas and Stolypin (1906-1911)

  • First Duma (27 April - 8 July 1906 OS): lasted only 72 days. Dominated by liberals (Kadets) and peasant deputies (Trudoviks), it demanded land reform and ministers responsible to it, and was dissolved; some 180 deputies who protested in the Vyborg Manifesto lost their political rights.
  • Second Duma (20 February - 3 June 1907 OS): even more radical (socialists, who had boycotted the first, now took part); dissolved after about 103 days.
  • Both were anti-Tsarist, so the Tsar and Stolypin changed the electoral law on 3 June 1907 (the "coup of 3 June"), cutting the voting weight of peasants, workers and the minorities and favouring landowners.
  • Third Duma (1907-1912): the only one to serve its full five-year term; conservative and pro-government (Octobrist majority).
  • Fourth Duma (1912-1917): also conservative; in February 1917 its leaders formed the Provisional Government. It was formally dissolved in October 1917 †.

[Correction] Three points about the Dumas in the notes. (1) The Second Duma lasted about 103 days (20 February - 3 June 1907; it worked 102 days), not 112. (2) "4/12/2017" should read the Fourth Duma, 1912-1917. (3) The Third and Fourth Dumas were pro-government (conservative), not "pro-Duma"; that is why they survived.

  • Pyotr Stolypin was appointed Prime Minister (Chairman of the Council of Ministers) in July 1906, before the Second Duma. His policy was "pacification first, then reform":
    • Repression: field courts-martial hanged so many revolutionaries that the noose was nicknamed "Stolypin's necktie".
    • Agrarian reform (decree of 9 November 1906): peasants could leave the commune and consolidate their strips into private farms, creating a class of loyal prosperous peasants (the "wager on the strong"); redemption payments were ended; the Peasant Land Bank helped purchases; peasants were resettled in Siberia along the Trans-Siberian Railway (about 3 million moved east in 1908-13).
    • Some industrial and social measures (for example, worker insurance in 1912 †).
  • Results: farm output improved, but population grew rapidly, so food shortages remained; only a minority of peasants left the commune. Industrial reforms brought only a marginal improvement for workers. Russia remained backward compared with western Europe.
  • Stolypin was disliked by both the left and the right. He was shot at the Kiev Opera on 1 / 14 September 1911, in the presence of the Tsar, and died four days later.
  • The assassination increased suspicion of the court and police: the investigation was stopped on the Tsar's orders, and rumours spread that the police or reactionaries were behind it. After his death, the pace of reform slowed.

[Correction] The notes say Stolypin "got assassinated by royalists" and that the Tsar "did not order an enquiry". The assassin was Dmitry Bogrov, a young anarchist revolutionary who was also an informer of the Okhrana; the police let him into the theatre. An inquiry was begun, but the Tsar halted it. Whether Bogrov acted alone, under pressure from revolutionaries, or with the connivance of reactionary officials is still debated; involvement of royalists or of the Tsar was a rumour, not an established fact.

12.7 Russia, 1911-1917: unrest and World War I

  • Discontent grew again: peasants protested, workers went on strike and educated people criticised the regime. The shooting of striking workers at the Lena goldfields (April 1912) set off a strike wave, about 2,000 strikes in 1912 [Verification Required: published counts for 1912 vary]. Strikes were suppressed by police and troops; people felt alienated and hatred of the Tsar grew.
  • Revolutionaries, especially the Bolsheviks, spread their ideas among workers (Pravda, 1912). By 1914 the situation in the cities was close to a state of civil conflict.
  • At this point Russia entered World War I (August 1914) (11-16 note, 11.5.3).
  • Russia suffered repeated defeats and huge losses (Tannenberg 1914; the Great Retreat from Poland, 1915). The war exposed corruption in the administration, the weakness of the army (shortages of rifles and shells) and the inefficiency of the Tsar's leadership.
  • The war created new problems: unemployment, inflation, food and fuel shortages and rising prices, as railways were overloaded and peasants were conscripted; the standard of living fell.
  • People lost faith in the Romanov dynasty:
    • In September 1915 the Tsar made himself Commander-in-Chief and went to the front, so he was blamed personally for defeats.
    • In his absence Alexandra and Rasputin interfered in the administration; ministers were changed again and again ("ministerial leapfrog"), and scandalous rumours spread about Rasputin.
    • The Empress's German birth led to rumours of treason.
    • Rumours also linked the court to Stolypin's murder (above).
    • Rasputin was murdered by nobles led by Prince Felix Yusupov on 17 / 30 December 1916 †, but it was too late to save the regime.
  • With this, revolutionaries of all kinds united against the Tsar, which led to the first revolution of 1917 in February.

[Correction] The notes say Russia "lost 5 million troops in 2 years". This figure counts casualties (killed, wounded and captured), not deaths. Russian military deaths in the whole war are usually estimated at about 1.7-2 million. (The same correction was made in the 11-16 note, c11.)

12.8 The February (March) Revolution of 1917 and dual power

  • 23 February / 8 March 1917 (International Women's Day): thousands of women textile workers in Petrograd went on strike and joined women marching for equal rights, protesting against bread and fuel shortages and rising prices. Within two days the strikes became a general strike of some lakhs of workers †.
  • The government sent troops to suppress the strikers; on 26 February / 11 March some units fired on crowds, but on 27 February / 12 March the Petrograd garrison mutinied and joined the strikers. The February Revolution was not bloodless: over 1,300 were killed and wounded in Petrograd by contemporary counts †.
  • Workers and soldiers formed the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies (27 February / 12 March), supported by the troops. Soviets then sprang up across Russia; the Petrograd Soviet was the most powerful of them. Its Order No. 1 (1 / 14 March) told soldiers to obey their officers only if the Soviet agreed.
  • The Tsar abdicated on 2 / 15 March 1917 (for himself and his son); his brother Grand Duke Michael declined the throne the next day. Over 300 years of Romanov rule ended.
  • Leaders of the Duma formed a Provisional Government (2 / 15 March), headed by Prince Georgy Lvov; Alexander Kerensky, a socialist (Trudovik/SR), was Minister of Justice, then War, and became Prime Minister in July 1917. It introduced civil liberties, abolished the death penalty and promised a Constituent Assembly elected by universal suffrage.
  • Dual power (dvoevlastie): the Provisional Government had formal authority, but the Petrograd Soviet controlled the soldiers, railways and workers. Russia had a dual power structure (Figure 4).
  • People demanded elections to the Constituent Assembly, but the Provisional Government postponed them because of the war (they were held only in November 1917).
  • It did not quit the war, because of its obligations to the Allies (the 1914 agreement not to make a separate peace) and its need for Allied loans, and because of patriotism and fear of a harsh German peace. The Central Powers already held Russian Poland, Lithuania and Courland (western Latvia) from 1915 (11-16 note, Map 4). The Kerensky (June) Offensive of 1917 failed and desertions soared.
  • The problems of the war continued, and the government could not introduce pro-peasant (land) or pro-worker policies; peasants began to seize land themselves. It became unpopular because it did not meet expectations.

[Correction] Two points. (1) The government formed in March 1917 was the Provisional Government (temporary, until a Constituent Assembly decided the form of state), not a "provincial" government. (2) It did not announce a republic in March: the form of the state was left to the Constituent Assembly. Kerensky proclaimed Russia a republic on 1 / 14 September 1917, after the Kornilov affair.

[Correction] The notes say the Central Powers had conquered "Poland, Latvia, Lithuania, Ukraine, Belarus" while the Provisional Government fought on. In 1917 they held Russian Poland, Lithuania and Courland (western Latvia), taken in 1915; Riga fell in September 1917. Ukraine, Belarus, Estonia and the rest of Latvia were occupied only in February-March 1918, during the German advance that forced the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk.

12.9 Lenin's return, the April Theses, the July Days and the Kornilov affair

  • Lenin's return: Lenin was in exile in Switzerland. The German government, hoping he would take Russia out of the war, allowed him and other exiles to cross Germany in a "sealed" train; he reached the Finland Station in Petrograd on 3 / 16 April 1917.
  • April Theses (4 / 17 April 1917): no support for the Provisional Government; an end to the "imperialist" war; "All power to the Soviets"; confiscation of landlords' estates and nationalisation of land; workers' control of production. The popular slogan was "Peace, Land and Bread". Bolshevik popularity rose, and workers' soviets in the cities came under Bolshevik control.
  • July Days (3-7 / 16-20 July 1917): armed demonstrations in Petrograd against the government failed. The Bolsheviks were blamed and accused of being German agents; Trotsky and others were arrested and Lenin fled to Finland. [Additional]
  • Kornilov affair (27-31 August / 9-13 September 1917): General Lavr Kornilov, whom Kerensky had made Commander-in-Chief in July, sent troops towards Petrograd, aiming to restore order and, in the common view, to set up a military regime. Kerensky dismissed him and turned to the Soviet for defence. Bolshevik Red Guards were armed, railway workers blocked the troop trains and agitators persuaded Kornilov's soldiers to desert, so the march collapsed without a battle. Kornilov was arrested.
  • Result: Kerensky looked weak, and the Bolsheviks appeared as defenders of the revolution. They won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets, and Leon Trotsky, who had joined the Bolsheviks in summer 1917, was elected chairman of the Petrograd Soviet (25 September / 8 October 1917).

[Correction] The notes say Lenin "struck a deal with the Central Powers, particularly Germany, to reach Russia". The established fact is that the German government allowed and arranged his transit through Germany in a sealed train, expecting him to undermine Russia's war effort. How far Germany also funded the Bolsheviks in 1917 is debated: the Provisional Government accused them of being German agents in July 1917, and some German money is documented, but historians disagree on its scale and importance. It should not be stated as a formal deal.

[Correction] On the Kornilov affair, the notes say the Commander-in-Chief "attempted a coup against the Provisional Government in September 1917" and "the Bolsheviks crushed the coup". (1) The dates are 27-31 August OS / 9-13 September NS. (2) Kornilov's aims are disputed: some historians see a planned coup, others a misunderstanding between Kerensky and Kornilov in which Vladimir Lvov acted as a go-between. (3) The march collapsed mainly through railway workers' obstruction and the desertion of Kornilov's troops; the Bolsheviks played a leading part in this defence, together with the Soviet and other socialists, but there was no battle to "crush".

12.10 The October (November) Revolution of 1917

Summary (as dictated in class, with dates): the February Revolution abolished the Russian monarchy, and the Duma leaders formed a Provisional Government for what became the Russian Republic. But dual power weakened the Provisional Government; its authority eroded and its rule became unpopular when it failed to hold elections or leave the war. Meanwhile Lenin's return, his promises in the April Theses and the Kornilov affair made the Bolsheviks immensely popular, and Leon Trotsky became chairman of the Petrograd Soviet in September 1917.

  • The seizure of power: Lenin returned secretly and persuaded the Bolshevik Central Committee to vote for an armed rising on 10 / 23 October. The Military Revolutionary Committee of the Petrograd Soviet, led by Trotsky, organised it. On 24-25 October / 6-7 November 1917 Red Guards, soldiers and sailors occupied bridges, railway stations, the telegraph and government offices in Petrograd, almost without bloodshed; the cruiser Aurora fired a blank signal shot and the Winter Palace, seat of the Provisional Government, was taken in the night of 25-26 October. Kerensky escaped. This was the Bolshevik (October) Revolution.
  • The Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets (25-26 October / 7-8 November) approved the new government, the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom), with Lenin as chairman, Trotsky for foreign affairs and Stalin for nationalities.
  • First socialist measures:
    • Decree on Peace: an immediate peace "without annexations or indemnities"; secret treaties to be published.
    • Decree on Land: landlords', crown and church estates confiscated without compensation and handed to the peasants through local committees (in effect approving what peasants were already doing).
    • Workers' control of factories (November 1917); nationalisation of banks; the eight-hour day; separation of church and state; the right of nations to self-determination; the Gregorian calendar from February 1918 †.
    • The Cheka (secret police) under Felix Dzerzhinsky was created in December 1917 †.
  • Constituent Assembly: elections in November 1917 gave the SRs the most seats; the Bolsheviks won about a quarter of the vote. The Assembly met once, on 5 / 18 January 1918, and was dissolved by the Bolsheviks the next day. [Additional]
  • Quitting the war: an armistice was signed on 15 December 1917. Trotsky tried to delay ("neither war nor peace"), but the Germans resumed their advance on 18 February 1918, so the Bolsheviks were arm-twisted into the humiliating Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918). Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland, Ukraine and Belarus, and Kars, Ardahan and Batum to Turkey: about a third of its population, most of its coal and much of its industry (11-16 note, 11.5.6). Lenin accepted it to gain a "breathing space" for the revolution, but it made the Bolsheviks unpopular, and the Left SRs left the government.
  • The capital was moved from Petrograd to Moscow in March 1918, and the party took the name Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks).

[Correction] The notes say the Bolsheviks distributed land to peasants "and peace land to workers". The Decree on Land gave land to the peasants; the Decree on Peace concerned the war. For workers, the Bolsheviks introduced workers' control of factories (decree of November 1917) and the eight-hour day, followed by nationalisation in 1918.

Revolution or coup? Soviet historians presented October as a mass proletarian revolution. Many Western historians (for example, Richard Pipes) describe it as a coup d'etat by a disciplined minority party; others (for example, Alexander Rabinowitch) stress the real popular support the Bolsheviks had in Petrograd by autumn 1917. Both views should be shown in Mains answers. [Additional]

12.11 The Russian Civil War (1918-1921)

  • Using the unpopularity of the Bolsheviks after Brest-Litovsk and the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly, their opponents formed anti-Bolshevik ("White") forces: other parties (SRs, Mensheviks, liberals), Kornilov and his supporters (the Volunteer Army in the south, 1918; Kornilov was killed in April 1918), monarchists and Cossacks, and nationalists in the borderlands.
  • They were supported by the capitalist Western powers, which could not tolerate the world's first communist government, which had repudiated the Tsar's foreign debts (February 1918) and made a separate peace with Germany. Allied intervention (1918-1920): British, French and US troops at Murmansk and Archangel, the French at Odessa, Japanese and US troops at Vladivostok (the Japanese stayed until October 1922) (Map 3).
  • The revolt of the Czechoslovak Legion (May 1918), former prisoners of war travelling east to rejoin the Allies, seized the Trans-Siberian Railway and opened the Volga and Siberian fronts.
  • This culminated in the Russian Civil War, 1918-1921 (fighting began in late 1917 and the last campaigns in the Far East ended in 1922): anti-Bolsheviks (Whites) against the Bolshevik government (Reds) under Lenin, whose Red Army was built by Trotsky as Commissar for War.
  • Main White campaigns of 1919: Admiral Kolchak from Omsk towards the Volga; General Denikin from the south to Orel, about 400 km from Moscow; General Yudenich from Estonia to the outskirts of Petrograd. All were driven back. The last White army, under Wrangel, was evacuated from the Crimea in November 1920.
  • Polish-Soviet War (1919-1921): the Red Army reached Warsaw but was defeated there in August 1920; by the Treaty of Riga (18 March 1921) Poland kept western Ukraine and western Belarus †.
  • To win the war Lenin adopted War Communism (12.12) and the Red Terror (from September 1918), carried out by the Cheka; the Whites also carried out a "White Terror".
  • The Bolsheviks executed Tsar Nicholas II and his family (with their doctor and servants) in the Ipatiev House at Yekaterinburg on the night of 16-17 July 1918, as Czech and White forces approached the city.
  • Lenin founded the Communist International (Comintern, or Third International) in Moscow in March 1919; its goal was world communist revolution.
  • Human cost: the Civil War period cost an estimated 7-12 million lives, mostly civilians, through fighting, terror, epidemics (typhus) and hunger; the famine of 1921-22 (Volga region) killed about 5 million more by common estimates, though published estimates range widely. Some 1.5-2 million Russians emigrated (the "White emigration").
  • The Bolsheviks were victorious.

On the figures in the notes: the class figures "9 million died" in the civil war and "5-7 million" in the famine fall within the ranges historians give (7-12 million and about 5 million, with some estimates of the famine from 1 to 10 million). Treat them as estimates; sources do not agree on what is counted (battle deaths, disease, famine).

12.12 War Communism (June 1918 - March 1921): features (asked in class)

War Communism was the economic and political system of the Bolsheviks during the Civil War. Its chief features were the nationalisation of industry and the forced requisition of surplus grain from the peasants. [Additional, asked in class]

  1. Nationalisation of industry: the decree of 28 June 1918 nationalised all large-scale industry; by November 1920 even small workshops with 5-10 workers were taken over. The economy was run centrally by the Supreme Council of the National Economy (VSNKh) and its departments ("glavki").
  2. Grain requisitioning (prodrazvyorstka): the state took the peasants' "surplus" grain (in practice often much of their food) at fixed low prices or for nothing, using armed food detachments; a state monopoly of grain (the "food dictatorship" of May 1918) and Committees of Poor Peasants (kombedy) set up in June 1918 to turn poor peasants against the "kulaks".
  3. Ban on private trade and private enterprise; a state monopoly of foreign trade.
  4. Rationing of food and most goods, distributed centrally by class (workers and soldiers first).
  5. Compulsory labour for all able-bodied adults ("militarisation of labour"); strict factory discipline; strikes forbidden.
  6. Money lost its value through hyperinflation; wages were paid largely in kind, and barter replaced money; some public services were made free.
  7. Military-style control of the railways and of the economy in general.
  8. Politically: the one-party state took shape, other parties were suppressed, and the Red Terror of the Cheka was used against "class enemies".

Results: War Communism helped the Reds win the war, by feeding the Red Army and the cities, but it wrecked the economy. With no incentive to grow surplus grain, peasants sowed less; by 1921 industrial output had fallen to about one-fifth of the 1913 level and workers' real wages by about two-thirds; cities emptied as people fled to the countryside (Petrograd lost most of its population). Peasant risings spread (Tambov, 1920-21), and in March 1921 the sailors of Kronstadt, once the "pride of the revolution", rebelled, demanding "soviets without Bolsheviks". With the drought this produced the famine of 1921-22. (Figure 5)

12.13 The end of Brest-Litovsk, the new Soviet republics and the formation of the USSR (1922)

  • The territories under Bolshevik rule became the Russian Socialist Federative Soviet Republic (RSFSR), proclaimed in January 1918 and given its first constitution in July 1918.
  • Parallel to the Civil War, the Paris Peace Conference met and the Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) was signed. Germany's gains at Brest-Litovsk were cancelled.
  • The Bolsheviks reconquered many of the lands lost in 1918, mainly Ukraine, Belarus, the Caucasus and Central Asia, and created new Soviet socialist republics there: the Ukrainian SSR, the Byelorussian SSR (1 January 1919) and, after the Red Army took Azerbaijan (1920), Armenia (1920) and Georgia (1921), the Transcaucasian Socialist Federative Soviet Republic (1922) †.
  • On 30 December 1922 the RSFSR, the Ukrainian SSR, the Byelorussian SSR and the Transcaucasian SFSR signed the treaty forming the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR). Each was called a union (constituent) republic, with a formal right to secede, but the RSFSR was the leading member and power lay with the single Communist Party in Moscow. The first USSR constitution followed in January 1924 †.
  • The number of union republics grew as Central Asia was reorganised (1924-36) and as territories were annexed in 1940 (the Baltic states, Moldavia and the Karelo-Finnish SSR): 16 by 1940, and 15 from 1956, when the Karelo-Finnish SSR was merged into the RSFSR. The USSR dissolved in December 1991 into these 15 states.

[Correction] The notes say the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk was annulled "with the Treaty of Versailles". It was annulled earlier: the Armistice of 11 November 1918 obliged Germany to renounce it, and the Soviet government declared it void on 13 November 1918 †. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) then required Germany to recognise its abrogation formally.

[Correction] The notes imply that the Bolsheviks reconquered "many lost territories" after Versailles. They regained Ukraine, Belarus, the Caucasus and Central Asia, but Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland remained independent (peace treaties of 1920-21, including Riga, 1921), Bessarabia went to Romania (1918), and Kars and Ardahan to Turkey (1921). The new republics were the Ukrainian SSR, the Byelorussian SSR and the Transcaucasian SFSR (a federation of the Georgian, Armenian and Azerbaijani SSRs), not simply "Trans-Caucasia".

[Correction] The notes say "by 1940 the USSR had 15 constituent states". In 1940 there were 16 union republics, because the Karelo-Finnish SSR was created on 31 March 1940; it was reduced to an autonomous republic within the RSFSR on 16 July 1956, after which there were 15.

12.14 The New Economic Policy (NEP), 1921

  • Lenin announced the New Economic Policy at the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, the same month as the Kronstadt revolt, to revive the Russian economy. It involved small-scale capitalism under state control ("state capitalism", in Lenin's phrase). Lenin presented it as a temporary retreat. [Additional, features]
  • Main features:
    1. Grain requisitioning was replaced by a tax in kind (later in money); peasants could sell their surplus on the open market.
    2. Private trade and small private businesses were allowed again (the "Nepmen" traders); small firms were returned or leased to private owners.
    3. The state kept the "commanding heights": large industry, banks, transport and foreign trade.
    4. Foreign concessions and investment were invited; a stable currency, the chervonets (1922), was introduced †.
    5. Wages and incentives returned in place of rationing.
  • Results: by about 1926-28 farm and industrial output were back near 1913 levels. But the political system was not relaxed: the same Congress banned factions in the party. Stalin ended the NEP in 1928 with forced collectivisation and the Five-Year Plans (next classes).

12.16 Significance of the Bolsheviks and of the Russian Revolution (added on request)

[Additional] This whole section was not part of the class notes. It was added on request so that the fifth part of the outline (significance) is complete before the class on Stalin. It answers four exam questions: what 1905 and February 1917 achieved; why the Bolsheviks won and held power; what the revolution changed in Russia, in the world and in India; and how historians judge it.

12.16.1 Significance of the Revolution of 1905: the "dress rehearsal"

  • First breach in autocracy: the October Manifesto and the Duma gave Russia its first elected parliament, legal parties, trade unions and a freer press, although the Fundamental Laws (1906) kept the Tsar's final power (12.5, 12.6).
  • New tools of revolution: the soviet (St Petersburg, 1905) and the general strike appeared for the first time; both returned in 1917. Lenin later called 1905 the "dress rehearsal" for 1917 †.
  • Lessons drawn: the regime survived because the army stayed loyal and liberals accepted the Manifesto. Lenin concluded that the workers needed the peasants as allies and that the liberals could not be trusted; in 1917 the army itself broke.
  • Asia: 1905 coincided with Japan's victory, and both encouraged constitutional and national movements in Persia (1906), the Ottoman Empire (1908), China and India (the Swadeshi movement) †.

12.16.2 Significance of the February Revolution of 1917

  • It ended 304 years of Romanov rule (1613-1917) and the monarchy itself; Russia was proclaimed a republic on 1 / 14 September 1917 (12.8).
  • It was largely spontaneous: bread queues, Women's Day strikes and a mutinous garrison, not a party plan. No party, Bolsheviks included, led it.
  • The Provisional Government gave Russia, for a few months, the freest political system in its history: civil liberties, amnesty, the end of the death penalty (restored at the front in July), and votes for women by a decree of 20 July 1917 (women aged 20 and above), the first among the major powers.
  • But dual power, continuing the war and postponing land reform and the Constituent Assembly made it weak, and opened the way for October (12.9).

Trap: votes for Russian women came from the Provisional Government (July 1917), not the Bolsheviks. The Bolsheviks added legal equality, civil marriage, easier divorce and (1920) legal abortion.

12.16.3 Significance of the Bolsheviks: why they won in 1917 and held power to 1921

The Bolsheviks were a small party in February 1917, with perhaps about 24,000 members †, yet within eight months they held power and within four years they had won the Civil War. Their success is significant because it created a new type of political organisation, the disciplined vanguard party, that ruled Russia for 74 years and was copied by communist parties worldwide.

12.16.4 Significance for Russia

Political

  • Autocracy, the nobility's privileges and the established Church lost their power. Power passed, in name, to the soviets; the first Soviet constitution (RSFSR) followed in July 1918 †.
  • In practice a one-party state emerged: the Constituent Assembly was dispersed (6 / 19 January 1918), other parties were banned step by step (by 1921-22), and factions inside the party were banned in March 1921 (12.14).
  • The world's first state built on Marxist ideology: "dictatorship of the proletariat", a planned economy as the goal and atheism as state policy.

Economic

  • The Decree on Land abolished private ownership of landlords' land; peasants took and shared the estates. Banks, foreign trade and large industry were nationalised (1917-18); foreign debts were repudiated (1918) †.
  • The state became the main owner and planner: the State Planning Commission (Gosplan) was set up in 1921 † and the electrification plan (GOELRO, 1920) was the first long-term plan †. This led to Stalin's Five-Year Plans (from 1928), which made the USSR an industrial power.
  • Experiments and reversals: War Communism, then the mixed economy of the NEP (Figure 5).

Social and cultural

  • Estates abolished: the decree of 11 / 24 November 1917 ended titles and ranks; everyone became a citizen.
  • Church and state separated: the decree of 20 January / 2 February 1918 made the state and the schools secular; church property was taken over; religion was persecuted.
  • Women: legal equality, civil marriage and easy divorce (Family Code, 1918 †), equal pay in law, maternity protection; the party's women's department, Zhenotdel (1919); Alexandra Kollontai, Commissar for Social Welfare, was the first woman minister in modern Europe; in 1920 Soviet Russia became the first country to legalise abortion (banned again under Stalin, 1936-55).
  • Literacy: only about 21-24% of the empire was literate in 1897 (estimates differ). The likbez decree of 26 December 1919 obliged everyone aged 8 to 50 to learn to read and write; by the 1930s most of the population was literate †. The spelling reform of 1918 and the Gregorian calendar (February 1918) were part of the same modernisation.
  • Social mobility for workers and peasants through education and party membership, but also class discrimination against "former people" (nobles, priests, merchants).

Nationalities

  • The Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia (2 / 15 November 1917), signed by Lenin and Stalin, promised equality and self-determination up to secession. Finland, Poland and the Baltic states did become independent; Ukraine, Belarus, the Caucasus and Central Asia were brought back by the Red Army (12.13).
  • The USSR (1922) was federal in form, with national republics, languages and schools, but centralised through the single party. The right of secession written into it was used only in 1991.

The cost

  • Civil War, terror and the famine of 1921-22 killed millions (12.11); about 1-2 million people emigrated †. The Cheka, labour camps and the one-party state created the machinery that Stalin later used.

12.16.5 Significance for the world

  • An alternative to capitalism: for the first time a state claimed to abolish private property and class rule. The world was divided ideologically between liberal capitalism and communism for most of the 20th century.
  • A new diplomacy: the Decree on Peace called for a peace "without annexations or indemnities", and the Bolsheviks published the Allies' secret treaties (November 1917). Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 1918) were in part a reply to this challenge †. Russia left World War I (Brest-Litovsk).
  • The Comintern (March 1919) organised communist parties worldwide: Germany (1918-19), China (1921), the emigre CPI at Tashkent (1920) and the CPI in India (1925) †. Communist revolutions followed later in China (1949), Vietnam and Cuba.
  • Anti-colonialism: Lenin's Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1916) and the Comintern's support for national liberation, for example the Baku Congress of the Peoples of the East (September 1920) †, gave colonial nationalists an ally and a theory. The USSR later backed decolonisation at the United Nations.
  • Reaction and fear: the fear of "Bolshevism" fed the Allied intervention, the cordon sanitaire in Eastern Europe, the Red Scare in the USA (1919-20) and the rise of fascism in Italy and Nazism in Germany, which presented themselves as defences against communism.
  • Roots of the Cold War: mutual distrust from 1917-20 (intervention, non-recognition until 1924-33) carried into the post-1945 rivalry between the USA and the USSR.
  • Social and economic ideas: state planning, full employment, free education and health care, and women's equality pressed capitalist states to reform; the welfare state, the ILO (1919) and labour laws are often linked in part to the fear of revolution †. After 1929, the Soviet Five-Year Plans looked successful while the West was in the Great Depression.
  • The end of the story: the USSR collapsed in December 1991; its 15 republics became independent states.

12.16.6 Significance for India

  • Communist movement: M.N. Roy's emigre CPI at Tashkent (October 1920); the CPI founded at Kanpur (December 1925) †; British prosecutions in the Peshawar (1922-27), Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy (1924) and Meerut Conspiracy (1929) cases †.
  • Workers and peasants: the AITUC (1920), Kisan Sabhas and workers' and peasants' parties (1920s) gave the national movement a class dimension †.
  • Socialist ideas in the Congress: Jawaharlal Nehru visited the USSR in 1927 and wrote Soviet Russia (1928); the Congress Socialist Party (1934); Bhagat Singh's HSRA (1928) added "Socialist" to its name †. Tagore praised Soviet education in Letters from Russia after his 1930 visit †.
  • After 1947: the Planning Commission (1950) and the Mahalanobis model of the Second Five-Year Plan (1956) drew on Soviet planning; heavy industry such as the Bhilai steel plant was built with Soviet help; the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation (1971) †.

12.16.8 How historians judge it

  • Soviet (Marxist-Leninist) view: a popular proletarian revolution led by the party, the start of a new era of world history.
  • Liberal or "totalitarian" view (for example Richard Pipes): a coup by a small, ruthless minority that hijacked the democratic February Revolution; Stalinism grew directly out of Leninism.
  • Revisionist (social history) view (for example Sheila Fitzpatrick, Alexander Rabinowitch): in 1917 the Bolsheviks had real support among Petrograd workers and soldiers; later authoritarianism owed much to civil war and isolation.
  • Balanced answer: October was both a popular revolution in the cities and a planned seizure of power by one party; its achievements (land, literacy, equality, industrial power, decolonisation) came at a huge human cost (terror, famine, loss of freedom).
One-line conclusion for answers: the Russian Revolution turned a backward autocracy into the first socialist state and a future superpower, gave the 20th century its central ideological conflict and inspired colonial peoples, but it also produced the one-party dictatorship that became Stalinism.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Romanovs: 1613-1917. Tsars: Alexander I (1801-25), Nicholas I (1825-55), Alexander II (1855-81), Alexander III (1881-94), Nicholas II (1894-1917). Capital St Petersburg from 1712 (Petrograd 1914); Moscow again from March 1918.
  • Church: Russian Orthodox, autocephalous; Moscow patriarchate 1589; abolished by Peter I in 1721 (Holy Synod); restored 1917.
  • 1897 census: 125.6 million; Great Russians about 44% †.
  • Nicholas I: Third Section (1826); Greek independence (Adrianople 1829, London 1830); Crimean War 1853-56, Sevastopol; Treaty of Paris, 30 March 1856 neutralised the Black Sea.
  • Alexander II: Emancipation of serfs 19 Feb / 3 Mar 1861; zemstvos and judicial reform 1864; universal military service 1874; Alaska sold to the USA 1867; Black Sea clauses repudiated 1870-71; San Stefano (3 Mar 1878) and Congress of Berlin (1878): Romania, Serbia, Montenegro independent, Bulgaria autonomous; killed by Narodnaya Volya, 1 / 13 March 1881.
  • Alexander III: Okhrana 1881; Russification; Alexander Ulyanov hanged 1887; Franco-Russian Alliance 1891-94; Trans-Siberian Railway begun 1891 (all-Russian route 1916).
  • Socialism: Emancipation of Labour (Plekhanov, Geneva, 1883); RSDLP, Minsk, March 1898; Iskra, December 1900; What Is to Be Done? (1902); split 1903 (2nd Congress, Brussels-London): Bolsheviks (Lenin) v Mensheviks (Martov); separate party 1912 (Prague); Pravda 1912; SRs 1901-02.
  • Russo-Japanese War 8 Feb 1904 - 5 Sep 1905: Port Arthur, Mukden, Tsushima (27-28 May 1905); Treaty of Portsmouth, 5 Sep 1905 (Theodore Roosevelt).
  • 1905: Bloody Sunday 9 / 22 Jan 1905 (Father Gapon); Potemkin mutiny (June); St Petersburg Soviet (Trotsky); October Manifesto 17 / 30 Oct 1905; Fundamental Laws 23 Apr / 6 May 1906.
  • Dumas: 1st 1906 (72 days); 2nd 1907 (about 103 days); electoral law / "coup" of 3 June 1907; 3rd 1907-12 (full term); 4th 1912-17.
  • Stolypin: PM July 1906; land reform decree 9 Nov 1906; "Stolypin's necktie"; shot by Dmitry Bogrov at the Kiev Opera, 1 / 14 Sep 1911.
  • Lena goldfields massacre, April 1912; Tsar Commander-in-Chief Sept 1915; Rasputin killed Dec 1916.
  • February Revolution: 23 Feb / 8 Mar 1917 (Women's Day); Petrograd Soviet 27 Feb / 12 Mar; Order No. 1; abdication 2 / 15 Mar 1917; Provisional Government (Lvov; Kerensky from July); republic proclaimed 1 / 14 Sep 1917.
  • Lenin returns 3 / 16 April 1917 (sealed train); April Theses ("Peace, Land, Bread"; "All power to the Soviets"); July Days; Kornilov affair 27-31 Aug / 9-13 Sep 1917; Trotsky chairman of the Petrograd Soviet Sept 1917.
  • October Revolution: 25 Oct / 7 Nov 1917; Military Revolutionary Committee; Winter Palace; Sovnarkom (Lenin); Decrees on Peace and Land; Cheka (Dec 1917); Constituent Assembly dissolved 6 / 19 Jan 1918; Gregorian calendar Feb 1918.
  • Brest-Litovsk 3 March 1918; annulled by the Armistice (11 Nov 1918) and by Soviet decree (13 Nov 1918).
  • Civil War 1918-21: Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich, Wrangel (Crimea evacuated Nov 1920); Czech Legion (May 1918); Allied intervention; Romanovs shot at Yekaterinburg 16-17 July 1918; Comintern March 1919; Treaty of Riga 18 Mar 1921.

  • NEP: March 1921 (10th Party Congress): tax in kind, private trade, "commanding heights".
  • Anglo-Soviet Trade Agreement 16 March 1921 (de facto recognition).
  • Women's vote: Provisional Government decree of 20 July 1917 (women 20 and over), not the Bolsheviks. Soviet Russia was the first country to legalise abortion (1920); Zhenotdel 1919; Kollontai first woman minister in modern Europe.
  • Constituent Assembly election, 25 Nov 1917: SRs about 37.6%, Bolsheviks about 23.3% (estimates vary); dispersed 6 / 19 Jan 1918.
  • Early Soviet decrees: Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia 2 / 15 Nov 1917 (Lenin and Stalin); estates abolished 11 / 24 Nov 1917; Church separated from state 20 Jan / 2 Feb 1918; likbez (literacy) decree 26 Dec 1919 (ages 8-50). Literacy in 1897 about 21-24%.
  • USSR 30 Dec 1922: RSFSR + Ukrainian SSR + Byelorussian SSR + Transcaucasian SFSR. 16 republics in 1940 (Karelo-Finnish SSR 1940-56); 15 from 1956.

Mains Points

  • Long-term causes of 1917: autocracy with no outlet for opposition; a backward agrarian society (serfdom to 1861, land hunger, redemption payments); late, state-driven industrialisation creating a concentrated, exploited working class; a multi-ethnic empire under Russification; spread of populism and Marxism (12.1, 12.3).
  • Reform and reaction: military defeat forced reform (Crimea to the Great Reforms; Manchuria to the Duma) but each reform was half-hearted and followed by reaction (Alexander III; the Fundamental Laws and the 1907 electoral law), so expectations were raised and frustrated (Figure 1, Figure 3).
  • 1905 as the "dress rehearsal": the soviets, the mass strike and the gap between liberals and socialists all appeared first in 1905; the regime survived because the army stayed loyal, the October Manifesto split the opposition and peace with Japan freed troops.
  • War as the immediate cause: defeats, losses, inflation and food shortages, the Tsar's personal command and the Rasputin scandal destroyed the dynasty's legitimacy; this time the army deserted the regime (12.7, 12.8).
  • Why the Provisional Government failed: no electoral legitimacy; dual power; it continued the war and postponed land reform and the Constituent Assembly; the Kornilov affair (12.9).
  • Why the Bolsheviks succeeded: Lenin's leadership and simple slogans, a disciplined party, Trotsky's organisation, control of the Petrograd Soviet and the garrison, and the weakness of opponents. Present both the "popular revolution" and the "coup" interpretations (12.10).
  • Holding power: Brest-Litovsk as a "breathing space"; Red Army, War Communism and the Cheka; White disunity and dependence on foreign help (table).
  • Pragmatism v ideology: War Communism to the NEP shows Lenin's flexibility in economics alongside the hardening of the one-party state (Figure 5).
  • Nationalities: self-determination in theory, reconquest of Ukraine, the Caucasus and Central Asia in practice; the USSR as a federation in form, centralised in reality (12.13).
  • Significance for Russia: end of autocracy and estates; land to the peasants; nationalisation and planning; secular state, women's rights and mass literacy; federal USSR; but a one-party state, terror and famine (12.16.4).
  • Significance for the world: a rival model to capitalism; Decree on Peace and secret treaties; Comintern; support for anti-colonial movements; fear of Bolshevism feeding fascism and the Red Scare; roots of the Cold War; pressure for welfare states (12.16.5, Figure 7).
  • French v Russian Revolution: bourgeois-liberal v Marxist-socialist; European v global impact (table).
  • Interpretations: popular revolution (Soviet, revisionist) v coup by a minority (liberal); use both (12.16.8).
  • India link: the Great Game and the 1907 Convention; Japan's 1905 victory; the CPI at Tashkent (1920); Nehru and socialist planning (12.15).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • February / October v March / November: Old Style dates are 13 days behind; the October Revolution was on 7 November NS. Russia switched to the Gregorian calendar in February 1918.
  • Provisional, not "provincial", Government; and Russia was proclaimed a republic only in September 1917.
  • October Manifesto (1905) v Fundamental Laws (1906): the first promised; the second limited. Russia became a semi-constitutional monarchy.
  • Bolshevik = majority, Menshevik = minority, named after a single vote at the 1903 congress; the Bolsheviks were often the smaller group later.
  • Trotsky was not a Bolshevik in 1903; he joined in 1917. Plekhanov ended up a Menshevik.
  • Emancipation of Labour (1883) v RSDLP (1898): group v party.
  • Third Section (Nicholas I) v Okhrana (Alexander III) v Cheka (Bolsheviks).
  • Narodnaya Volya killed Alexander II (1881), not Nicholas II; Alexander Ulyanov's plot (1887) against Alexander III failed.
  • Trans-Siberian began in 1891 (not 1871); through to the Pacific by 1904 via Manchuria, all-Russian route 1916.
  • Brest-Litovsk was annulled in November 1918 (Armistice, Soviet decree), confirmed at Versailles.
  • Treaty of Paris 1856 (Crimean War) v Paris Peace Conference 1919.
  • Anglo-Russian Convention 1907 (Great Game) v Anglo-Soviet Trade Agreement 1921 (recognition).
  • USSR founded 30 Dec 1922 by four republics, not fifteen; 16 in 1940, 15 from 1956.
  • Russo-Japanese War deaths about 43,000-71,000 Russians, not "one lakh"; WWI "5 million" were casualties, not deaths.
  • Kornilov was the Provisional Government's own Commander-in-Chief; the affair made the Bolsheviks, not Kerensky, stronger.
  • Women's suffrage in Russia came from the Provisional Government (July 1917), not from the Bolsheviks.
  • The Bolsheviks did not win the Constituent Assembly election (SRs first); they won the soviets and the cities, then dispersed the Assembly.
  • Self-determination was promised (2 / 15 Nov 1917), but only Finland, Poland and the Baltic states actually left; the rest was reconquered.
  • The impact of the French Revolution was not confined to Europe either (Haiti, Latin America); "global" v "European" is a matter of degree.

30-Second Revision

Romanov autocracy + serfdom (to 1861) + late industry = a backward empire. Defeat brought reform: Crimea (1856) gave the Great Reforms of Alexander II (killed 1881); Manchuria (1905) gave Bloody Sunday, soviets, the October Manifesto and the Duma, but the Fundamental Laws kept autocracy, and Stolypin mixed land reform with the noose (shot 1911). WWI broke the regime: Women's Day strikes, a mutinous garrison, abdication (March 1917), dual power. Lenin's April Theses and the Kornilov fiasco made the Bolsheviks strong; on 25 Oct / 7 Nov 1917 they took Petrograd. Then Brest-Litovsk (1918), Civil War (Reds v Whites and the Allies), War Communism, Kronstadt, the NEP (March 1921), British trade recognition (16 March 1921) and the USSR (30 Dec 1922). Significance: first socialist state; land, literacy and women's rights at home but one-party rule and terror; abroad the Comintern, anti-colonial inspiration, fascist reaction and the roots of the Cold War.

9. UPSC CSE Mains 2021 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(c) (10 marks)

Revolutions, whether in Russia (1917) or in China (1949), are a disastrous way of transforming a country. Comment.

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Case for "disastrous": civil war, Red Terror, famine (1921-22), the one-party state and later Stalin's purges; in China the Great Leap famine and the Cultural Revolution (12.16.4).

10. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - History Optional, Paper II, Q7(a) (20 marks)

11. UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - History Optional, Paper II, Q5(d) (10 marks, 150 words)

12. UPSC CSE Mains 2012 - History Optional, Paper II, Q6(a) (30 marks)

  16. c16 - 2nd Duma lasted about 103 days; the 4th Duma was 1912-1917; the 3rd and 4th were pro-government.
  17. c17 - Stolypin was shot by Dmitry Bogrov (anarchist and Okhrana informer), not royalists; the inquiry was halted by the Tsar.
  18. c18 - Russia's "5 million" in WWI were casualties; deaths were about 1.7-2 million.
  19. c19 - Provisional (not provincial) Government; Russia was proclaimed a republic only on 1/14 Sept 1917.
  20. c20 - In 1917 the Central Powers held Poland, Lithuania and Courland; Ukraine and Belarus were occupied in Feb-Mar 1918.
  21. c21 - Germany arranged Lenin's sealed-train transit; a formal "deal" and the scale of German funding are debated.
  22. c22 - Kornilov affair: 27-31 Aug / 9-13 Sep 1917; intent disputed; it collapsed through desertion and railway workers, not a battle.
  23. c23 - Decree on Land for peasants, Decree on Peace for the war; workers got workers' control and the eight-hour day.
  24. c24 - Brest-Litovsk was annulled in November 1918 (Armistice; Soviet decree of 13 Nov), then confirmed at Versailles.
  25. c25 - Ukraine, Belarus, the Caucasus and Central Asia were regained; Finland, the Baltic states and Poland stayed independent.
  26. c26 - There were 16 union republics in 1940 (Karelo-Finnish SSR), 15 only from 1956.
  27. c27 - The 1921 agreement was the Anglo-Soviet Trade Agreement (16 March), giving de facto recognition.
