1. Financial-Market Basics: Indices, Liquidity, Arbitrage and G-Secs

1.1 Sensex and Nifty 50

A stock-market index is a single number that tracks the combined price movement of a chosen basket of shares. Sensex is the benchmark index of the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) and covers 30 large, actively traded companies; Nifty 50 is the benchmark index of the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and covers 50 large companies. Both are meant to represent the broad Indian equity market and, through it, the corporate side of the economy.

[Verification Required] The exact launch dates, the dates of the switch to free-float weighting and the review months were taken from index-provider descriptions as reported in secondary sources and were not re-opened from the index methodology documents.

Free-float market-cap index = (Free-float market capitalisation of the constituents / Base market capitalisation) x Base value
Free-float market capitalisation = share price x number of shares readily available for public trading (promoter and government holdings are excluded). An index level is in points, not rupees or per cent; the percentage change in the index is what shows market movement.

[Correction] The rough notes say the index tracks the "top 30 well-established and financially sound companies" (Sensex) and the "top 50 companies" (Nifty). The index providers choose constituents mainly by free-float market capitalisation, liquidity (how actively and cheaply the share trades) and sector representation; "financially sound" is not a formal selection test, and the list is revised from time to time. Also, the Nifty 50 covers a large part of the market, not "a major portion of the Indian economy": an equity index reflects listed companies only, and listed firms are a subset of the whole economy (the unlisted, informal and agricultural sectors are outside it).

Exam relevance (class guidance): for UPSC, daily Sensex and Nifty movements, corporate-sector news, quarterly results and detailed financial-sector regulations are not required. Learn only the concepts: what an index is, which exchange each belongs to, the number of companies, the base year and the free-float idea. Do not memorise index levels.

1.2 Market Liquidity

Liquidity is the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold quickly, at low cost and without a significant change in its price. Cash is the benchmark: it is already money, so converting it costs nothing and takes no time.

(most liquid)→Bank deposit; T-bills→Listed shares→Gold→Property

[Correction] "Property documents" should read property (real estate): the asset is the property, and the documents only prove title. "Cash is more liquid than gold; gold more than property" is correct as a ranking, but liquidity is a matter of degree and context, not a fixed property of an asset. A thinly traded share can be less liquid than gold; a very large sale of even a liquid asset moves its price. [Additional] Two related senses: market liquidity (ease of trading an asset) and banking-system liquidity (the stock of funds banks hold, which the RBI manages through repo, SDF and OMO; see 13 October notes). In the market sense, a common measure is the bid-ask spread (gap between the buying and selling price); a narrow spread means high liquidity. NSE uses impact cost (the extra cost of executing a large order) as a liquidity test for including a share in the Nifty 50 ([Verification Required]; reported in secondary sources).

1.3 Arbitrage

Arbitrage is buying an asset where it is cheap and selling it, at the same time, where it is dearer, to earn a profit from the price difference alone. The profit is meant to be (nearly) risk-free.

Class example: the same asset in two markets (per unit, before costs)
StepMarket AMarket B
PriceRs 100Rs 102
Action by the arbitrageurBuySell
Gross profitRs 102 − Rs 100 = Rs 2 per unit; net profit = Rs 2 minus transaction costs
Price gap (A cheap, B dear)→Buy in A, sell in B at the same time→Price in A rises; price in B falls→Gap closes (one price)

[Correction] The class definition ("same or closely related assets") is right, but note three points. (i) The buy and sell must be simultaneous (or nearly so); otherwise it is speculation, which carries price risk. (ii) The gap must exceed transaction costs (brokerage, taxes, transport) to be profitable. (iii) Arbitrage is self-eliminating: as traders exploit the gap, prices converge, so arbitrage improves market efficiency (the "law of one price"). [Additional] Types: spatial (two markets, as above), currency (triangular), and between a cash market and a derivatives market. Regulatory arbitrage is a different use of the word: moving activity to the lightest-regulated place.

1.4 Government Securities (G-Secs)

A Government Security (G-Sec) is a tradeable debt instrument issued by the Central Government or a State Government to borrow money. It is an acknowledgement of the government's debt obligation. Short-term G-Secs are Treasury bills (original maturity under 1 year); long-term ones are government bonds or dated securities (original maturity of 1 year or more). (Source: RBI, Government Securities Market in India: A Primer.)

Treasury bills vs dated securities
PointTreasury bills (T-bills)Dated securities / government bonds
PurposeShort-term borrowing; bridging cash gapsLong-term borrowing, for example to finance the fiscal deficit
Maturity91, 182 or 364 days (under 1 year)5, 10 years and longer (class note); original maturity of 1 year or more
ReturnIssued at a discount, redeemed at face value; no couponPeriodic coupon (interest), fixed or floating, plus redemption at maturity
IssuerCentral Government onlyCentral Government; State Governments issue State Development Loans
MarketMoney market (short-term)Capital / bond market (long-term)

Illustration (RBI primer): a 91-day T-bill of face value Rs 100 may be issued at about Rs 98.20 and redeemed at Rs 100; the investor's return is the Rs 1.80 difference.

[Correction] (i) "Central government borrows for less than 1 year by T-bills" is right, and the Centre also uses Cash Management Bills (CMBs) (maturity under 91 days; introduced in 2010) for temporary cash gaps. (ii) State governments do not issue T-bills; they issue only dated securities called State Development Loans (SDLs). (iii) "Gilt-edged (like gold) = low risk" is correct for credit (default) risk: G-Secs carry practically no default risk. They still carry interest-rate (price) risk: when market yields rise, the price of an existing bond falls. (iv) "G-Secs are not backed by any physical asset (only trust-based)" is better put as: they are backed by the sovereign's taxation and borrowing power and its ability to create the currency it owes, not by specific assets (they are unsecured sovereign debt); the "trust" is the government's credit. "Gilt-edged" comes from the gilded edges of old certificates.

[Correction] "RBI acts as banker to Central and State governments": the RBI conducts Central Government banking business by statute (RBI Act, 1934, Sections 20 and 21) and State Government business by agreement (Section 21A). [Additional] The RBI also manages the public debt: it conducts the auctions of G-Secs, services the interest and redeems them. This banker-to-government role links to its open market operations in G-Secs. [Verification Required] The section numbers of the RBI Act are standard facts not re-opened from the Act.

[Additional] Why G-Secs matter: they are the benchmark for all other interest rates (the "risk-free" rate), banks hold them to meet the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR), and the RBI buys and sells them in open market operations. A rising G-Sec yield raises the government's cost of borrowing and, through it, the cost of funds across the economy. Retail investors can buy T-bills and bonds in the primary market through the RBI Retail Direct portal (see the 2021 PYQ).

1A. Gig Work (Task-Based Work)

Gig work is short-term, task-based work paid per task, delivery, ride or project rather than a fixed salary, typically outside the traditional employer-employee relationship (delivery partners, cab drivers, freelancers). When the work is arranged through an online platform, the worker is a platform worker.

  • Legal recognition: the Code on Social Security, 2020 defines gig worker, platform worker and aggregator (the platform company). The four Labour Codes, including this one, were made effective from 21 November 2025.
  • Funding social security: aggregators are to contribute 1-2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of the amount paid to gig and platform workers, to a social security fund; workers register on the e-Shram portal. [Verification Required] The contribution range and cap are from legal commentary on the Code; confirm in the Code text and the notified rules.
  • Size: NITI Aayog (June 2022) estimated about 7.7 million gig workers in 2020-21, projected to reach about 23.5 million by 2029-30 (a projection, not a count). [Verification Required] The figures were not re-opened from the report; check them there before quoting.
  • National-accounts link: gig work is a service; its earnings are factor income (wages or mixed income) and its output is counted in GDP.

[Correction] The rough note "gig - task basis" is a fragment. A gig worker is not necessarily on a platform (a freelance electrician is a gig worker, but not a platform worker); every platform worker working through an app is a gig-type worker, and the defining features are no fixed employer, payment per task and, historically, no access to employer-provided social security.

[Correction] "Floor used to make bread" should be "flour". A good's class depends on its use: flour bought by a household for the kitchen is a final (consumer) good, while the same flour bought by a bakery is an intermediate good. A car bought for personal use is a consumer durable; the same car bought for a taxi business is a capital good (class example). [Additional] Capital goods are final goods counted as investment, but they wear out; depreciation (consumption of fixed capital) is the loss in their value, which is why GDP differs from net domestic product (NDP). Also semi-durable goods (clothing, footwear) lie between durable and non-durable.

Why intermediate goods are not counted in GDP

Note from class: intermediate goods are not included in GDP; final goods are. Counting both would count the same output many times (double counting).

3.3 Merit Goods, Demerit Goods and Externalities

Merit vs demerit goods

EffectPositive impact on individuals and society; under-consumed if left to the marketNegative impact on individuals and society; over-consumed if left to the market

Examples (class)Mid-day meal programme; ambulance servicesViolent films and games; high-caffeine energy drinks; tobacco products

[Correction] The rough notes tie merit and demerit goods to "positive" and "negative externalities". The usual definition is wider: a merit good is one the government judges people should consume more of than they would choose, often because individuals underestimate its benefit or lack information (education, vaccination, mid-day meals). Many merit goods do have positive externalities, but the defining idea is social valuation above individual valuation. A demerit good is the opposite (tobacco, alcohol). Merit goods are not necessarily public goods: a mid-day meal is excludable and rivalrous. "Voilet" should read "violent" and "caffine" "caffeine". [Additional] Under GST 2.0 (GST Council, 56th meeting, September 2025; effective 22 September 2025) India introduced a separate 40% GST rate for "sin" and luxury goods, alongside 5% and 18% slabs, which is a real-world example of taxing demerit goods. [Verification Required] The transition date for tobacco products was reported as deferred while compensation-cess liabilities were settled; confirm the current status on the CBIC rate list before quoting it.

Externalities

Externalities are the side effects of an economic activity that affect people not directly involved in it, and for which no price is paid or received. They can be positive (benefit to others) or negative (cost to others).

[Correction] The note says economic goods are "scarce related to demand". Put precisely, economic goods are scarce relative to the wants they can satisfy, so using them means giving up an alternative (opportunity cost; see 22 September notes). "Free good" means no scarcity, not "free of charge": a mid-day meal is free of charge to the child but is still an economic good. Free-good status is context-specific (sunlight is free for a farmer in the open, but not for a company that buys solar panels or sells shade).

  • Excludability: people can be prevented from using the good (for example, by a price).

[Correction] "Non-rivalryious" should read "non-rivalrous", and the note on club goods ends at "E;" (an unfinished "Eg"), so examples are supplied above. Three further precisions. (i) Street lights are a standard textbook example, but in practice most public goods are impure (they can become congested, or access can be limited); national defence is the purest example. (ii) A public good is not the same as a good provided by the government: PDS grain is provided by the State but is rivalrous and excludable (a private good with a public supply); a private firm can provide a public good, but it rarely will because of the free-rider problem (people enjoy it without paying), which is why the State usually provides it. (iii) Common-pool resources face the "tragedy of the commons" (over-use because no one can be excluded); Elinor Ostrom shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics for showing how communities can manage them. [Verification Required] The Ostrom detail is general knowledge not re-opened from the Nobel Committee.

4. Circular Flow of Income

[Additional] The rough notes contain only the heading. The content below is the standard textbook model (see NCERT, Introductory Macroeconomics, Class XII) and should be matched with what was drawn in class.

The circular flow of income shows how output, income and spending move continuously between the sectors of the economy. Households own the factors of production and consume; firms hire the factors and produce goods and services. There are two flows in opposite directions: a real flow (factor services and goods and services) and a money flow (factor payments and spending).

4.1 Two-Sector Model (Households and Firms)

Two-sector flows
MarketReal flowMoney flow
Factor marketHouseholds → firms: land, labour, capital, enterpriseFirms → households: rent, wages, interest, profit (income)
Product marketFirms → households: goods and servicesHouseholds → firms: consumption expenditure (firms' revenue)

[Additional] This closed loop is why national output, national income and national expenditure are equal in value: every rupee of output becomes someone's income, and that income returns as spending. This identity is the basis of the three methods of measuring GDP (production, income and expenditure) in the next topics. Income and output are flows, measured per period.

4.2 Leakages and Injections: Adding Saving, Government and Trade

The simple loop ignores that households save, the government taxes and spends, and the economy trades with the rest of the world. These change the size of the flow:

  • Leakages (withdrawals) take money out of the circular flow: saving (S), taxes (T) and imports (M).
  • Injections put money into the flow: investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X).
Sectors added step by step
ModelSectorsLeakagesInjectionsEquilibrium condition
Two-sector (simple)Households, firmsNoneNoneY = C
Two-sector with financial marketHouseholds, firms, banks and financial marketsSIS = I
Three-sectorPlus governmentS, TI, GS + T = I + G
Four-sector (open economy)Plus rest of the worldS, T, MI, G, XS + T + M = I + G + X
Expenditure identity: Y = C + I + G + (X − M)
Equilibrium (leakages = injections): S + T + M = I + G + X, which can be rearranged as (S − I) = (G − T) + (X − M).
Y = national income (output); C = consumption; I = investment; G = government spending; X = exports; M = imports; S = saving; T = net taxes (taxes less transfers). This is a simplified model.
Injections > leakages→Spending exceeds income earned; firms raise output→Income and output expand (boom)
Leakages > injections→Spending falls short of income; stocks pile up→Income and output contract (slowdown)

[Additional] Link to the next topic: persistent swings between these two cases are one way to understand the business cycle (Topic 3). Link to the twin-deficit idea: rearranging the identity gives M − X = (G − T) + (I − S), so a trade or current-account deficit tends to come with a fiscal deficit (G > T) or an excess of investment over saving. This is an accounting identity that holds by construction; it does not by itself say which variable causes which.

[Correction] "Circular flow of income" is not a measure but a model: it explains why income, output and expenditure are equal in value, and which flows add to or subtract from the national income. Saving is not "lost": it leaks from the spending stream but returns when banks and markets lend it for investment; the economy is in balance when S = I (in the two-sector case). Do not confuse "leakage" (a flow out of the spending stream) with "wasteful" (it need not be).

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Sensex = BSE, 30 companies, base 1978-79 = 100. Nifty 50 = NSE, 50 companies, base 3 November 1995 = 1000. Both use free-float market capitalisation.
  • Liquidity = ease of converting an asset to cash quickly, cheaply and without moving its price; cash > gold > property.
  • Arbitrage = simultaneous buying and selling of the same asset in different markets for a risk-free gain; it narrows price gaps.
  • G-Secs: T-bills (91, 182, 364 days; discount; Centre only); CMBs (under 91 days); dated securities (1 year or more; coupon); SDLs (States issue only these). Gilt-edged = near-zero default risk. RBI is banker and debt manager to the Centre and States.
  • Intermediate goods are not counted in GDP (double counting); final goods are. Capital goods are final goods counted as investment. The class of a good depends on its use.
  • Merit goods: subsidised or free (mid-day meal); demerit goods: taxed or restricted (tobacco); externality = side-effect on third parties.
  • Public goods: non-rivalrous and non-excludable (defence); private: rivalrous and excludable; club: non-rivalrous, excludable; common-pool: rivalrous, non-excludable.
  • Circular flow: leakages (S, T, M) vs injections (I, G, X); equilibrium when S + T + M = I + G + X.
  • Gig worker: task-based, outside employer-employee relationship; recognised in the Code on Social Security, 2020 (Labour Codes effective 21 November 2025).

Mains Points

  • Public goods and the State's role: free-riding and non-excludability explain why the State provides defence, street lighting and public health; merit and demerit goods explain subsidies (mid-day meals) and sin taxes (GST 40% slab).
  • Externalities and policy design: positive externalities justify subsidy; negative externalities justify taxes and regulation (Pigouvian logic); common-pool resources (forests, fisheries) need community or State management to avoid over-use.
  • Government securities and the economy: G-Sec yields set the benchmark for borrowing costs; the Centre's and States' large borrowing programmes compete with private borrowers for savings (crowding out), and the RBI manages both debt issuance and liquidity.
  • Gig work: flexibility and income opportunity against income insecurity and weak social security; the Code on Social Security, 2020 and aggregator contributions are the policy response; measurement of gig work in GDP and employment statistics is a challenge.
  • Circular flow as a policy lens: raising injections (public investment, exports) or cutting leakages (imports of non-essentials) expands income; the saving-investment gap and fiscal deficit feed into the current account (twin-deficit link).
