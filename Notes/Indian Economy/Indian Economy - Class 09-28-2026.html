1.1 (b) Credit Inclusion

Credit inclusion means access to credit/loans at an affordable rate for all in the economy. This is possible when credit is made available through the formal sector (regulated lenders) instead of informal moneylenders.

  • It encourages small businesses, and helps people from lower-income backgrounds to start their own enterprises.
  • It improves access to education through education loans.
  • It improves access to health care through loans for medical treatment.
Formal credit at affordable interest→Investment in a small enterprise, education, health→Higher income, employment, human capital→Less dependence on moneylenders; lower inequality

[Correction] "At a cheaper rate for all" should read "at an affordable cost". Formal credit is usually cheaper than moneylender credit, but interest still reflects risk and cost; the Rangarajan Committee (2008) standard is "timely and adequate credit ... at an affordable cost". The formal sector means regulated lenders: scheduled commercial banks, Regional Rural Banks, cooperative banks, Small Finance Banks and RBI-regulated NBFCs/NBFC-MFIs. Informal lenders (moneylenders, traders, landlords) are unregulated and often charge very high interest, which can trap borrowers in debt.

[Correction] Loans for medical treatment do protect households from distress sale of assets, but borrowing to pay for illness is itself a sign of weak financial protection. The preferred route is health insurance and public provision (see Section 1.2 and Section 3); credit is a fallback, not the main tool, for health care.

[Additional] Why formal credit matters - evidence: the All India Debt and Investment Survey (AIDIS) 2019 (NSS 77th round, NSO/MoSPI; released September 2021) found that in rural India 66% of outstanding cash debt was owed to institutional credit agencies and 34% to non-institutional agencies such as moneylenders; in urban India the institutional share was about 87%. One-third of rural household debt still comes from informal sources.

1.2 (c) Insurance Inclusion

Insurance inclusion means providing access to insurance support for all in the economy. It is reflected in rising insurance density and rising insurance penetration.

Insurance protects households from sudden shocks:

  • Crop insurance supports poor farmers in case of sudden loss of crops (drought, flood, pests).
  • Life insurance saves families from financial distress in case of the sudden death of the only earning member.
  • [Additional] Health insurance prevents large out-of-pocket medical bills from pushing families into poverty (see Section 3).

[Correction] Spelling fixed ("incase" = in case; "familiesfrom" = families from). [Additional] Insurance works by risk pooling: many people pay small, certain premiums so that the few who suffer a loss receive large compensation. That is why it is a core pillar of financial inclusion alongside savings, credit and pensions.

Insurance penetration (%) = (Total insurance premium in the economy in a given year / GDP of the economy in that year) × 100
Insurance density = Total insurance premium in the economy in a given year / Total population of the economy (premium per person; IRDAI and Swiss Re report it in US dollars)
Variables: premium = gross direct premium written by life and non-life insurers (a flow for the year); GDP = nominal GDP of the same year; population = mid-year population.
Illustrative check (not an official calculation): premium Rs 11.93 lakh crore (FY 2024-25) / about 145 crore people ≈ Rs 8,200 per person ≈ USD 97 at about Rs 85 per USD, which matches the reported density.

[Correction] Penetration uses premium (what policyholders pay), not "total insurance paid" (which could be misread as claims paid or sum assured). "Gicen" = given. Penetration is a ratio to GDP (%), density is a per capita amount (currency) - they are not interchangeable.

[Correction] Rising density alone does not prove deeper inclusion: density rises whenever premium grows faster than population, even if premium grows only as fast as GDP (so penetration stays flat). India's current data show exactly this - penetration flat at 3.7% while density rose marginally to USD 97.0 (2024-25). Life-insurance penetration has fallen to 2.7%.

[Additional] Recent reforms: the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025 raised the FDI limit in Indian insurance companies from 74% to 100%, created a Policyholders' Education and Protection Fund and raised maximum penalties from Rs 1 crore to Rs 10 crore. Exam point: India is the 10th largest insurance market by premium volume, yet penetration (3.7%) is low and flat - the "protection gap". The inclusion challenge is coverage and usage, not just market size. Cause-effect: low awareness + low incomes + distrust of claims + weak rural distribution → low penetration → shocks fall on household savings and informal borrowing.

1.3 (d) Pension Inclusion

Pension inclusion means providing older people with income security in old age through regular pension support. It saves people from the vulnerability of financial exclusion in old age.

It can be achieved by subsidising pension schemes, so that people from vulnerable backgrounds can also receive a pension.

[Correction] "Accessibility ... regarding insurance of money" should read assurance of a regular income in old age. A pension is a regular payment after retirement, funded by contributions during working life (contributory) or by the government (non-contributory). [Additional] It also insures against longevity risk - the risk of outliving one's savings. "Oldage" = old age.

[Correction] The government usually does not "subsidise pension funds" as such. It supports vulnerable people in two ways: (i) co-contribution / matching contribution into the individual's pension account (for example, PM-SYM, 50:50 matching), and (ii) non-contributory social pensions paid from the budget to the poorest elderly (NSAP).

[Additional] Why it matters now: the share of people aged 60+ is projected to rise from 10.5% in 2022 to 20.8% by 2050 (UNFPA-IIPS, India Ageing Report 2023). Most workers are in the unorganised sector without an employer pension, and joint-family support is weakening - so old-age poverty risk rises unless pension coverage expands.

Defined-benefit promises create long-term fiscal liabilities - a link to intergenerational equity.

2. Inclusive Education

Inclusive education means providing education support for all in the economy, because a person with better education:

  1. has better and more employment opportunities;
  2. makes more rational decisions (health, saving, family size, voting);
  3. finds it easier to adapt to technological change.

[Additional] Education is a merit good with positive externalities: an educated mother improves child nutrition and health; an educated workforce raises everyone's productivity. It is the main route of intergenerational mobility - it breaks the transmission of poverty from parents to children. Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002) and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009 make elementary education (age 6-14) a Fundamental Right.

Access + quality schooling for all→Skills and capabilities→Employability, productivity, adaptability→Higher income and intergenerational mobility

3. Inclusive Health Care

Inclusive health care means access to affordable, quality health services for all. It:

  • reduces out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) on health, which otherwise pushes households into poverty;
  • restricts the loss of labour days due to illness, protecting income and productivity.

[Correction] "Prevent the payment out of pocket expenditure and increase secondary poverty" is corrected to: inclusive health care reduces OOPE and thereby prevents health-induced impoverishment. The term "secondary poverty" (Seebohm Rowntree, 1901) means a household whose income is enough but is spent on other things; it is not the standard term here. Use "impoverishment due to health spending" or "catastrophic health expenditure" (health spending above a set share of household consumption or income - WHO/World Bank SDG indicator 3.8.2).

Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) = payments made directly by households at the time of using health services (fees, medicines, diagnostics), net of any insurance reimbursement. OOPE share (%) = OOPE / Total Health Expenditure (THE) × 100.

Illness→OOPE (fees, medicines, tests) + lost labour days→Borrowing, sale of assets, cut in food/education→Impoverishment

Break the chain with: prevention (fewer illnesses) + public provision and health insurance (lower OOPE) + sick-leave/ESI (income protection).

Exam point: OOPE share fell from 64.2% (2013-14) to 43.4% (2022-23); Government Health Expenditure rose to 1.43% of GDP (1.48% on the new 2022-23 GDP base) and 43.7% of THE; per capita GHE Rs 2,786 (2022-23, nominal). Social security expenditure on health (including PM-JAY) rose from 6% to 9.9% of THE, and private health insurance from 3.4% to 9.2%. The National Health Policy 2017 target is public health spending of 2.5% of GDP by 2025 - still unmet.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Insurance penetration = premium / GDP × 100; insurance density = premium / population (USD per capita).
  • India 2024-25: penetration 3.7% (life 2.7%, non-life 1.0%); density USD 97.0; 10th largest insurance market.
  • FDI in insurance: 100% under Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025; IRDAI goal "Insurance for All by 2047".
  • PMFBY (2016): farmer premium max 2% kharif, 1.5% rabi, 5% commercial/horticultural; covers post-harvest losses.
  • PMJJBY: Rs 2 lakh life cover, Rs 436/year, 18-50. PMSBY: Rs 2 lakh accident cover, Rs 20/year, 18-70.
  • AB PM-JAY (2018): Rs 5 lakh per family per year; all citizens aged 70+ covered from 2024.
  • APY (2015, PFRDA): Rs 1,000-5,000/month after 60; spouse gets same pension. PM-SYM (2019): Rs 3,000/month, 50:50 matching.
  • IGNOAPS (NSAP): Rs 200/month (60-79), Rs 500 (80+) central assistance - non-contributory.
  • Elderly (60+): 10.5% of population (2022) → 20.8% (2050) - India Ageing Report 2023 (UNFPA-IIPS).
  • PM-Vidyalaxmi (Nov 2024): collateral-free education loans; 75% guarantee up to Rs 7.5 lakh; 3% interest subvention (income up to Rs 8 lakh).
  • AIDIS 2019: 66% of rural household cash debt from institutional sources.
  • RTE Act 2009 / Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002): free and compulsory education, 6-14 years.
  • NEP 2020: mother tongue medium till at least Grade 5 "wherever possible"; 50% learners with vocational exposure by 2025; PARAKH for assessment.
  • UDISE+ 2025-26: 1.03 crore teachers; secondary dropout 7.0%; secondary GER 71.7%.
  • NHA 2022-23: OOPE 43.4% of THE (64.2% in 2013-14); GHE 1.43% of GDP; NHP 2017 target 2.5% of GDP.
  • Tele-MANAS (2022); Mental Healthcare Act 2017; Mission Indradhanush (2014); POSHAN Abhiyaan (2018).

Mains Points

  • Financial inclusion beyond accounts: credit, insurance and pensions turn a bank account into real risk protection. Access is near universal; usage and protection (penetration 3.7%, flat) remain weak.
  • Credit is a double-edged tool: formal credit replaces usurious moneylending and funds enterprise and education, but over-indebtedness (microfinance stress) and loans for medical bills signal gaps in insurance and public services.
  • Protection gap: low insurance penetration means shocks (crop failure, death, illness) fall on household savings - leading to distress sales and poverty traps. PMFBY, PMJJBY, PMSBY and PM-JAY socialise these risks.
  • Ageing and pensions: with the elderly share set to double by 2050 and a largely informal workforce, contributory schemes (APY, PM-SYM) need matching support, while non-contributory pensions (IGNOAPS, at Rs 200) need revision. Defined-benefit promises must be weighed against fiscal sustainability.
  • Education - from access to quality: infrastructure is near universal (UDISE+ 2025-26), but learning outcomes, secondary retention, teacher quality and digital access decide inclusion. NEP 2020 shifts to competency-based learning, mother-tongue instruction and vocational integration.
  • Health - financial protection: OOPE share has fallen sharply but is still about 43%; public spending (1.43% of GDP) is below the NHP 2017 goal. Strengthen primary and preventive care to cut future curative costs and lost labour days.
  • Capability approach (Amartya Sen): education and health are ends in themselves and means to growth - both are central to intragenerational equity.

30-Second Revision

Financial inclusion = banking + credit (formal, timely, affordable; rural debt still 34% informal - AIDIS 2019; PM-Vidyalaxmi for education) + insurance (penetration = premium/GDP x 100 = 3.7%, flat; density = premium/population = USD 97; PMFBY, PMJJBY, PMSBY, PM-JAY, 100% FDI) + pension (old-age income security; elderly 10.5% → 20.8% by 2050; APY, PM-SYM matching, IGNOAPS non-contributory, NPS/UPS). Inclusive education: jobs, rational decisions, tech adaptability; needs infrastructure (RTE norms, now near universal), after-school support, less absenteeism, creative curriculum, mother tongue, regular competency-based assessment (PARAKH), vocational training, industry links, TET-based recruitment; secondary dropout 7.0%, retention 51.9%. Inclusive health: cut OOPE (43.4% of THE, 2022-23) and lost workdays; preventive = vaccination (76.4% full immunisation), diagnostics, nutrition, NCD awareness, mental health (Tele-MANAS); curative = PHC to AIIMS, PM-JAY Rs 5 lakh, Jan Aushadhi; GHE 1.43% of GDP vs 2.5% target.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2016 (GS Paper I) - Crop insurance

With reference to 'Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana', consider the following statements:

  1. Under this scheme, farmers will have to pay a uniform premium of two percent for any crop they cultivate in any season of the year.
  2. This scheme covers post-harvest losses arising out of cyclones and unseasonal rains.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (b). Premium is not uniform: 2% kharif, 1.5% rabi, 5% commercial/horticultural. Post-harvest losses are covered. PMFBY is the main tool of insurance inclusion for farmers (Section 1.2).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2016 (GS Paper I) - Pension inclusion

Regarding 'Atal Pension Yojana', which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. It is a minimum guaranteed pension scheme mainly targeted at unorganized sector workers.
  2. Only one member of a family can join the scheme.
  3. Same amount of pension is guaranteed for the spouse for life after subscriber's death.

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (c). APY guarantees a minimum pension (Rs 1,000-5,000) to unorganised workers; there is no one-member-per-family limit; the spouse receives the same pension for life (Section 1.3).

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017 (GS Paper I) - Nutrition (preventive health)

Which of the following are the objectives of 'National Nutrition Mission'?

  1. To create awareness relating to malnutrition among pregnant women and lactating mothers.
  2. To reduce the incidence of anaemia among young children, adolescent girls and women.
  3. To promote the consumption of millets, coarse cereals and unpolished rice.
  4. To promote the consumption of poultry eggs.

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 1, 2 and 3 only
  3. 1, 2 and 4 only
  4. 3 and 4 only

Answer: (a). Awareness and anaemia reduction are stated objectives; promoting specific foods is not. Better nutrition is preventive health care point (c) (Section 3.1).

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - Teacher quality

Consider the following statements:

  1. As per the Right to Education (RTE) Act, to be eligible for appointment as a teacher in a State, a person would be required to possess the minimum qualification laid down by the concerned State Council of Teacher Education.
  2. As per the RTE Act, for teaching primary classes, a candidate is required to pass a Teacher Eligibility Test conducted in accordance with the National Council of Teacher Education guidelines.
  3. In India, more than 90% of teacher education institutions are directly under the State Governments.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 and 2
  2. 2 only
  3. 1 and 3
  4. 3 only

Answer: (b). Minimum qualifications are laid down by the NCTE (not State councils); TET is required; most teacher education institutions are private, not State-run. Links to recruiting teachers "on the basis of proper aptitude" (Section 2.1).
