1. Fiscal Policy and the Budget

1.1 Meaning and Institutions

Fiscal policy deals with the Government's income (receipts), expenditure and borrowings. The Ministry of Finance plays the key role, and within it the Department of Economic Affairs (DEA). Fiscal policy is implemented mainly through the Budget.

[Correction] Fiscal policy is the use of taxation, public expenditure and public borrowing to influence growth, employment, price stability and the distribution of income. The Budget is its main annual instrument, but not the only one: in-year changes (supplementary demands for grants, tax-rate and duty notifications, GST Council rate decisions), transfers recommended by Finance Commissions and the rules of the FRBM Act (26 September notes) also form part of fiscal policy. [Additional] Within the Ministry of Finance the DEA's Budget Division prepares the Budget, the Department of Revenue (CBDT for direct taxes, CBIC for GST, customs and central excise) administers taxes, and the Department of Expenditure controls spending.

1.2 The Budget

  • The Budget is presented in Parliament by the Union Finance Minister on 1 February (Union Budget 2026-27: 1 February 2026).
  • The word "budget" is not mentioned in the Constitution of India.

[Correction] Correct, and the precise position is: Article 112 requires the President to lay before both Houses an "annual financial statement" - the estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for each financial year (text verified at the Constitution of India website). Article 112(2) also says the estimates "shall distinguish expenditure on revenue account from other expenditure" - the constitutional basis of the revenue-capital split in Section 2. "Budget" is the popular name. The Budget is not law by itself: Parliament must pass the Appropriation Bill (expenditure) and the Finance Bill (tax proposals). [Verification Required] The 1 February date has been the practice since 2017 (earlier, the last working day of February) - standard fact not re-opened from a primary source; Articles 113-114 (demands for grants, Appropriation Bill) were also not re-opened.

[Additional] The Budget has two parts: the Revenue Budget (revenue receipts and revenue expenditure) and the Capital Budget (capital receipts and capital expenditure) - see Prelims 2016 in the PYQ section. The Union Budget is a cash-based statement of one financial year; the money flows through the Consolidated Fund of India.

2. Keywords: Receipts and Expenditure

The Government's accounts are classified by one test: does the transaction change the Government's assets or liabilities? If not, it is a revenue item; if yes, it is a capital item.

2.1 Receipts: Revenue vs Capital

Revenue receipts vs capital receipts
BasisRevenue receiptsCapital receipts
TestNeither assets lost nor liabilities createdEither assets lost or liabilities created
Nature (class note)RecurringNon-recurring
Examples (class)Income tax, corporation taxSelling Government property; selling Government companies (for example, Air India); taking loans
[Additional] Components in the BudgetTax revenue (net to the Centre, after States' share) + non-tax revenue (interest receipts, dividends and profits, external grants, other non-tax receipts, Union Territory receipts)Debt receipts (borrowings, a liability) + non-debt receipts (recovery of loans, disinvestment and other receipts: asset lost)
[Additional] 2026-27 BE (Rs crore)35,33,150 = net tax 28,66,922 + non-tax 6,66,228 (of which dividends and profits 3,91,000)18,14,165 = borrowings and other liabilities 16,95,768 + recovery of loans 38,397 + other receipts 80,000

[Correction] Revenue receipts are tax and non-tax revenue; only the tax half was named in class. They are not "income" in the sense of profit: the Government earns them by taxing and by owning assets (dividends, interest), and they leave the Government's balance sheet unchanged. Note that a grant received from abroad is also a revenue receipt (external grants Rs 2,327 crore in 2026-27 BE), although a grant given to a State is a revenue expenditure.

[Correction] "Non-recurring" is textbook shorthand and true item by item (a company can be sold only once), but borrowing is a capital receipt that recurs every year: borrowings and other liabilities form the largest capital receipt (Rs 16,95,768 crore, about 93% of capital receipts in 2026-27 BE). The correct test is the effect on assets and liabilities, not how often the receipt occurs. Also: recovery of loans is a capital receipt because the Government's loan asset falls; disinvestment (sale of shares in public-sector companies, for example the 2022 sale of Air India to Tata Sons) lowers the Government's equity assets. [Verification Required] The Air India sale date and buyer are a standard fact not re-opened; check the Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) record before quoting.

2.2 Expenditure: Revenue vs Capital

Revenue expenditure vs capital expenditure
BasisRevenue expenditureCapital expenditure
TestNeither assets created nor liabilities reducedEither assets created or liabilities reduced
Nature (class note)RecurringNon-recurring
Examples (class)Salaries, pensions, subsidies, public administration and maintenance, grantsSpending on roads, railway tracks, infrastructure projects; repayment of loans
[Additional] 2026-27 BE (Rs crore)41,25,494 (77.2% of total expenditure), of which interest payments 14,03,972 (34.0% of revenue expenditure) and grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets 4,92,70212,21,821 (22.8% of total expenditure). Effective capital expenditure = capital expenditure + grants-in-aid for capital assets = 12,21,821 + 4,92,702 = 17,14,523

[Correction] Revenue expenditure is Government spending on its day-to-day functions and obligations; it includes interest payments (the single largest item) and grants to States. Trap: a grant given to a State to build roads or schools is still revenue expenditure of the Centre, because the asset is created on the State's books, not the Centre's. The Budget therefore separates "grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets" (Rs 4,92,702 crore) and adds them to capital expenditure to get effective capital expenditure (Rs 17,14,523 crore); the effective revenue deficit (Rs 99,642 crore, about 0.3% of GDP) is the revenue deficit minus these grants (26 September notes).

[Correction] "Repayment of loans" as an example of capital expenditure is right in concept (a liability is reduced), but in the Union Budget's presentation the Government shows its debt receipts net of repayments ("the receipts are net of payment", Budget at a Glance), so principal repayment does not appear inside "capital expenditure" or "total expenditure". Capital expenditure in the Budget consists of capital outlay (roads, railways, defence equipment) and loans and advances given to States, Union Territories and others (a financial asset is created). Interest on debt is revenue expenditure, not capital.

2.3 Balanced, Surplus and Deficit Budgets

Budget balance (class note, made precise)
PositionClass notePrecise statement
Balanced budgetIncome = expenditureNon-borrowing receipts = total expenditure (no borrowing needed)
Surplus budgetIncome > expenditureNon-borrowing receipts > total expenditure (the Government can repay debt or build cash)
Deficit budgetIncome < expenditureNon-borrowing receipts < total expenditure; the gap is financed by borrowing and other liabilities

[Correction] "Income" here must exclude borrowings; if loans were counted as income, every budget would balance by definition. The balance can be measured at several levels, which is why there are several deficits (table below). Fiscal deficit - "how much the Government plans to borrow in a financial year" - is correct in meaning: it is the Government's net borrowing requirement (borrowings and other liabilities) and a flow variable (per year), unlike public debt, a stock (25 September notes). In 2026-27 BE it is financed by Rs 16,63,066 crore of net debt receipts plus Rs 32,702 crore of drawdown of cash balance (Budget at a Glance, "Receipts" statement). [Additional] The older "budgetary deficit" (total expenditure over total receipts, financed by RBI credit) was discontinued after the late-1990s reforms; the Union Budget now reports revenue, fiscal, primary and effective revenue deficits - [Verification Required] for the exact year of discontinuation.

Deficit measures (Union Government)

Revenue deficit = Revenue expenditure − Revenue receipts

Effective revenue deficit = Revenue deficit − Grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets

Fiscal deficit = Total expenditure − (Revenue receipts + Recovery of loans + Other (non-debt) capital receipts)

Primary deficit = Fiscal deficit − Interest payments

All terms are flows in Rs crore for one financial year; "other capital receipts" is mainly disinvestment. Worked check with 2026-27 BE: fiscal deficit = 53,47,315 − (35,33,150 + 38,397 + 80,000) = 16,95,768; primary deficit = 16,95,768 − 14,03,972 = 2,91,796.

Exam point: fiscal deficit = revenue deficit + capital expenditure − non-debt capital receipts. Check with 2026-27 BE: 5,92,344 + (12,21,821 − 1,18,397) = 5,92,344 + 11,03,424 = 16,95,768. So the fiscal deficit exceeds the revenue deficit whenever capital expenditure exceeds non-debt capital receipts, and the part of the borrowing not explained by the revenue deficit (here Rs 11,03,424 crore) is what funds net capital spending. Fiscal deficit, revenue deficit and primary deficit are flows; debt is the stock (see Figure 1 of the 26 September notes for the 2017-18 to 2026-27 trend).

[Correction] "Calendar year is used to compare different economies" is only partly right. Countries use different years (India, the UK and Japan: April-March; Australia: July-June; the US federal Government: October-September), so comparison needs the two series to be re-expressed on the same period or the mismatch to be flagged; there is no single rule that all comparisons use calendar years. "Most economic data are compared year-on-year (YoY)" is right: YoY compares a period with the same period of the previous year, which removes seasonality (11 October notes on inflation). Quarter-on-quarter and month-on-month changes are also used for turning points. [Verification Required] The financial-year definition in the General Clauses Act, 1897 and the practice of other countries are standard facts not re-opened from primary sources.

4. Tax, Duty and Fee

Tax vs duty vs fee
BasisTaxDutyFee
MeaningA compulsory payment to the GovernmentA tax imposed on a commodity or good (on its manufacture, sale or movement across the border)Paid to the Government or the private sector for a service
Quid pro quo?No - no direct benefit to the payerNo (it is a tax)Yes - the payer gets something in return
Examples (class)Income tax, corporation taxExcise duty on liquor; customs duty on crude oilCoaching fees; driving licence fee

[Correction] The tax-fee distinction is right: a tax is levied by the State by authority of law (Article 265: no tax may be levied or collected except by authority of law) and its proceeds go to the general pool, not to a specific service for the payer. A fee is paid for a specific service and is usually in proportion to its cost; coaching fees are a price paid to a private provider and a driving-licence fee is paid to the State. [Additional] A cess is a tax earmarked for a specified purpose (for example the Health and Education Cess on income tax); a fine or penalty is a punishment, not a tax.

[Correction] The two class examples of duty need updating. Excise duty on alcohol for human consumption is levied by the States (State excise) and is outside GST; the Union levies central excise on petroleum products and tobacco products (tobacco taxation was reported to change again on 1 February 2026; see Section 5.3). Customs duty on crude oil: the current tariff on crude petroleum was not re-checked - [Verification Required]; a safer textbook example of customs duty is the duty on imported gold or electronics. Duty is therefore "a tax on goods" (an indirect tax), but not every tax on goods is called a duty: GST is called a tax.

5. Types of Taxes: Direct and Indirect

5.1 Direct vs Indirect Tax

Direct tax vs indirect tax
BasisDirect taxIndirect tax
Imposed onIncome and wealth of persons and companiesGoods and services (their production, sale, import or export)
BurdenCannot be shifted: the person on whom it is levied bears it (impact and incidence on the same person)Can be shifted: collected from the seller, passed on to the final buyer (impact on seller, incidence on consumer)
Examples (class)Income tax, corporation tax, capital gains taxExcise duty (goods manufactured domestically); customs duty (imports and exports); sales tax / VAT (intra-State transactions); central sales tax (inter-State); GST replaced most of these
[Additional] Rate patternCan be progressive (higher rate on higher income)One rate per item, so the burden is a larger share of a poor person's income (regressive in effect)
[Additional] Share of Union gross tax revenue, 2026-27 BE61.2% (income tax 14,66,000 + corporation tax 12,31,000 = 26,97,000 Rs crore)38.1% (GST 10,19,020 + excise 3,88,910 + customs 2,71,200 = 16,79,130 Rs crore)

[Correction] The principle holds, but treat "burden cannot be shifted" as the textbook definition: a direct tax is one where the legal payer is meant to bear the burden, whereas in practice a firm may pass part of a corporation tax into its prices. Use the impact vs incidence distinction: impact is the point where the tax is first levied; incidence is who finally bears it. Direct tax: both on the same person. Indirect tax: impact on the seller, incidence on the consumer. Wealth: India has no wealth tax today (it was abolished in the 2015 Budget and replaced by a higher surcharge on the super-rich) - [Verification Required] standard fact not re-opened; the main direct taxes are income tax (individuals, HUFs and others), corporation tax (companies), and, in the Budget's own grouping, the Securities Transaction Tax (STT) inside "income tax". Capital gains tax is a tax on income from the sale of a capital asset and is part of income tax.

5.2 Composition of Union Tax Revenue

[Correction] The indirect-tax examples in the rough notes mix current and pre-2017 taxes. (i) Excise duty is a tax on the manufacture of goods; after GST, central excise survives mainly on petroleum products and tobacco. (ii) Customs duty is levied on goods crossing the border; India's customs revenue is almost entirely import duty (Rs 2,71,200 crore, 2026-27 BE), export duties being rare. (iii) Sales tax / VAT (intra-State) and central sales tax (inter-State) were pre-GST taxes: VAT was levied by the State, and CST by the Centre but collected and kept by the origin State; both were subsumed in GST on 1 July 2017. (iv) "GST replaced the majority of indirect taxes" - correct; PIB counts 17 taxes and 13 cesses subsumed (see below).

5.4 The Income-tax Act, 2025

[Additional] Direct tax law has also changed: the Income-tax Act, 2025 (passed by Parliament on 12 August 2025, assent 21 August 2025) came into force on 1 April 2026, replacing the Income-tax Act, 1961, with a simpler text and a unified "tax year" in place of the "previous year" and "assessment year" pair (PIB and CBDT, April 2026). Key Facts for Prelims

  • Fiscal policy = government receipts, expenditure and borrowing; Ministry of Finance (DEA prepares the Budget). The Constitution does not use the word "budget": Article 112 - "annual financial statement".
  • Budget presented on 1 February (Budget 2026-27: 1 February 2026); financial year = 1 April-31 March.
  • Revenue receipts: no asset lost, no liability created (taxes, non-tax). Capital receipts: asset lost or liability created (borrowings, disinvestment, loan recovery).
  • Revenue expenditure: no asset created, no liability reduced (salaries, pensions, subsidies, interest, grants). Capital expenditure: asset created or liability reduced (roads, railways, loans and advances).
  • Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts) = net borrowing; primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest; revenue deficit = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts.
  • 2026-27 BE: fiscal deficit Rs 16,95,768 crore (4.3% of GDP); revenue deficit 1.5%; primary deficit 0.7%; effective revenue deficit 0.3%.
  • Tax: compulsory, no quid pro quo (Article 265: authority of law). Duty: tax on goods. Fee: quid pro quo.
  • Direct (income, corporation, capital gains): burden on the payer. Indirect (GST, customs, excise): burden shifted to the consumer.
  • GST: 1 July 2017; 101st Amendment Act 2016; GST Council; 17 taxes and 13 cesses subsumed; rates 5% / 18% / 40% since 22 September 2025.
  • Income-tax Act, 2025 in force from 1 April 2026.

Mains Points

  • Quality of expenditure: revenue vs capital split matters; capital expenditure is 22.8% of total expenditure (2026-27 BE), rising to 32.1% if grants for capital assets are added (effective capex Rs 17,14,523 crore). Interest payments alone are 34.0% of revenue expenditure.
  • Deficits and sustainability: borrowed money is "good" if it funds capital assets that raise future income; the revenue deficit (1.5% of GDP) shows part of borrowing still funds consumption (26 September notes).
  • Direct vs indirect trade-off: direct taxes are progressive and more equitable but harder to collect from a large informal economy; indirect taxes are easier to collect but regressive. The Union's gross tax revenue is 61.2% direct, 38.1% indirect (2026-27 BE).
  • GST and cooperative federalism: the GST Council is a joint Centre-State body; GST removed cascading and inter-State barriers; the cost is loss of States' own tax autonomy and a revenue-compensation question (the compensation cess ended). The 2025 rate cut to two main slabs simplified the structure but cut tax rates on many items - a revenue-vs-simplicity trade-off.
  • Budget as a constitutional-democratic exercise: Article 112 and the Appropriation and Finance Bills give Parliament control over taxation and expenditure; the Budget documents allow outcomes to be audited.

30-Second Revision

Fiscal policy = Government receipts, expenditure and borrowing, run through the Budget (Ministry of Finance; Constitution: Article 112 "annual financial statement"; presented 1 February). Revenue items leave assets and liabilities unchanged (taxes, interest, salaries, subsidies); capital items change them (borrowing, disinvestment, roads, loans). Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − non-debt receipts = net borrowing, a flow (4.3% of GDP, 2026-27 BE); primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest. Financial year = 1 April-31 March. Tax = compulsory, no quid pro quo; duty = tax on goods; fee = quid pro quo. Direct taxes (income, corporation, capital gains) 61.2% and indirect (GST, customs, excise) 38.1% of gross tax revenue. GST from 1 July 2017 replaced 17 taxes and 13 cesses; rates 5% / 18% / 40% since 22 September 2025; Income-tax Act 2025 from 1 April 2026.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2016 (GS Paper I) - Capital budget

Which of the following is/are included in the capital budget of the Government of India?

  1. Expenditure on acquisition of assets like roads, buildings, machinery, etc.
  2. Loans received from foreign governments
  3. Loans and advances granted to the States and Union Territories

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (d). The capital budget covers capital receipts and capital expenditure: asset creation is capital expenditure, loans received create a liability (capital receipt), and loans and advances given to States create a financial asset (capital expenditure) - see Section 2.1 and Correction 6.

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2025 (GS Paper I) - Capital receipts

Consider the following statements:

  1. Capital receipts create a liability or cause a reduction in the assets of the Government.
  2. Borrowings and disinvestment are capital receipts.
  3. Interest received on loans creates a liability of the Government.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  1. I and II only
  2. II and III only
  3. I and III only
  4. I, II and III

Answer: (a). Statements I and II restate the capital-receipt test and examples (Correction 4); interest received on loans is a revenue receipt (non-tax revenue) and creates no liability, so III is wrong.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2025 (GS Paper I) - Deficits

Suppose the revenue expenditure is Rs 80,000 crores and the revenue receipts of the Government are Rs 60,000 crores. The Government budget also shows borrowings of Rs 10,000 crores and interest payments of Rs 6,000 crores. Which of the following statements are correct?

  1. Revenue deficit is Rs 20,000 crores.
  2. Fiscal deficit is Rs 10,000 crores.
  3. Primary deficit is Rs 4,000 crores.
  1. I and II only
  2. II and III only
  3. I and III only
  4. I, II and III

Answer: (d). Revenue deficit = 80,000 − 60,000 = 20,000; fiscal deficit = borrowings = 10,000; primary deficit = 10,000 − 6,000 = 4,000 (formula box).

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2025 (GS Paper I) - Primary deficit

A country's fiscal deficit stands at Rs 50,000 crores. It is receiving Rs 10,000 crores through non-debt creating capital receipts. The country's interest liabilities are Rs 1,500 crores. What is the gross primary deficit?

  1. Rs 48,500 crores
  2. Rs 51,500 crores
  3. Rs 58,500 crores
  4. None of the above

Answer: (a). Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest = 50,000 − 1,500 = 48,500. The non-debt receipts are already netted inside the fiscal deficit, so they do not change the calculation.

  1. Reducing revenue expenditure

  3. Rationalizing subsidies

Corrections Made

  1. Fiscal policy: taxation, expenditure and borrowing; the Budget is the main but not the only instrument; DEA's Budget Division.
  2. Budget and the Constitution: Article 112 uses "annual financial statement"; revenue-capital split; Appropriation and Finance Bills.
  3. Revenue receipts: tax and non-tax revenue; external grants are revenue receipts.
  4. Capital receipts: "non-recurring" is item-wise shorthand; borrowing recurs every year; loan recovery and disinvestment.
  5. Revenue expenditure: grants to States for assets are revenue expenditure; effective capital expenditure.
  6. Capital expenditure: debt receipts are netted of repayments in the Budget; loans and advances are capital expenditure.
  7. Balanced, surplus and deficit budgets: "income" excludes borrowing; fiscal deficit is net borrowing; flow vs stock.
  8. Financial vs calendar year: not all comparisons use calendar years; YoY removes seasonality.
  9. Tax vs fee: Article 265; cess; fines are not taxes.
  10. Duty examples: alcohol excise is a State levy outside GST; crude-oil customs example flagged.
  11. Direct tax: impact vs incidence; wealth tax abolished; STT and capital gains.
  12. Indirect-tax examples: excise, customs, sales tax/VAT/CST updated for GST.

Spelling fixes in the rough notes: "Reciepts" (receipts), "raods" (roads), "Calandar" (calendar), "compulsarily" (compulsorily), "coporation" (corporation), "Quid-pro-quo" (quid pro quo). "Is a flow var" is expanded to "flow variable".
