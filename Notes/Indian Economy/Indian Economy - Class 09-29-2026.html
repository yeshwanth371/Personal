1. Answer-Writing: GDP as a Measure of Welfare

Question (class): Critically analyse the role of GDP as a measure of welfare.

India's GDP growth has averaged about 6.5% a year over the last 35 years, and this is considered good for welfare because of the trickle-down effect.

[Correction] Growth here must mean real GDP growth (constant prices), not nominal (see 25 September, Section 2.1). The long-run average of "about 6.5%" since the 1991 reforms is a reasonable class approximation, but the exact figure depends on the national-accounts series and base year used (2004-05, 2011-12 or the new 2022-23 series) - [Verification Required] before quoting a precise number. Trickle-down is a hypothesis, not an automatic result (25 September correction).

1.1 Why High GDP Growth Supports Welfare

  1. High GDP provides more goods and services, which contributes to a better standard of living.
  2. High GDP growth creates a surplus (savings and tax revenue) that can be used for investment.
  3. Employment generation improves with GDP growth.
  4. Better technological upgradation is observed with high GDP growth.
  5. [Additional] GDP is objective, regular and internationally comparable (measured under the UN System of National Accounts), so it is the most practical single summary of economic activity.

1.2 Why GDP Growth Is Not Sufficient for Welfare

1.4 Model Answer (about 250 words)

Introduction. GDP is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country in a year. India's real GDP has grown at roughly 6-6.5% a year since 1991, and faster growth is often assumed to raise welfare through trickle-down.

GDP as a welfare measure - strengths

  1. More goods and services raise average consumption and living standards.
  2. Growth creates a surplus (savings, taxes) for investment and welfare spending.
  3. It can create jobs and move labour out of low-productivity farming.
  4. It finances technology upgradation and infrastructure.
  5. It is regular, objective and internationally comparable.

Limitations

  1. Population growth dilutes gains; per capita income matters.
  2. GDP ignores distribution: India's HDI (0.685) falls to 0.475 after adjusting for inequality.
  3. Pollution and resource depletion are not deducted; defensive spending even adds to GDP.
  4. Capital-intensive growth may be jobless.
  5. Unpaid care work, leisure and informal output are excluded.
  6. Composition is ignored - arms and tobacco count like schools.
  7. Transfer payments are excluded, so redistribution is invisible.

Better assessment. Use a dashboard: real per capita income, Gini coefficient, HDI/IHDI, NITI Aayog's Multidimensional Poverty Index (29.17% to 11.28%, 2013-14 to 2022-23), nutrition (NFHS), Green GDP (SEEA) and the SDG India Index, as the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission recommended.

Conclusion. GDP growth is necessary but not sufficient for welfare. Policy should pair growth with measures of health, education, nutrition, equity and sustainability.

Exam point: "Critically analyse" requires both sides + a judgement. Structure: define GDP → how it supports welfare → where it fails → alternative indicators with one data point each → balanced conclusion ("necessary but not sufficient").

2. Steps for Intragenerational Equity / Inclusive Growth: Public Distribution System (PDS)

The Public Distribution System procures food grains from farmers and distributes them at subsidised (now free) prices to identified beneficiaries through fair price shops (ration shops). It is a jointly run Centre-State system and India's largest food-security network.

2.1 Origin: FCI, MSP and Ration Shops

  • The government established the Food Corporation of India (FCI) to purchase food grains from farmers at the Minimum Support Price (MSP), and to distribute them at a cheaper rate through the PDS.
  • Under the PDS, the State was required to establish fair price shops (ration shops) by giving licences to traders. The State government was also required to identify beneficiaries by issuing ration cards.
  • The Central government provided financial support to procure food grains and then store, transport and allocate them, to be distributed at a subsidised rate up to a pre-fixed amount among beneficiaries.
  • Ration shops were later used to distribute other essential commodities as well (for example, sugar, kerosene, edible oil).

[Correction] FCI was set up on 14 January 1965 (not 1966) under the Food Corporations Act, 1964. The Agricultural Prices Commission (1965; renamed Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, CACP, in 1985) recommends MSPs. The PDS itself is older: it grew out of Second World War food rationing and was re-introduced after Independence to control food prices. [Additional] FCI buys at MSP, but the cost of PDS grain to the government is higher - the economic cost (see formula).

[Correction] "Vendor PDS" should read "Under PDS". The division of roles is: Centre - procurement, storage, inter-State transport and bulk allocation to States, and bearing the food subsidy; States/UTs - intra-State distribution, identification of beneficiaries, issue of ration cards, licensing and supervision of fair price shops. Under NFSA the Centre also shares the cost of intra-State transport, handling and FPS dealers' margin.

[Correction] The year "after 1978" for adding other essential commodities could not be confirmed from an official source - [Verification Required]. It is safe to write that the PDS basket historically included sugar, kerosene and edible oil in addition to cereals, varying by State and period.

2.2 Targeted PDS (TPDS)

The government introduced Targeted PDS, under which people were identified as Above Poverty Line (APL) and Below Poverty Line (BPL). APL families could buy grain at a higher issue price; BPL families paid about 50% of the cost.

[Correction] (i) TPDS was introduced in June 1997, not 1998. (ii) BPL families were initially entitled to 10 kg per family per month at 50% of the economic cost (not 50% of MSP); the entitlement was later raised, reaching 35 kg by 2002. The rough-note figure of 25 kg matches an intermediate step (2001) - [Verification Required]. (iii) APL families paid a higher price that moved to the full economic cost from 2000-01; they did not pay "the MSP". (iv) [Additional] Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY) was added in December 2000 for the "poorest of the poor", now 35 kg per household. (v) BPL identification used the BPL Census (2002) and State poverty ratios - a source of large targeting errors.

2.4 Benefits of PDS / NFSA

  1. It has helped in significantly reducing hunger in India.
  2. Free food grains allow people to have more money left for other expenditure (an implicit income transfer).
  3. Schemes like PM POSHAN (mid-day meal) and anganwadi nutrition can control malnutrition among children, which can prevent disease.
  4. It creates employment by developing a supply chain of food grains - production, trading, transporting, storing and distributing.
  5. It provides assured income to farmers (procurement at MSP), which helps in reducing rural poverty.
  6. [Additional] Shock absorber: PM-GKAY protected poor households during the COVID-19 income shock; ONORC lets migrant workers draw rations anywhere (more than 195.9 crore portability transactions since August 2019).
  7. [Additional] Price stabilisation: buffer stocks and Open Market Sale Scheme releases (for example, Bharat Atta and Bharat Rice) moderate food inflation.
Value of the PDS transfer to a household = (Pmarket - PPDS) × QPDS
Pmarket = open-market price per kg; PPDS = price paid at the ration shop (now zero); QPDS = kg received per month.
Illustrative (not an official estimate): a 5-member PHH family gets 25 kg free; at about Rs 35 per kg (the Bharat Rice MRP, used only as a price proxy) the implicit income transfer is about Rs 875 a month.

[Correction] Two benefits need qualification. (i) "Significantly reduced hunger": PDS has clearly improved calorie (cereal) security and reduced poverty - academic estimates using NSS data suggest it cut the rural poverty gap by about 18-22% in 2009-10 (Dreze and Khera, EPW 2013) - but nutrition outcomes remain weak (stunting 35.5%, NFHS-5). (ii) "Assured income to farmers": MSP procurement benefits are concentrated in a few States (Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Telangana and others) and among farmers with marketable surplus; the Shanta Kumar Committee (2015) found only about 6% of farm households sold to procurement agencies at MSP (NSSO 70th round). [Additional] Scale (official): 300.35 LMT wheat procured in RMS 2025-26 (25.1 lakh farmers) and 832.17 LMT paddy in KMS 2024-25 (1.19 crore farmers).

2.5 Challenges of PDS / NFSA

  1. It has not been able to provide nutrition security to a large number of people (the basket is mainly cereals; protein, pulses, micronutrients are missing).
  2. It has resulted in over-production of wheat and rice, with a negative impact through ecological degradation (groundwater depletion, stubble burning, soil health in Punjab-Haryana).
  3. The quality of food grains distributed has been poor in certain regions.
  4. It has large inclusion error - many people who should ideally not get the subsidy are included. This has resulted in diversion of grain for open-market sale and even for cattle feed.
  5. It creates exclusion error - vulnerable groups are deprived due to failure of biometric authentication, closure of shops, or lack of stock on a particular day.
  6. Lack of proper record keeping leads to leakages in distribution.
  7. [Additional] Outdated coverage base: State ceilings are frozen on Census 2011, so population growth since then is not covered - a source of large exclusion.
  8. [Additional] Fiscal cost: food subsidy released was Rs 1,99,500 crore in 2024-25; Rs 2,03,000 crore allocated for 2025-26.
Inclusion error (%) = Ineligible beneficiaries receiving the benefit / Total beneficiaries × 100
Exclusion error (%) = Eligible persons not receiving the benefit / Total eligible persons × 100

2.6 Way Forward

  1. Digitisation of records should be promoted to assure fewer leakages.
  2. There should be alternative identification methods (OTP, iris, nominee, manual override with audit) so that exclusion error is reduced.
  3. Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) using digital money can be an option in urban areas to reduce the burden of transportation, storage and distribution.
  4. The number of beneficiaries should be rationalised using the latest Census.
  5. There should be a focus on nutrition by introducing fortified food to reduce malnutrition.
  6. [Additional] Diversify the basket - millets (nutri-cereals), pulses and edible oil - which also eases the rice-wheat ecological burden.
  7. [Additional] End-to-end supply-chain reforms - route optimisation (Anna Chakra), steel silos, depot rating (Depot Darpan), AI-based grievance feedback (ASHA) - and social audits.

[Correction] Update the way forward to the current status. (i) Digitisation is largely achieved: 100% ration-card data digitised, more than 99.9% Aadhaar seeding, 99.8% of 5.51 lakh fair price shops have ePoS devices, and more than 98% of transactions are biometrically or Aadhaar authenticated (PIB, 31 December 2025) - the remaining task is last-mile reliability and grievance response. (ii) Fortified rice is already supplied through PDS and nutrition schemes; its supply has been extended to December 2028. (iii) DBT for food has run in the UTs of Chandigarh, Puducherry and Dadra and Nagar Haveli since 2015; evaluations found delays and higher access costs for some beneficiaries, so DBT should be optional and limited to areas with strong banking and markets. (iv) The next Census (reference date 1 March 2027) will allow State ceilings to be revised - [Verification Required] for the final Census schedule.

[Additional] Shanta Kumar High Level Committee (January 2015) on restructuring FCI recommended reducing NFSA coverage from 67% to about 40%, a gradual shift to cash transfers in cities with a population above 1 million, handing procurement to States that are already efficient, and end-to-end computerisation. Use it as a contrasting view in answers, since its coverage recommendation was not adopted.

4. Model Answer: Critically Analyse the Role of PDS in Inclusive Growth (250 words)

Introduction. Inclusive growth means growth whose benefits reach the poor and vulnerable. The Public Distribution System (PDS), made a legal entitlement by the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013, is India's largest instrument for this.

Role in inclusive growth

  1. Right to food: up to 75% of the rural and 50% of the urban population (81.35 crore) get 5 kg grain per person (35 kg per Antyodaya household) a month, free under PM-GKAY till December 2028.
  2. Income support: free grain releases household income for health and education; studies estimate PDS reduced the rural poverty gap by about one-fifth (2009-10).
  3. Life-cycle nutrition: maternity benefit (at least Rs 6,000), anganwadi meals and PM POSHAN link food to human capital.
  4. Farmer support: MSP procurement (832 LMT paddy, 2024-25) stabilises farm incomes and prices.
  5. Resilience: PM-GKAY cushioned the COVID-19 shock; One Nation One Ration Card serves migrants.

Limitations

  1. Cereal-centric: stunting is still 35.5% (NFHS-5).
  2. Coverage frozen on Census 2011 excludes the new poor; inclusion errors and diversion persist.
  3. Biometric failures and shop closures exclude the vulnerable.
  4. MSP-led rice-wheat bias depletes groundwater and helps a minority of farmers.
  5. Fiscal cost: food subsidy of about Rs 2 lakh crore a year.

Way forward. Update beneficiary lists with the next Census; allow non-biometric authentication; diversify the basket with millets, pulses and fortified rice; offer DBT as an option where banking and markets are strong; strengthen social audits and grievance redressal.

Conclusion. PDS has turned growth into a floor of food security for 80 crore people. Making it nutrition-sensitive and better targeted will make growth truly inclusive.

Exam point: in "critically analyse" answers on PDS, use at least two official numbers (coverage 81.35 crore / about 80 crore; ePoS 99.8%; food subsidy about Rs 2 lakh crore) and one evidence-based limitation (NFHS-5 stunting 35.5%; Census 2011 base).

Mains Points

  • GDP vs welfare: GDP measures production, not well-being. Growth is necessary but not sufficient; distribution, environment, employment and capabilities decide welfare.
  • PDS as a tool of intragenerational equity: a legal floor of food security for about 80 crore people; income transfer effect; life-cycle nutrition; shock absorber (COVID-19); portability for migrants (ONORC).
  • Targeting trade-off: inclusion errors waste money; exclusion errors deny rights. Near-universal coverage with self-exclusion is simpler than precise targeting, but more costly.
  • Nutrition, not just calories: the cereal basket (millets only about 1.4% of allocation) does not match the problem (stunting 35.5%). Diversify towards millets, pulses and fortified foods.
  • Ecological link: open-ended MSP procurement of rice and wheat distorts cropping patterns and depletes groundwater - food security today versus water security tomorrow (intergenerational equity).
  • Cash vs kind: DBT cuts logistics costs and gives choice, but in-kind grain protects against food inflation and works where banks and markets are weak; pilots show transition costs.
  • Technology with safeguards: ePoS and Aadhaar reduce identity fraud; alternative authentication and grievance redressal prevent exclusion.

30-Second Revision

GDP vs welfare: growth gives more goods, surplus for investment, jobs and technology, but ignores population growth (use per capita), pollution, inequality (HDI 0.685 → IHDI 0.475), jobless growth, non-market work, composition, and redistribution through transfers - so assess welfare with a dashboard (HDI, MPI 11.28%, NFHS, Green GDP, SDG Index). PDS: FCI 1965 (procure at MSP) → RPDS 1992 → TPDS 1997 (APL/BPL; BPL at 50% of economic cost) → AAY 2000 → NFSA 2013 (legal right; up to 75% rural/50% urban = 81.35 crore; PHH 5 kg/person, AAY 35 kg/household; maternity at least Rs 6,000; anganwadi and PM POSHAN meals; DGRO and State Food Commission; Food Security Allowance; force majeure) → free under PM-GKAY till December 2028. Benefits: hunger and poverty reduction, income effect, nutrition schemes, supply-chain jobs, farm price support, ONORC. Challenges: weak nutrition, rice-wheat ecology, quality, inclusion and exclusion errors, leakages, Census 2011 base, Rs 2 lakh crore subsidy. Way forward: last-mile reliability, alternative authentication, optional DBT, new Census, fortified and diversified basket.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - NFSA provisions

With reference to the provisions made under the National Food Security Act, 2013, consider the following statements:

  1. The families coming under the category of 'below poverty line (BPL)' only are eligible to receive subsidised food grains.
  2. The eldest woman in a household, of age 18 years or above, shall be the head of the household for the purpose of issuance of a ration card.
  3. Pregnant women and lactating mothers are entitled to a 'take-home ration' of 1600 calories per day during pregnancy and for six months thereafter.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 and 2
  2. 2 only
  3. 1 and 3
  4. 3 only

Answer: (b). NFSA covers Priority and Antyodaya households, not only BPL; the eldest woman is head of household; the Act entitles women to a free meal and at least Rs 6,000, not a specified 1600-calorie take-home ration (Section 2.3).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019 (GS Paper I) - Economic cost

The economic cost of food grains to the Food Corporation of India is Minimum Support Price and bonus (if any) paid to the farmers plus

  1. transportation cost only
  2. interest cost only
  3. procurement incidentals and distribution cost
  4. procurement incidentals and charges for godowns

Answer: (c). See the economic cost formula.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020 (GS Paper I) - MSP and procurement

Consider the following statements:

  1. In the case of all cereals, pulses and oil-seeds, the procurement at Minimum Support Price (MSP) is unlimited in any State/UT of India.
  2. In the case of cereals and pulses, the MSP is fixed in any State/UT at a level to which the market price will never rise.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (d). Procurement is limited and concentrated (mainly wheat and paddy in some States); MSP is a floor, and market prices often rise above it. Links to the farmer-income benefit and its limits (Section 2.4).
