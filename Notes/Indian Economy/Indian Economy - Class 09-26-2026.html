1. Sustainable Development / Intergenerational Equity

Sustainable development or intergenerational equity means that the development policies of the current generation should not compromise the development prospects of future generations.

[Correction] The two terms are closely linked but not identical. Sustainable development is the goal - "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs" (Brundtland Commission, Our Common Future, 1987). Intergenerational equity is the fairness principle behind it (fair sharing of resources, environment and debt between generations). Sustainability has three pillars - economic, social and environmental - so the class split into ecological and fiscal challenges covers two of them.

There are two main challenges to sustainable development:

1.1 Ecological Challenges

Deforestation, topsoil erosion, over-extraction of natural resources, water-table depletion, air and water pollution, lack of waste treatment, extinction of species (biodiversity loss), etc.

[Correction] "Extension of species" should be extinction of species (loss of biodiversity). These are cases of negative externalities and depletion of natural capital: the present generation enjoys the output, while future generations bear the cost of degraded soil, water, air and ecosystems.

1.2 Fiscal Challenges

These are related to poor revenue generation, wasteful expenditure on populism, poor management of the public sector leading to high losses, delay in completing projects (time and cost overruns), etc. All of these lead to a high fiscal deficit, which adds to the debt burden of the government.

Weak revenue, populist spending, PSU losses, cost overruns→High fiscal deficit
(flow)→More borrowing→Higher public debt
(stock)→Higher interest payments→Less fiscal space for future generations

[Correction] Precise link: the fiscal deficit is a flow (per year) equal to the government's net borrowing; each year's borrowing adds to the stock of public debt. The "burden" is mainly the interest future taxpayers must pay and the spending it crowds out. A deficit is not always harmful: borrowing for capital expenditure (roads, power, schools) creates assets that also benefit future generations, whereas borrowing for revenue expenditure (current consumption, untargeted subsidies) leaves debt without assets. That is why the revenue deficit is the sharper test of intergenerational fairness.

Fiscal deficit = Total expenditure - (Revenue receipts + Non-debt capital receipts)
Revenue deficit = Revenue expenditure - Revenue receipts; Primary deficit = Fiscal deficit - Interest payments.
Non-debt capital receipts = recovery of loans + other receipts (mainly disinvestment). Check with Budget 2026-27 BE (Rs crore): 53,47,315 - (35,33,150 + 38,397 + 80,000) = 16,95,768 = fiscal deficit (4.3% of GDP); 16,95,768 - 14,03,972 interest = 2,91,796 primary deficit (0.7% of GDP).
[Additional] Debt dynamics: the debt-to-GDP ratio falls when nominal GDP growth (g) exceeds the effective interest rate (r) and the primary deficit is kept small. Sustainability therefore depends on both growth and deficit control.

Exam point: Budget 2026-27 BE - fiscal deficit 4.3%, revenue deficit 1.5%, effective revenue deficit 0.3%, primary deficit 0.7% of GDP. Interest payments (Rs 14,03,972 crore) = about 39.7% of revenue receipts (Rs 35,33,150 crore), and are about 3.4 times the three major subsidies combined (food + fertiliser + petroleum = Rs 4,10,495 crore). Central Government debt (FRBM definition) is 55.6% of GDP (BE 2026-27), down from 61.4% in 2020-21.

1.3 Policy Solutions to Challenges of Intergenerational Equity

(A) Policies for Ecological Sustainability

  1. Promoting afforestation (and reforestation), and restricting activities such as poaching through wildlife protection.
  2. Making better-quality environmental laws to prevent over-exploitation of resources, and enforcing them.
  3. Training farmers in the right choice of crops suited to agro-climatic conditions (for example, less water-intensive crops in water-stressed regions).
  4. Controlling emissions from vehicles and factories through proper standards and restrictions.
  5. Setting up waste-treatment facilities so that untreated waste is not released into the environment.

[Correction] (i) "Aforestation" should be afforestation (planting forests on land that was not forest; reforestation = replanting deforested land). Afforestation does not itself "lead to" restrictions on poaching - they are two separate measures (habitat creation vs wildlife protection under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972). (ii) Untreated liquid and solid waste is released mainly into water bodies and land, not the atmosphere; air pollution is covered by the emissions point.

[Additional] India's updated Nationally Determined Contribution (Paris Agreement, submitted August 2022): reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45% from the 2005 level by 2030; reach about 50% cumulative installed electric power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030; create an additional carbon sink of 2.5-3 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent. The long-term goal is net zero by 2070 (announced at COP26, Glasgow, 2021).

(B) Policies for Fiscal Sustainability

  1. Improving tax collection through better identification of sources of income and by formalising the economy (GST, e-invoicing, digital payments widen the tax base).
  2. Better targeting of subsidies through better identification of beneficiaries and fixing accountability (see Section 2).
  3. Building political consensus to reduce wasteful subsidies.
  4. Better management of the public sector to reduce its losses (corporate governance, disinvestment, monetisation of idle assets).
  5. Better project design so that projects are completed on time without excessive cost overruns.

[Correction] Spelling: "consencus" = consensus. [Additional] The legal anchor for fiscal sustainability is the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 (fiscal deficit target 3% of GDP). From Budget 2025-26 the Centre shifted its main anchor to debt: keep fiscal deficits such that Central Government debt falls to about 50 ± 1% of GDP by 31 March 2031. The N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee (2017) had recommended a general government debt ceiling of 60% of GDP (Centre 40%, States 20%).

[Additional] "Freebies" has no statutory definition. Welfare spending on merit goods (education, health, nutrition, social security) builds capabilities and is usually treated as investment; non-merit subsidies (for example, free power to all, untargeted cash promises) have weaker justification. In S. Subramaniam Balaji v. State of Tamil Nadu (2013) the Supreme Court held that poll promises are not a "corrupt practice" under Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and asked the Election Commission to frame guidelines, which were added to the Model Code of Conduct. On 19 February 2026 a bench led by CJI Surya Kant orally observed that most States are revenue-deficit States and yet announce large free schemes (reported by The Times of India); the broader matter is still pending. [Verification Required] Oral remarks are not a judgment; confirm the current status of the case before citing it.

[Additional] Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) answers Q2 and Q3 together: benefits go directly into Aadhaar-seeded bank accounts, reducing leakages, ghost beneficiaries and intermediaries. Cumulative DBT: Rs 52,89,019 crore through 320 schemes of 56 Ministries (as on 10 August 2026; PIB, 12 August 2026).

Exam point: link the five questions to Section 1: well-targeted, time-bound subsidies on merit goods support intragenerational equity; untargeted, borrowed, open-ended subsidies hurt intergenerational equity. This trade-off is the core of any Mains answer on freebies.

3.1 Development of Physical Infrastructure

Physical infrastructure includes transportation, electrification (energy) and communication. Developing it creates more economic opportunities for marginalised sections.

  • Transport: better road links between rural and urban areas let people live in rural areas and work in urban areas. This increases employment and speeds up the transmission of development from urban to rural areas.
  • Electrification: increases the possible hours of work, enables mechanisation, raises productivity, and improves the quality of education and health services.
  • Communication: reduces search cost - the cost in money, time and resources incurred to find the best alternative (a job, a buyer, a price). It also creates new employment opportunities and supports better health and education services (telemedicine, online learning).

[Correction] Typos fixed ("velopment", "opprtunities", "incrurred", "deducation"). Physical (economic) infrastructure is wider than the three items: it also covers energy generally (power, oil and gas pipelines), water supply and irrigation, ports and airports. [Additional] The Department of Economic Affairs' Harmonised Master List of Infrastructure uses five categories: Transport and Logistics; Energy; Water and Sanitation; Communication; Social and Commercial Infrastructure.

3.2 Development of Social Infrastructure

Social infrastructure refers to civic amenities provided in an economy - housing, drinking water, sanitation, drainage, etc. These facilities reduce the cost of living. Policy support such as Housing for All, drinking water supply for all and better sanitation improves the standard of living of people who cannot afford these on their own.

[Correction] In standard usage, social infrastructure also includes education and health (schools, hospitals) - the facilities that build human capital. Civic amenities (housing, water, sanitation, drainage) are one part of it. Note also that the Harmonised Master List places water supply and sanitation under a separate "Water and Sanitation" category, so classification varies by source.

4. Financial Inclusion

Financial inclusion means providing access to financial services for all in the economy.

[Correction] Access alone is not enough. The standard definition (C. Rangarajan Committee on Financial Inclusion, 2008): "the process of ensuring access to financial services and timely and adequate credit where needed by vulnerable groups such as weaker sections and low income groups at an affordable cost". RBI's FI-Index therefore measures Access (35%), Usage (45%) and Quality (20%).

Banking+Credit+[Additional] Insurance+[Additional] Pension+[Additional] Financial literacy and digital payments→Financial inclusion

4.1 Banking Inclusion

Banking inclusion means providing a bank account for all. It gives everyone a financial identity, which helps in better distribution of subsidies (DBT) and credit to deprived people. It also encourages saving, because savings are better protected and earn returns (interest).

[Correction] "Better protection for savings" should be made specific: bank deposits are insured by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC), a wholly owned subsidiary of RBI, up to Rs 5 lakh per depositor per bank (principal + interest; limit raised from Rs 1 lakh with effect from 4 February 2020). Bank saving also moves household saving from unproductive forms (cash, gold) into financial saving that banks can lend for investment - a link that helps break the vicious circle of poverty.

4.2 Credit Inclusion

[Correction] The rough notes stop at this heading; the point is completed below.

Credit inclusion means providing timely, adequate and affordable formal credit to farmers, micro-entrepreneurs, street vendors, women and other excluded groups, so that they do not depend on moneylenders charging very high interest. Access to credit lets poor households invest in livelihoods (livestock, a shop, a sewing machine), smooth consumption during shocks and build assets.

Mains Points

  • Two equities, one budget: the same rupee spent can serve intragenerational equity (a targeted subsidy today) or intergenerational equity (capex, lower debt). Good policy favours merit goods and capital assets, targets transfers, and avoids borrowing for open-ended revenue subsidies.
  • Fiscal space: interest payments take about 40% of the Centre's revenue receipts (BE 2026-27); every rupee of interest is a rupee not spent on health, education or infrastructure. Revenue-deficit States offering unconditional freebies worsen this trade-off.
  • Subsidy reform: DBT and JAM answer "who" and "quality"; sunset clauses and independent evaluation answer "when". Political consensus is needed because subsidy cuts have concentrated losers and diffuse gainers.
  • Ecology as capital: groundwater depletion, soil erosion and pollution are losses of natural capital that GDP does not subtract; crop choice suited to agro-climatic zones and pricing of power/water for irrigation are key levers.
  • Infrastructure and inclusion: roads, power and connectivity lower transport and search costs, integrate rural labour into urban markets and raise productivity; social infrastructure lowers the cost of living and builds human capital.
  • DPI as a public good: open, interoperable rails (identity, payments, data) cut transaction costs for the poor and small firms; limits are the digital divide, authentication failures, cyber-fraud and privacy.
  • From access to usage: near-universal account ownership has shifted the policy focus to credit, insurance, pensions and literacy (usage and quality of inclusion).

30-Second Revision

Sustainable development (Brundtland 1987) = present development without harming future prospects; intergenerational equity is its fairness principle. Ecological threats: deforestation, soil erosion, groundwater depletion (60.63% extraction), pollution, extinction. Fiscal threats: weak revenue, populism, PSU losses, overruns → FD (4.3% BE 2026-27) → debt (55.6%) → interest (about 40% of revenue receipts). Fix: laws, afforestation, crop choice, emission norms, waste treatment; better taxes, targeting, consensus, PSU reform, project design; FRBM debt anchor 50 ± 1% by 2031. Subsidy test: What, Who, Quality, Financing, Sunset. Intragenerational equity via physical infrastructure (transport, power, communication cut search cost), social infrastructure (housing, water, sanitation, health, education) and DPI (Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, ONDC, ePathshala, eSanjeevani). Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2015 (GS Paper I) - PMJDY

'Pradhan Mantri Jan-Dhan Yojana' has been launched for

  1. providing housing loan to poor people at cheaper interest rates
  2. promoting women's Self-Help Groups in backward areas
  3. promoting financial inclusion in the country
  4. providing financial help to the marginalised communities

Answer: (c). PMJDY (28 August 2014) is the National Mission for Financial Inclusion - universal bank accounts as the base for DBT, credit, insurance and pension (Section 4.1).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2016 (GS Paper I) - MUDRA

Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana is aimed at

  1. bringing the small entrepreneurs into formal financial system
  2. providing loans to poor farmers for cultivating particular crops
  3. providing pensions to old and destitute persons
  4. funding the voluntary organizations involved in the promotion of skill development and employment generation

Answer: (a). MUDRA gives collateral-free credit to non-corporate, non-farm micro and small enterprises - a core tool of credit inclusion (Section 4.2).

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017 (GS Paper I) - UPI

Which of the following is a most likely consequence of implementing the 'Unified Payments Interface (UPI)'?

  1. Mobile wallets will not be necessary for online payments.
  2. Digital currency will totally replace the physical currency in about two decades.
  3. FDI inflows will drastically increase.
  4. Direct transfer of subsidies to poor people will become very effective.

Answer: (a). UPI allows direct bank-account-to-bank-account payments through any participating app, so a separate pre-loaded wallet is not needed. DBT runs on Aadhaar-seeded bank accounts (APB), not on UPI (DPI table).

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - Aadhaar

Consider the following statements:

  1. Aadhaar card can be used as a proof of citizenship or domicile.
  2. Once issued, Aadhaar number cannot be deactivated or omitted by the Issuing Authority.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (d). Section 9 of the Aadhaar Act, 2016: Aadhaar is not proof of citizenship or domicile. UIDAI can omit or deactivate an Aadhaar number under the Act and its regulations. Aadhaar is an identity layer of DPI, available to residents (Figure 2). (ForumIAS words statement 1 as "citizenship and domicile".)

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - FRBM and public debt

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Review Committee Report has recommended a debt to GDP ratio of 60% for the general (combined) government by 2023, comprising 40% for the Central Government and 20% for the State Governments.
  2. The Central Government has domestic liabilities of 21% of GDP as compared to that of 49% of GDP of the State Governments.
  3. As per the Constitution of India, it is mandatory for a State to take the Central Government's consent for raising any loan if the former owes any outstanding liabilities to the latter.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (c). Statement 1 is the N.K. Singh Committee recommendation; statement 3 is Article 293(3). Statement 2 is wrong - the Centre's liabilities are much larger than the States'. Debt ceilings are the core of fiscal sustainability (Section 1.3 B).

Q6. UPSC CSE Prelims 2022 (GS Paper I) - Open-source digital platforms

Consider the following:

  1. Aarogya Setu
  2. CoWIN
  3. DigiLocker
  4. DIKSHA

Which of the above are built on top of open-source digital platforms?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2, 3 and 4 only
  3. 1, 3 and 4 only
  4. 1, 2, 3 and 4

Answer: (d). All four are built on open-source/open-standard technology - the defining feature of Digital Public Infrastructure (open, interoperable, reusable) (Section 3.3).

Corrections Made

  1. Sustainable development vs intergenerational equity: goal vs principle; Brundtland definition; three pillars.
  2. Ecological challenges: "extension" of species = extinction; externalities and natural capital.
  3. Fiscal deficit and debt: flow vs stock; capex vs revenue borrowing; interest burden.
  4. Ecological policies: afforestation spelling; afforestation and anti-poaching are separate; untreated waste goes to water and land.
  5. Fiscal policies: "consensus"; FRBM Act and debt anchor added.
  6. Physical infrastructure: typos; wider scope; Harmonised Master List.
  7. Social infrastructure: includes education and health.
  8. DPI names: Aadhaar, ePathshala, eSanjeevani, DigiLocker function; "world's largest" claim qualified.
  9. Financial inclusion definition: access plus usage and quality, at affordable cost (Rangarajan 2008).
  10. Banking inclusion: deposit protection specified (DICGC Rs 5 lakh).
  11. Credit inclusion: incomplete point completed.
