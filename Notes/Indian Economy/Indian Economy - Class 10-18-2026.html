1. Circular Flow of Income: Roles of the Sectors

The circular flow of income is a model of how money, goods and services move between the economy's agents (households, firms, government, financial institutions and the rest of the world). It shows that the sectors depend on one another: one sector's spending is another sector's income.

The two-sector and four-sector diagrams, the leakages-and-injections table and the equilibrium condition S + T + M = I + G + X are in the 14 October notes (Section 4, Figures 4 and 5). This section adds the role of each sector from today's class.

[Correction] (1) The rough notes say "foreign markets"; the sector is the rest of the world (foreign sector), which trades with the domestic economy. (2) Transfer payments (pensions, subsidies, scholarships) are payments made without any current good or service received in return, so they are not counted in government spending G for GDP; only government purchases of goods and services and salaries of its employees are. In the model they are treated as negative taxes (net taxes = taxes less transfers). (3) Banks do not create a separate "income" stream; they are intermediaries that channel saving into investment. (4) "Businesses pay rent, interest, wages and profits": profit is what is left after the other factor payments, not a contractual payment.

[Additional] Because every rupee paid out as factor income returns as spending (or leaks and is injected back), national output = national income = national expenditure. This is the basis of the three methods of measuring GDP in Section 5.3.

2. Business Cycle

The business cycle is the recurring fluctuation of economic activity around its long-run growth trend, with alternating periods of expansion (growth) and contraction (recession) in aggregate output such as GDP, employment, income and sales.

[Additional] Business cycles are recurrent but not periodic: their length and depth vary from cycle to cycle, so the phases cannot be timed exactly. They are driven by changes in aggregate demand (consumption, investment, government spending, exports), supply shocks (oil, monsoon, pandemic), credit conditions and expectations.

[Correction] (1) The rough notes define contraction as a decline "for at least two consecutive quarters". That is the technical-recession rule of thumb (two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP), widely used in the UK and the euro area. It is not a universal official definition: the US National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) dates recessions by a broad, judgement-based test of the depth, spread and duration of the decline in activity. (2) Depression has no official definition; it is used for a recession that is both severe and prolonged (the Great Depression of the 1930s is the standard example). A common rule of thumb is a GDP fall of more than 10% or a downturn lasting several years. (3) In the rough notes the list of characteristics (declining GDP, rising unemployment and so on) is written under "Depression"; those are characteristics of contraction, and a depression is the extreme case. (4) Peak and trough are turning points, not periods. [Verification Required] India has no official body that dates business cycles; no authoritative Indian dating source was found.

Exam point: Recession = contraction phase (peak to trough). Recovery = the expansion from the trough back to the previous peak. Boom = the strong late stage of expansion near the peak. Do not confuse the level of GDP (falling in recession) with the growth rate (a slowdown is not yet a recession if GDP still grows).

2.2 Fiscal Stimulus

In a recession, private spending falls. The government can offset this by expansionary fiscal policy: cutting taxes and/or raising government expenditure. This is counter-cyclical policy (it works against the direction of the cycle).

Recession
(demand falls)→Government cuts taxes and/or raises spending→Disposable income and demand rise→Output and jobs recover→Cost: larger fiscal deficit and debt

[Correction] The rough notes say govt must lower taxes "by increasing the govt expenditure". These are two separate instruments; the position is lower taxes and/or higher spending. Fiscal stimulus is also different from monetary stimulus (RBI cutting policy rates or adding liquidity), although governments often use both together. Also, the package announced in 2020 is called the Atmanirbhar Bharat Abhiyan (Self-Reliant India Mission).

Example - COVID-19, India (2020): On 12 May 2020 the Government announced the Atmanirbhar Bharat package, described as about ₹20 lakh crore (about 10% of GDP). [Additional] Analysts (for example PRS Legislative Research) pointed out that this headline figure included much more than budgetary spending: RBI liquidity and credit measures, credit guarantees and loan-related support, and spending already budgeted. The direct additional fiscal cost was therefore much smaller than the headline. [Verification Required] The exact split and the net fiscal cost differ across analyses (a figure of roughly 1-2% of GDP is often quoted) and were not confirmed from a single official table.

[Additional] Trade-offs: stimulus raises the fiscal deficit and public debt, and can cause inflation or crowding out of private borrowing if used too long. Under the FRBM Act, deficit targets can be relaxed in specified emergencies (such as a national calamity or a sharp fall in output), which is how stimulus is reconciled with fiscal rules. Link: injections (G) and leakages (T) in the circular flow.

3. Shapes of Economic Recovery

The shape of a recovery describes the path of output (or GDP growth) after a downturn: how deep the fall, how long the bottom, and whether the old level or trend is regained. Letters are used as a memory aid; they are descriptive labels from commentary, not formal terms.

Exam point: V = fastest recovery; L = worst (no recovery); W = double-dip; K = unequal recovery (the concern is distribution, not speed). The fastest way to remember: the letter traces the line of output over time.

4. GDP, GNP, National Income and Per-Capita Income: Overview

All four measure the economy's output or income, but on different bases. GDP is about where production takes place (the economic territory); GNP (now called GNI) is about whose production it is (normal residents); national income is the net, factor-cost version of GNP; and per-capita income is national income divided by population.

GDPMP→+ NFIA→GNPMP→− depreciation→NNPMP→− net indirect taxes→NNPFC = National income

MP = market price; FC = factor cost; NFIA = net factor income from abroad. Detailed definitions and formulas are in Section 5.

5. GDP and GNP in Detail

5.1 Economic Territory

GDP is a territory-based concept ("where"). The economic territory is the geographical territory administered by a government within which persons, goods and capital circulate freely. It is wider than the political map.

What the economic territory includes and excludes
IncludedExcluded
Political frontiers, including territorial waters and airspaceEmbassies, consulates and military establishments of foreign governments located within the country's political frontiers
The country's own embassies, consulates and military bases located in other countriesInternational organisations located in the country (the UN and its agencies, IMF, World Bank and similar bodies)
Ships and aircraft operated by the country's residents between two or more countries
Fishing vessels, oil and natural-gas rigs and floating platforms operated by residents in international waters

[Correction] The rough notes read "embassies, consulates, military bases located abroad excluding those located political frontiers". The correct position: the country's own embassies, consulates and military bases abroad are included, while foreign embassies, consulates and bases located inside its borders are excluded (they belong to the economic territory of the foreign country). Likewise, international organisations are excluded.

[Additional] Normal resident: a person or institution whose centre of economic interest lies in the economic territory and who stays (or intends to stay) there for one year or more. Nationality and residence are different: a foreign citizen working in India for more than a year is a resident (and an Indian worker abroad for more than a year is a non-resident). Territory decides GDP; residence decides GNP.

5.2 Meaning of GDP

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic (economic) territory of a country in a given time period, usually a year or a quarter.

GDP = monetary (market) value + of final goods and services only + produced in a specified time period + within the economic territory
  • Monetary value: unlike physical quantities (tonnes of wheat, number of cars), money lets different goods be added.
  • Final goods and services only: intermediate goods are excluded to avoid double counting (see goods and services, 14 October).
  • Time period: GDP is a flow variable measured per period (a stock, such as the capital stock or wealth, is measured at a point in time; see stock and flow).
  • Domestic territory: all production within the territory counts regardless of whether it is done by a resident or a non-resident, local or foreign-owned.
  • Who calculates it in India: the National Statistics Office (NSO) under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) publishes quarterly and annual estimates (advance, provisional and revised estimates).

[Correction] (1) "GDP means where" should read: GDP is territory-based (where), whereas GNP is resident-based (whose). (2) The rough notes say production by "national residents / non-residents in a country gets included regardless of whether that production is done by local resident / foreign resident": the point is that production by non-residents inside the territory is included in GDP and production by residents outside the territory is not (it appears in GNP). (3) The statistic is the market value of final output; "monetary value" alone could be read as including intermediate goods. (4) Reporting period: India's financial year runs 1 April to 31 March (for example, 2025-26); quarterly figures use April-June, July-September, October-December and January-March.

[Correction] "GDP is a quantitative concept" needs qualification: GDP measures the quantity (value) of output, not its quality or the quality of life. It leaves out unpaid household work, much informal activity, environmental damage and the distribution of income. See GDP as a measure of welfare (29 September).

5.3 Numerical Formulas of GDP

1. Expenditure method: GDPMP = C + I + G + (X − M)
C = private final consumption expenditure; I = investment (gross fixed capital formation plus change in stocks); G = government final consumption expenditure (excluding transfer payments); X = exports; M = imports; (X − M) = net exports. In practice a statistical discrepancy is also reported.
2. Income method: GDPMP = Compensation of employees (wages and salaries) + Operating surplus (rent, interest, profit) + Mixed income of the self-employed + Depreciation (consumption of fixed capital) + Net indirect taxes (indirect taxes − subsidies)
The first three items together are the factor incomes; adding depreciation gives GDP at factor cost, and adding net indirect taxes gives GDP at market price.
3. Production (value-added) method: GVA = Value of output − Intermediate consumption; GDPMP = Σ GVA at basic prices + Product taxes − Product subsidies
GVA = Gross Value Added; the sum is taken over all sectors (agriculture, industry, services).
Real vs nominal: Real GDP = (Nominal GDP / GDP deflator) × 100; GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100; Growth rate (%) = [(Real GDPt / Real GDPt-1) − 1] × 100
Nominal GDP is measured at current-year prices; real GDP at constant base-year prices. The growth rate usually quoted is the real growth rate.

[Additional] All three methods should give the same value because of the circular-flow identity (Section 1): output = income = expenditure. In India the headline measures are GDP at market prices and GVA at basic prices; GDP at factor cost was dropped from headline reporting with the 2011-12 base series (2015), in line with the UN System of National Accounts. Textbook formulas that use "factor cost" remain useful for understanding.

[Additional] NFIA includes compensation of employees and property and entrepreneurial income (interest, dividends, profit). It is negative for a country that pays more factor income abroad than it receives, and then GNP < GDP; India's NFIA has generally been negative in the national accounts, so GNI is a little below GDP. Remittances are current transfers, not factor income, so they are not part of NFIA. International usage now prefers Gross National Income (GNI) to "GNP"; they mean the same thing.

5.6 Per-Capita Income

Per-capita income = National income / Population (mid-year population)
In MoSPI releases the measure used is per-capita Net National Income (NNI), reported at current prices (nominal) and at constant prices (real). Real per-capita income shows the change in living standards after removing inflation.
  • Average, not distribution: it hides inequality; a rise in the average can coexist with a fall for the poor.
  • Not the same as GDP per capita (GDP / population), which is used in international comparisons; GNI per capita is also used by the World Bank. [Verification Required] Check which price concept (market or basic) MoSPI uses for NNI in the latest release.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Circular flow: households supply factors and receive rent, wages, interest, profit; firms produce; government taxes and spends; banks channel saving to investment; foreign sector = exports and imports. Real flow is opposite to money flow.
  • Business cycle phases: expansion → peak → contraction (recession) → trough → recovery. Recession (technical) = two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP; depression = prolonged and severe recession (no official definition).
  • Fiscal stimulus = lower taxes and/or higher government expenditure in a recession; Atmanirbhar Bharat package = about ₹20 lakh crore announced 12 May 2020, but not all of it was budgetary spending.
  • Recovery shapes: V (quick), U (long bottom), W (double-dip), L (no recovery), K (uneven), Z (non-standard).
  • Economic territory includes the country's own embassies, consulates and military bases abroad, its residents' ships, aircraft, fishing vessels and rigs in international waters; excludes foreign embassies inside India and international organisations.
  • GDP = market value of final goods and services produced within the economic territory in a period; a flow; calculated quarterly and annually by the NSO (MoSPI).
  • GNP = GDP + NFIA; NNP = GNP − depreciation; National income = NNP at factor cost = NNPMP − net indirect taxes; per-capita income = national income / population.
  • GDP formulas: C + I + G + (X − M); sum of factor incomes + depreciation + net indirect taxes; Σ GVA + product taxes − product subsidies.
  • India's GDP base year is 2022-23 in the new series (replacing 2011-12) [Verification Required].

Mains Points

  • Counter-cyclical policy: expansionary fiscal and monetary policy in downturns, tightening near the peak; the trade-off is a higher fiscal deficit, debt and inflation, and the need for fiscal rules with escape clauses (FRBM).
  • Stimulus design: headline package size versus real fiscal cost; direct transfers and capital expenditure have different multipliers; K-shaped recovery argues for targeted support to informal workers and MSMEs.
  • Reading recoveries: aggregate GDP can show a V while distribution is K-shaped; track employment, consumption and credit to small firms, not only GDP.
  • GDP, GNP and welfare: GDP counts value of output within the territory, not distribution, quality, unpaid work or environmental cost; per-capita income is an average; use real, PPP and distribution measures (29 September).
  • Rebasing: a new base year improves coverage of the digital, gig and informal economy, but changes the level of GDP and therefore deficit and debt ratios, so series should not be mixed across base years.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2015 (GS Paper I) - Real GDP growth and GDP at market prices

With reference to Indian economy, consider the following statements:

  1. The rate of growth of Real Gross Domestic Product has steadily increased in the last decade.
  2. The Gross Domestic Product at market prices (in rupees) has steadily increased in the last decade.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (b) 2 only. The real growth rate fluctuates over the cycle (statement 1 is false), whereas GDP at market prices (nominal, in rupees) rises almost every year because of growth plus inflation (statement 2 is true). Links to Section 5.3 (real vs nominal) and the business cycle (Section 2).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021 (GS Paper I) - Policy in an economic recession

Which among the following steps is most likely to be taken at the time of an economic recession?

  1. Cut in tax rates accompanied by increase in interest rate
  2. Increase in expenditure on public projects
  3. Increase in tax rates accompanied by reduction of interest rate
  4. Reduction of expenditure on public projects

Answer: (b) Increase in expenditure on public projects. In a recession, the government uses expansionary fiscal policy (higher spending, lower taxes) to raise demand; raising interest rates or taxes, or cutting public spending, would deepen the downturn. Links to Section 2.2.
