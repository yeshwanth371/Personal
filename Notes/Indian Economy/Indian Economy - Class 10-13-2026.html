1. Monetary Policy and the RBI

1.1 Meaning, Institution and Frequency

Monetary policy deals with the cost of money (interest rates) and the availability of money and credit (liquidity and money supply) in the economy. It is run by the central bank; in India, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). The RBI's own preamble says its aim is "to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth".

[Correction] Monetary policy is not only "interest rates and money supply". It is the central bank's use of its policy rate, liquidity operations and reserve requirements to steer inflation and growth. In India the operating target is the overnight money-market rate (the weighted average call rate, WACR); money supply is an intermediate variable that responds with a lag. On 7 October 2026 the Governor said the RBI "will use an appropriate mix of liquidity management tools and strive to align the WACR with the policy repo rate". Monetary policy is different from fiscal policy (government taxes, spending and borrowing).

1.2 The RBI Board and the Governor

  • The Central Board of the RBI includes a Governor and up to four Deputy Governors, appointed by the Central Government.
  • The RBI Act does not prescribe educational or professional qualifications for the Governor; the Governor is appointed for a term fixed by the Government.
  • Current Governor: Shri Sanjay Malhotra (chair of the MPC, per the 7 October 2026 resolution).

[Correction] "RBI = 1 Governor + 4 Deputy Governors" should read "a Governor and not more than four Deputy Governors" (a ceiling, not a fixed number) on a larger Central Board that also has nominated and official directors. The Governor "draws his power from the RBI Act" and is appointed by the Central Government (see PYQ 2021 in the PYQ section); the Constitution does not itself give the Government the right to direct the RBI. [Verification Required] The Section 8 wording of the RBI Act, the absence of prescribed qualifications and the Governor's term are standard facts not re-opened from the Act text. The RBI Bulletin of September 2026 identifies Dr Poonam Gupta as a Deputy Governor; the other Deputy Governors were not verified.

1.3 Repo Rate and the Policy Corridor

Repo rate (class note): "the rate at which the RBI lends to banks". On 7 October 2026 the MPC raised the policy repo rate by 25 bp to 5.50%, and the SDF rate and the MSF rate / Bank Rate moved with it.

[Correction] "Repo rate = rate at which RBI lends to banks" is right in everyday terms; the precise position is: the policy repo rate is the rate on collateralised, short-term (overnight and term) lending by the RBI under the LAF, set by the MPC. It is the anchor for the whole corridor and the signal for bank lending and deposit rates. Do not confuse it with the reverse repo (the RBI borrows from banks; in practice used as variable-rate reverse repo, VRRR, auctions to absorb surplus liquidity: 55 VRRR auctions were held between the August and October 2026 policies) or with the MSF rate and Bank Rate (both 25 bp above repo). [Verification Required] The collateral-free nature of the SDF, the emergency role of the MSF and the "repo = repurchase agreement" explanation are standard facts not re-opened from RBI circulars.

1.4 Open Market Operations (OMO)

OMO = buying and selling of government securities (G-secs) by the RBI in the open market.

OMO and liquidity (class note, made precise)
CaseClass notePrecise statement
1. RBI buys G-secsBanks have money; expansionary policyThe RBI pays banks for the securities, so banks' reserves and system liquidity rise (durable liquidity injection). Government bond prices rise and yields fall. This eases conditions.
2. RBI sells G-secsBanks have less money; contractionary policyBanks pay the RBI, so reserves and liquidity fall (liquidity absorption). Bond prices fall and yields rise. This tightens conditions.

[Correction] OMO is a liquidity (quantity) tool; the policy repo rate is the price tool. OMO purchases or sales do not by themselves change the MPC's "stance". Today's data show the difference: in September 2026 the RBI announced OMO sale auctions of Rs 1 lakh crore in three tranches (17, 21 and 28 September) and ran VRRR auctions to mop up surplus liquidity that surged after the FCNR(B) swap window (average daily net absorption under the LAF: Rs 5.8 lakh crore in August and Rs 7.8 lakh crore in September, per the Governor's Statement). This was liquidity management to keep the WACR near the repo rate, before the 7 October rate hike. Another OMO sale auction was held on 13 October 2026 (result released by the RBI; amount not opened).

[Additional] Two sources give different September liquidity figures: the RBI Bulletin reports Rs 9.2 lakh crore (average daily net absorption under the LAF, 1-21 September) and the Governor's Statement reports Rs 7.8 lakh crore for September as a whole. The difference lies in the period covered (partial month vs full month) and possibly in what is counted; the exact definitional gap was not reconciled here. [Verification Required]

1.5 Raising and Cutting the Policy Rate

Repo rate raised→Banks' funding and lending rates tend to rise (partial, with lag)→Cost of borrowing rises; less borrowing and investment→Credit and money growth slow; demand cools→Inflation pressure falls (tight / hawkish)
Repo rate cut→Banks' lending rates tend to fall (partial, with lag)→Cost of borrowing falls; more borrowing and investment→Demand and growth rise (easy / dovish)→Inflation risk rises if overdone
Tight vs easy policy: vocabulary
DirectionEveryday and market terms (class note)PurposeExample
Raising the rateContractionary / tight / costly (dear money) / hawkishControl inflationMPC hike of 25 bp to 5.50%, 7 October 2026
Cutting the rateExpansionary / easy / cheap / loose / dovishSupport growthCumulative cut of 125 bp, February 2025 to July 2026

[Correction] Case 3 (rate hike) is right in direction, but add three precisions. (i) "Banks may increase their rates" is the transmission step, and it is partial and lagged (see Figure 2). (ii) "Less money supply" is better stated as slower growth of credit and money; monetary policy does not shrink the money stock in absolute terms in normal times. (iii) The terms hawkish and dovish are market shorthand. The RBI's formal vocabulary is the stance: accommodative, neutral, calibrated tightening or withdrawal of accommodation. The stance is separate from the rate decision. On 7 October 2026 the MPC voted unanimously for the 25 bp hike and, with two members (Dr Nagesh Kumar and Prof. Ram Singh) preferring to keep it neutral, changed the stance to "calibrated tightening", meaning "rate cuts are off the table in the near term and policy action ahead can only be a rate hike or a pause".

[Correction] Case 4 (rate cut): lower rates make borrowing cheaper, so investment and consumption can rise and support GDP growth. The chain is not automatic: it depends on pass-through to bank rates, credit demand and confidence, and an overdone cut raises inflation risk. Also, the MPC's primary mandate is price stability; growth is the secondary objective. The Governor noted on 7 October 2026 that monetary policy acts on supply-side inflation mainly by curbing second-round effects (inflation expectations, firm-level pricing), not by lowering oil or food prices directly.

Real interest rate (Fisher approximation)

Real interest rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Expected inflation

Both terms in per cent per annum. A rate that looks high in nominal terms can be low in real terms when inflation is high (nominal vs real; 11 October notes for inflation measures).

2. Banks, Balance Sheet, NPAs, Interest and Credit

2.1 Bank

A bank is a financial institution that accepts deposits from the public and gives loans, earning a profit on the gap between lending and deposit rates.

[Correction] The statutory meaning (Banking Regulation Act, 1949, Section 5(b)) is "the accepting, for the purpose of lending or investment, of deposits of money from the public, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order or otherwise". "Earning profits" describes commercial banks' business motive; co-operative banks also accept public deposits but have a different ownership model. A bank is not the same as an NBFC, which can lend but cannot accept demand deposits. [Verification Required] The Section 5(b) wording is a standard fact not re-opened from the Act; the NBFC point follows Prelims PYQs (for example 2010 and 2026) in the UnlockIAS compilation.

2.2 Balance Sheet

A balance sheet summarises the assets and liabilities of an individual or institution at a point in time, so it is a stock variable. Identity: Assets = Liabilities + Net worth. See the simplified bank balance sheet (11 October) and stock vs flow (25 September). For a bank, loans are assets and deposits are liabilities. The BoP (Section 3) is the opposite kind of statement: a flow over a period.

[Correction] The class definition is correct; complete it with the net worth term (assets minus liabilities, owners' equity). A stock is a level at one date, so it changes over time only through flows (11 October notes). For a bank, a rising NPA ratio erodes the asset side of the balance sheet and its net worth (Section 2.3).

2.3 NPA (Non-Performing Asset)

Class note: a loan or advance whose principal or interest payment remains overdue for 90 days or more; also called a bad asset.

[Correction] Under the RBI's prudential norms a loan becomes an NPA when interest and/or an instalment of principal remains overdue for more than 90 days, not "90 days or more"; the RBI's consolidated Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning (IRACP) Directions, 2025 (updated as on 1 October 2026) govern commercial banks. "Bad asset" is an informal synonym. NPAs are further classed as sub-standard, doubtful and loss assets, and banks must keep provisions against them. [Verification Required] The "more than 90 days" wording and the three sub-classes are standard facts: the RBI Directions page was opened but the PDF text was not retrievable, and special rules for agricultural loans are not covered here.

2.4 Interest Rate as the Cost of Capital

Interest rate = cost of capital for the borrower (the price of using someone else's money) and the return for the saver or lender.

[Correction] The class note "High interest rates, less investments. Less interest rates high borrowing rate" should read: High interest rates raise the cost of capital, so borrowing and investment fall; low interest rates cut the cost of capital, so borrowing and investment rise (other things equal). "High borrowing rate" would wrongly suggest a high rate of interest. The decision to borrow also depends on expected profit, confidence and credit availability (Prelims 2014 in the PYQ section). Distinguish the nominal rate from the real rate (formula).

[Correction] "Formal means banks and co-operatives, regulated by RBI" is the NCERT-style summary; the precise point is that formal lenders are under a legal supervisor, whereas informal lenders are not covered by RBI regulation (some States regulate moneylenders under their own State Moneylenders Acts). "Low interest rates" is a relative statement, not a rule: formal credit can be costly for risky borrowers (see the BBB-minus bond yield above 12% in Section 5.1), and informal rates vary. 3.1 Closed and Open Economy

Closed vs open economy

ExampleTheoretical benchmark; no major economy is fully closedIndia (a partially open economy: full capital-account convertibility has not been adopted)

[Correction] "Closed economy" is an analytical benchmark; real economies sit on a spectrum of openness. India's current account is largely convertible while the capital account is open only selectively (limits on foreign investment, external commercial borrowing and capital outflows). The openness of India's economy is visible in the figures below: net FDI inflows, FPI flows, remittances and trade all move the BoP. [Verification Required] The capital-account convertibility status is a standard fact not re-opened.

3.2 Meaning and Structure of the BoP

The balance of payments (BoP) records all economic transactions between residents of a country and non-residents during a period. It is a flow variable. The RBI compiles and publishes it.

[Correction] "BoP data expressed in dollars" is incomplete: the RBI publishes BoP in US dollars and also in rupees (Rs crore) (RBI Bulletin Tables 40 and 42 in US$ million and Table 43 in Rs crore). The RBI releases the BoP quarterly and, now, a preliminary monthly release (for example, "Developments in India's Balance of Payments for the Month of August 2026", 15 October 2026). The BoP is a record of transactions (credits and debits), not only of "money moving": exports of goods, services, income and transfers, and capital flows all appear. Do not confuse the BoP with the balance of trade, which covers goods only (see PYQ 2013 and 2014 in the PYQ section).

[Correction] "Exports: rupee will appreciate; imports: rupee will depreciate" holds only ceteris paribus. The exchange rate is set by the total supply of and demand for foreign exchange, which includes capital flows (FDI, FPI, NRI deposits), oil prices, global interest rates and RBI intervention. Current data show this: merchandise exports grew 26.1% y-o-y in August 2026 (the fastest growth since June 2022), yet the rupee depreciated for most of August because West Asia tensions and high crude prices raised import and dollar demand (RBI Bulletin, September 2026). "Appreciation" means the rupee buys more dollars (fewer rupees per dollar); "depreciation" means the opposite. Credits and debits record transactions, including non-cash items such as grants in kind.

3.5 Twin Deficit and the 2-3-4 Rule

Twin deficit = a high fiscal deficit together with a high current account deficit.

Why the two deficits are linked (national-income identity)

Current account balance = National saving − Investment, so CAD arises when Investment > National saving

National saving = Private saving + Government saving (= Taxes − Government spending)

CAD = (Investment − Private saving) + (Government spending − Taxes)

A larger fiscal deficit (government dissaving) raises CAD unless private saving rises or investment falls. The link is not automatic: 2026-27 BE fiscal deficit is 4.3% of GDP (12 October notes) while Q1 2026-27 CAD was only 0.5% of GDP.

[Correction] Twin deficit is a hypothesis (the "twin deficits hypothesis"), not a law; the identity above shows the possibility of a link, and empirical links vary. Class note: "Twin deficit = Fiscal deficit (high) + high Current account deficit" is correct as a definition.

(i) Only inflation has a notified target (4%, band 2%-6%); (ii) the fiscal-deficit 3% is the long-run FRBM benchmark for the Centre, which has not been met in recent years; (iii) the 2% CAD level is a rule of thumb used in commentary. 3.6 Trade Barriers

Trade barriers are government policies that discourage trade with other countries.

Tariff vs non-tariff barriers

5. Financial Markets and Financial-Sector Regulators

5.1 Financial Markets, Bonds and Shares

A financial market is a place (physical or electronic) where individuals, institutions and governments trade financial instruments: shares, bonds, commodities (crude, gold, silver) and currencies (dollars, euros).

[Correction] Strictly, shares and bonds are financial instruments (claims). Commodities and currencies are traded in the commodity (derivatives) market and the foreign exchange market, which are separate segments; in India the forex market is regulated by the RBI and commodity derivatives by SEBI. The main segments are the money market (short-term: call money, T-bills, commercial paper), the capital market (long-term: equity and bonds), the forex market and the derivatives market. [Verification Required] The segment-regulator mapping is a standard fact not re-opened from SEBI and RBI sources.

Three precisions: (i) the RBI is both central bank and regulator, and its regulatory powers also cover foreign exchange (under FEMA) and the government-securities market; (ii) SEBI took over commodity derivatives regulation from the Forward Markets Commission (merged in 2015) and regulates credit rating agencies (Prelims 2022 PYQ); (iii) the IBBI is a regulator of the insolvency profession and process, not the court that decides insolvency cases. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Monetary policy: cost and availability of money; RBI via the six-member MPC (Governor in the chair); six meetings a year by convention; 4% (+/- 2 pp) CPI target.
  • 100 bp = 1 pp. The RBI hiked the repo rate by 25 bp to 5.50% on 7 October 2026; SDF 5.25%; MSF and Bank Rate 5.75%; stance calibrated tightening; next meeting 2-4 December 2026.
  • Repo = RBI lends to banks against G-secs; reverse repo = RBI borrows; OMO = RBI buys or sells G-secs (buy = liquidity in; sell = liquidity out).
  • RBI: a Governor and not more than four Deputy Governors, appointed by the Central Government; powers from the RBI Act.
  • NPA: overdue more than 90 days; GNPA of scheduled commercial banks 1.67% (June 2026). Balance sheet = stock; BoP = flow.
  • BoP: all transactions between residents and non-residents; RBI publishes in US$ and Rs; current account = goods, services, primary and secondary income; capital and financial accounts = FDI, FPI, loans, reserves.
  • Trade deficit = goods only. CAD = current account only. Q1 2026-27 CAD: US$ 4.2 billion (0.5% of GDP). Twin deficit = high fiscal deficit + high CAD.
  • 2-3-4: CAD 2% | fiscal deficit 3% | CPI inflation 4% (mnemonic; only inflation has a notified target).
  • IMF (191 members) and World Bank: Washington DC; WTO (166 members, 1 January 1995): Geneva.
  • Regulators: RBI (banking), SEBI (securities, commodity derivatives, rating agencies), IRDAI (insurance), PFRDA (pensions), IBBI (insolvency).

Mains Points

  • Supply shocks and the MPC's dilemma: with crude near US$ 100+ per barrel, a deficient monsoon and El Nino, headline inflation is driven partly by supply; rates act on second-round effects, so the hike is a pre-emptive step while growth (7.8% in Q1) is strong. The 125 bp earlier easing and the 7 October hike show policy turning within a few months.
  • Transmission is partial: only about two-thirds to three-quarters of a repo change reaches fresh lending rates (Figure 2); deposit rates and lending rates moved in opposite directions in July-August 2026 amid a liquidity surge, so the central bank also relies on liquidity management (OMO, VRRR).
  • External vulnerability: a rising goods deficit (US$ 146.0 billion in April-August) is offset mainly by services and remittances; capital flows (FDI up, FPI out, FCNR(B) deposits surging) decide reserves. Tools: reserves, the FCNR(B) swap window, tariffs (gold) and trade agreements.
  • Fiscal-external-monetary links: the twin-deficit identity, a fiscal deficit of 4.3% of GDP, rising bond yields and the RBI's liquidity operations interact; monetary and fiscal policy must be coordinated (fiscal vs monetary table, 12 October).
  • Financial-sector architecture: five sectoral regulators plus the FSDC; issues of overlap, regulatory arbitrage and the need for coordination; asset quality has improved (GNPA 1.67%) but credit growth of 18.1% means vigilance.
  • Global institutions: reforming the WTO dispute system and the role of the IMF and World Bank for the Global South are recurring Mains themes (links to trade barriers and BoP support).

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 (GS Paper I) - Open market operations

In the context of Indian economy, 'Open Market Operations' refers to

  1. borrowing by scheduled banks from the RBI
  2. lending by commercial banks to industry and trade
  3. purchase and sale of government securities by the RBI
  4. None of the above

Answer: (c). OMO is the RBI's buying and selling of government securities to add or remove liquidity (Section 1.4, Correction 5).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020 (GS Paper I) - Expansionary monetary policy

If the RBI decides to adopt an expansionist monetary policy, which of the following would it not do?

  1. Cut and optimize the Statutory Liquidity Ratio
  2. Increase the Marginal Standing Facility Rate
  3. Cut the Bank Rate and Repo Rate

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (b). Cutting SLR, Bank Rate and repo rate eases policy; raising the MSF rate raises the ceiling of the corridor and is tightening, so 2 is what the RBI would not do (rates table, Section 1.5).

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014 (GS Paper I) - Interest rate and investment

If the interest rate is decreased in an economy, it will

  1. decrease the consumption expenditure in the economy
  2. increase the tax collection of the Government
  3. increase the investment expenditure in the economy
  4. increase the total savings in the economy

Answer: (c). A lower interest rate lowers the cost of capital and raises investment (Correction 12, Figure 3).

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021 (GS Paper I) - RBI Governor and the Government

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Governor of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is appointed by the Central Government.
  2. Certain provisions in the Constitution of India give the Central Government the right to issue directions to the RBI in public interest.
  3. The Governor of the RBI draws his power from the RBI Act.

Which of the above statements are correct?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (c). The Governor is appointed by the Central Government and derives powers from the RBI Act; the power to issue directions to the RBI in public interest is statutory (RBI Act), not a Constitutional provision, so statement 2 is wrong (Correction 4).

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014 (GS Paper I) - Current account

With reference to Balance of Payments, which of the following constitutes/constitute the Current Account?

  1. Balance of trade
  2. Foreign assets
  3. Balance of invisibles
  4. Special Drawing Rights

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3
  3. 1 and 3
  4. 1, 2 and 4

Answer: (c). The current account has the balance of trade (goods) and the balance of invisibles (services, income, transfers); foreign assets and SDRs belong to the financial/reserve side (BoP structure table).

Q6. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 (GS Paper I) - Capital account

Which of the following constitute Capital Account?

  1. Foreign Loans
  2. Foreign Direct Investment
  3. Private Remittances
  4. Portfolio Investment

Select the correct answer using the codes given below.

  1. 1, 2 and 3
  2. 1, 2 and 4
  3. 2, 3 and 4
  4. 1, 3 and 4

Answer: (b). Loans, FDI and portfolio investment are capital flows; private remittances are current transfers (secondary income) in the current account. In today's BoP presentation these flows appear in the financial account, but the exam uses the older "capital account" grouping (Correction 14).

Q7. UPSC CSE Prelims 2025 (GS Paper I) - Bonds and shares

Consider the following statements: Statement I: As regards returns from an investment in a company, generally, bondholders are considered to be relatively at lower risk than stockholders. Statement II: Bondholders are lenders to a company whereas stockholders are its owners. Statement III: For repayment purpose, bondholders are prioritized over stockholders by a company. Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

  1. Both Statement II and Statement III are correct and both of them explain Statement I
  2. Both Statement I and Statement II are correct and Statement I explains Statement II
  3. Only one of the Statements II and III is correct and that explains Statement I
  4. Neither Statement II nor Statement III is correct

Answer: (a). Bondholders are lenders with priority in repayment, which is why they carry lower risk than owners (bond vs share table).

Q8. UPSC CSE Prelims 2022 (GS Paper I) - Credit rating agencies

Consider the following statements: 1. In India, credit rating agencies are regulated by Reserve Bank of India. 2. The rating agency popularly known as ICRA is a public limited company. 3. Brickwork Ratings is an Indian credit rating agency. Which of the statements given above are correct?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (b). Credit rating agencies are regulated by SEBI, not the RBI, so statement 1 is wrong; statements 2 and 3 are correct (regulators table, Correction 24).

Corrections Made

  1. Monetary policy: not only interest rates and money supply; operating target is the call rate.
  2. "Every 2 months": six-meeting convention; MPC decides; at least four meetings required.
  3. Basis points: 100 bp = 1 percentage point; percent vs percentage point.
  4. RBI Governor and Deputy Governors: "not more than four"; appointed by the Centre; powers from the RBI Act.
  5. OMO: a liquidity tool, separate from the policy rate and stance.
  6. Rate hike (Case 3): partial, lagged transmission; "slower credit growth" not "less money"; stance vs hawkish/dovish.
  7. Rate cut (Case 4): not automatic; inflation is the primary mandate.
  8. Repo rate: collateralised short-term lending under the LAF; do not confuse with reverse repo, MSF and Bank Rate.
  9. Bank: statutory definition (Section 5(b)); NBFC vs bank.
  10. NPA: "more than 90 days"; sub-standard, doubtful, loss; current ratios.
  11. Balance sheet: net worth term added; stock changes only through flows.
  12. Interest rate: garbled sentence repaired; nominal vs real.
  13. Formal vs informal credit: supervision, not just "low rates".
  14. BoP: US$ and Rs; quarterly and monthly release; transactions, not only money.
  15. Closed vs open economy: benchmark; India partially open.
  16. Exports, imports and the rupee: ceteris paribus; August 2026 counter-example.
  17. Trade deficit: goods only; 2025-26 China and US figures; services surplus.
