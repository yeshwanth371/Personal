1. What Is an Economy?

An economy is the system through which people and institutions organise the production, distribution, exchange and consumption of goods and services, using available resources.

An economy can be studied at a household, local, regional, national or global level. Rules and institutions influence how it functions: property rights, contracts, markets, banks, firms and government are examples.

[Correction] The rough definition, "any place/region with defined rules and institutions to implement them", identifies an institutional setting but does not define an economy fully. Add the organisation of economic activities and resource allocation; an economy is not merely a geographical area with rules.

1.1 Household Economy

A household makes choices about income, spending, saving, borrowing, work and time. It may also produce goods or services. A household is an economic decision-making unit, not necessarily a single individual.

[Additional] Microeconomics studies individual decision makers and particular markets; macroeconomics studies aggregates such as national output, overall employment and inflation. Household decisions are usually examined through microeconomics.

2. Empirical Data and Its Context

Empirical means based on observation or measurement. Examples include household-survey responses, recorded prices, quantities produced and administrative tax records. A theoretical assumption or a made-up classroom example is not observed empirical data.

[Additional] A number becomes useful evidence only when its context is clear:

  • What: definition of the indicator, population covered and what is excluded.
  • Where and when: geographical coverage; reference period; fiscal year or calendar year; release date where relevant.
  • How much: unit and denominator, for example rupees per person, percentage of workers or tonnes per year.
  • How measured: survey or administrative source; sample/coverage limitations; estimates and later revisions.
  • Comparable basis: current or constant prices, base year where applicable, consistent definitions and methods.

Example: "Income rose by 10%" is incomplete without the period, income definition and price basis. [Additional] A nominal increase does not by itself establish a rise in purchasing power; correlation between two series does not by itself establish causation.

3. Economic Activities, Goods and Services

Economic activities concern producing, exchanging, distributing and using goods and services to satisfy wants with scarce resources. Monetary measurement is useful, but neither a cash payment nor easy measurement is a universal test of economic relevance.

[Correction] "All activities which can be measured and create value" is too broad as a definition and too narrow as an exclusion rule. Distinguish economic analysis from the specific production boundary used to compile GDP. Consumption is an economic activity, but it is not itself production.

[Correction] Production is not confined to turning raw materials into a final tangible product. Services are produced too, and intermediate goods are also outputs. GDP calculations must avoid double counting intermediate inputs and final output.

3.1 Goods versus Services

  • Goods: typically tangible products, such as food, clothing or machinery, over which ownership can be established.
  • Services: activities or outputs such as teaching, treatment and transport; they are typically intangible.

[Additional] "Tangible versus intangible" is a useful introductory distinction, not a complete classification of every digital product. "Commodity" often refers to a traded good; do not assume every use of the word has an identical scope.

4. Economic Value versus the GDP Production Boundary

Three different questions must be kept separate: Does an activity use resources and provide a service? Is it inside the national-accounts production boundary? Is it actually measured in published statistics?

4.1 Illegal Activities

[Correction] The statement "all illegal activities are non-economic" is not a valid general rule. Under the System of National Accounts (SNA), illegal production can fall inside the production boundary when it produces goods or services for which there is effective market demand. Legality and accounting inclusion are separate issues.

Illegality does not make a productive activity cease using labour, inputs or resources. However, theoretical inclusion does not prove complete measurement in India's published GDP. Do not confuse an illegal market transaction with theft: theft is not production or a mutually agreed exchange.

4.2 Services for Own Household Use

[Correction] A teacher teaching their own child, a doctor caring for their own parent and unpaid cooking, cleaning or care by a homemaker have value. Most such unpaid domestic and personal services produced and consumed within the same household are excluded from the SNA GDP production boundary, not economically worthless.

Valuation and measurement problems matter, but the exclusion is an accounting convention, not proof that the value is impossible to estimate. Paid provision of comparable services generally falls inside the boundary.

[Additional] Important exceptions to a blanket "own-use services are excluded" rule are imputed housing services of owner-occupied dwellings and domestic services produced by employing paid domestic staff. Unpaid household work can also be studied through time-use data and supplementary accounts without being included in headline GDP.

4.3 Goods for Own Use

The production of goods retained for a producer's own final consumption or own capital formation is within the SNA production boundary. A farmer's food kept for household consumption and own-account construction are examples.

[Correction] The rough note's own-consumption goods point is broadly right. Its value need not be an actual cash sale: comparable market prices can be used, with suitable cost-based valuation where necessary. Measurement coverage in practice should not be confused with the conceptual boundary.

5. Economics: Scarcity, Choice and Alternatives

Economics studies how people and societies make choices when resources are scarce and can be put to alternative uses. Human wants tend to exceed the means available to satisfy them.

Scarcity is the insufficiency of available resources relative to the wants they could satisfy. It is a relative concept, not merely a small physical quantity or a problem limited to poor economies.

Many competing wants+Scarce resources
with alternative uses→Choice→Opportunity cost

[Additional] Scarcity is not the same as a shortage. A shortage in a market occurs when quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the prevailing price. Scarcity is the broader resource constraint and can exist without a market shortage.

[Correction] "For what to produce" should be "For whom to produce". This concerns the distribution of output among people. Income from productive resources affects purchasing power, but taxes, transfers, wealth and public provision also influence access; it is not determined only by functional income distribution.

[Additional] A more labour-intensive method uses relatively more labour; a more capital-intensive method uses relatively more produced capital. Neither means the complete absence of the other input, and neither is automatically the best choice in every setting.

Money can finance machinery, but money itself is not the physical machine. Personal distribution asks how income is shared among people or households, who may receive several types of factor income.

8. Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best feasible alternative forgone when a choice is made.

[Correction] Do not define it as a sacrifice made only "to attain the best alternative". Any choice can have an opportunity cost, including a poor choice. The relevant alternative is the best among the unchosen feasible alternatives, not the sum of every rejected option.

  • It may be measured in money, time, output or another relevant benefit.
  • A zero money price does not establish zero opportunity cost.
  • It is a foregone alternative, not necessarily a payment to another person.

[Additional] Hypothetical example: During a two-hour period, a student can either revise economics, take a tutoring assignment paying Rs. 600, or do another activity they value less than tutoring. If revision is chosen and tutoring is the next-best feasible alternative, the opportunity cost is the foregone tutoring benefit of Rs. 600, not the sum of all rejected alternatives. The ranking depends on the decision maker's circumstances.

8.1 Free Government Schooling: Who Bears the Cost?

A government school can be free at the point of use while still using teachers' time, buildings, books and funds. Those resources have alternative uses, so the service is not costless to society.

Class question: "Opportunity cost of free government service is transferred from:"

  1. Taxpayer to beneficiary
  2. Beneficiary to government
  3. Beneficiary to taxpayer
  4. None

Intended classroom answer: (c), beneficiary to taxpayer. Under the simplified assumption of tax-financed provision, the beneficiary no longer pays the tuition fee directly; taxpayers collectively finance it and forgo alternative uses of those funds.

[Correction] The original explanation is a useful financing-burden shorthand, not a universal transfer theorem. A beneficiary may also be a taxpayer and may still incur travel, time or foregone-earnings costs. Government forgoes other spending choices; financing may also include borrowing or non-tax revenue. The underlying social opportunity cost remains the next-best use of scarce resources.

Do not equate "free government service" with a free good in the scarcity sense. Also, government provision alone does not make every service a pure public good; that is a separate classification.

9. Production Possibility Curve / Frontier (PPC / PPF)

A PPC shows the maximum feasible combinations of two outputs that an economy can produce during a specified period, with given resources and technology, when resources are used fully and efficiently.

[Correction] "All possible combinations" describes the production possibility set, including interior points. The frontier is the boundary: producing more of one output requires giving up some of the other. Merely keeping resources busy is not enough; they must be used efficiently.

9.1 Assumptions of the Basic Two-Good Model

  • Two goods, or two broad output categories, are used to make the trade-off visible.
  • The quantity and quality of resources and the state of technology are fixed for the frontier being drawn.
  • Outputs refer to the same period.
  • Points on the frontier assume full and efficient use of available resources.
  • [Additional] Increasing marginal opportunity cost additionally requires resources not to be equally adaptable between the two uses. That is not a universal law of every possible PPC.

10. Marginal Opportunity Cost (MOC)

MOC of X in terms of Y is the amount of Y that must be sacrificed for one additional unit of X along the frontier. It is also called the marginal rate of transformation (MRT) in this context.

MOC of X = quantity of Y forgone / extra quantity of X
For an interval with increasing X: MOCX = -ΔY / ΔX.
Here ΔX = new X - old X > 0, and ΔY = new Y - old Y < 0. The minus sign gives the sacrifice as a positive magnitude.

With machines on the horizontal axis and cloth on the vertical axis, MOC is measured in thousand metres of cloth per machine. The PPC slope, ΔY/ΔX, is negative; MOC is its positive magnitude.

10.1 Worked Calculations

  • A to B: (15 - 14) / (1 - 0) = 1 thousand metres per extra machine.
  • C to D: (12 - 9) / (3 - 2) = 3 thousand metres per extra machine.
  • E to F: (5 - 0) / (5 - 4) = 5 thousand metres per extra machine.
  • C to E: two extra machines require 12 - 5 = 7 thousand metres. Average opportunity cost over this interval is 7 / 2 = 3.5 thousand metres per machine; the successive one-machine costs are 3 and 4.

[Additional] For a smooth frontier, MOC at a point is the magnitude of the tangent slope, -dY/dX. A two-point calculation is the interval's average trade-off; with a discrete one-unit step it is the incremental cost of that unit. Distinguish a marginal cost, a multi-unit total sacrifice and an average sacrifice.

10.2 Why Does MOC Usually Increase?

Resources differ in their suitability for different uses. As production shifts from cloth to machines, the economy initially moves resources relatively well suited to machine production. Further expansion draws in resources better suited to cloth, so progressively more cloth must be forgone per additional machine.

Direction matters: moving rightward along this PPC, MOC of machines rises from 1 to 5 thousand metres per machine and the frontier becomes steeper. Reversing the same C-D interval gives a cost of 1/3 machine per extra thousand metres of cloth. Do not carry the old units or axis order into the reversed calculation.

Opportunity cost patternPPC shapeMeaning

[Additional] The PPC is not a demand curve. Its negative slope describes a production trade-off, not a relationship between price and quantity demanded. MOC is not necessarily a monetary marginal cost of production.

Common trap: unemployment or idle factories can move actual output inside an unchanged PPC. They do not automatically shift the frontier inward. Conversely, higher actual output can reflect better utilisation without any outward shift in capacity.

[Additional] Present versus future trade-off: producing more capital goods today may reduce current consumption but help expand future productive capacity. The eventual effect depends on investment quality, depreciation, skills, technology and use of the new capital; growth is not automatic.
