1. Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

A PPC (also Production Possibility Frontier, PPF, or transformation curve) shows the maximum combinations of two goods that an economy can produce during a given period when all its resources are used fully and efficiently, with a given state of technology.

[Correction] The rough note says "all combinations... after using all its resources". The PPC shows the maximum (efficient) combinations only; combinations inside the curve are also possible but are not on the PPC. The basic model uses two goods (or two groups of goods) so the trade-off can be drawn; "two / more" applies only to the general idea.

Assumptions: resources are fixed in quantity and quality; technology is given; resources are fully and efficiently employed; resources are not equally efficient in producing both goods (this gives increasing MOC).

[Additional] Distinguish three measures: total opportunity cost of 4 clothes = 100 food; average opportunity cost = 100 / 4 = 25 food per cloth; marginal opportunity cost of the 4th cloth = 40 food. Exam questions often test which one is being asked.

2. Marginal Opportunity Cost (MOC)

MOC is the amount of one good that must be sacrificed to produce one additional unit of another good. Along a PPC it is also called the marginal rate of transformation (MRT).

MOC of clothes = Food sacrificed / Extra clothes produced = -ΔFood / ΔClothes
ΔFood is negative when clothes increase, so the minus sign gives a positive sacrifice. Unit: units of food per unit of cloth.
MOC = slope of the PPC (in absolute value).

[Additional] MOC is measured in physical units of the forgone good, not in rupees. The rough-note phrase "extra units" should be read as "one extra unit" for a marginal measure; a sacrifice over several units is a total or average measure.

Exam point: the normal PPC is concave to the origin because MOC increases - resources are specialised and not equally efficient in producing both goods. As more clothes are produced, resources better suited to food are shifted, so more food is lost per extra cloth.

4. Features of the PPC

  1. Downward sloping: resources are limited, so producing more of one good requires sacrificing some of the other.
  2. Concave to the origin due to increasing MOC. Decreasing MOC gives a convex PPC; constant MOC gives a straight line.
  3. Points on, inside and outside:
    • On the PPC (A-E): full and efficient utilisation of resources.
    • Inside (U): under-utilisation or inefficient use - for example, unemployment or idle factories.
    • [Additional] Outside (Z): unattainable with existing resources and technology.
  4. Outward shift: an increase in resources (labour force, capital stock, new mineral discoveries) or improvement in technology increases productive capacity and shifts the PPC outward (away from the origin). This represents economic growth.
  5. Inward shift: a decrease in resources or deterioration of technology decreases productive capacity and shifts the PPC inward (towards the origin).

[Correction] "Shift PPC upwards to the right" is better stated as an outward shift (rightward and upward, away from the origin); similarly an inward shift is towards the origin. [Additional] A shift need not be parallel: technology that improves only cloth production pivots the PPC outward along the clothes axis only.

[Correction] "War, pandemic" as examples: war that destroys factories, infrastructure and lives reduces resources and shifts the PPC inward. A pandemic lockdown that temporarily idles workers and machines mainly moves actual output to a point inside the existing PPC; the PPC itself shifts inward only if productive capacity is lost (deaths, lasting health damage, permanent closure of firms). Similarly, unemployment is a point inside, not an inward shift.

5. Theory of Demand

Demand is the quantity of a commodity that consumers are willing and able to purchase at a given price during a given period of time.

[Correction] Demand is a flow, so it is stated "per period" (per day, week or month), not at "a given point of time". Desire alone is not demand: it needs desire + ability to pay + willingness to pay (effective demand). In the rough schedule, "100 id the purchase power" is read as quantity demanded of 100 units at Rs 10; quantity demanded is not the same as purchasing power.

5.1 Law of Demand

According to the law of demand, other things remaining constant (ceteris paribus), when the price of a commodity rises, its quantity demanded falls, and when its price falls, quantity demanded rises. Price and quantity demanded are inversely related.

[Additional] Why the demand curve slopes downward: (i) substitution effect - the good becomes cheaper relative to substitutes; (ii) income effect - a lower price raises real income (purchasing power); (iii) diminishing marginal utility - consumers buy additional units only at lower prices; (iv) new buyers enter the market at lower prices.

5.2 Movement along vs Shift of the Demand Curve

[Correction] A rise in quantity due to a fall in the good's own price is expansion (extension) of quantity demanded, and a fall due to a rise in price is contraction of quantity demanded. Both are movements along the same demand curve. When a non-price factor (income, related-good prices, tastes) changes, the whole curve shifts: this is an increase (rightward shift) or decrease (leftward shift) in demand.

5.3 Other Factors Affecting Demand

The good's own price is the first factor (it causes movement along the curve). The other factors shift the curve:

  1. Income of the consumer
    • Normal goods: demand rises when income rises and falls when income falls (positive income relationship).
    • Inferior goods: demand falls when income rises and rises when income falls (negative income relationship). As income rises, consumers switch to preferred alternatives.

    [Additional] "Inferior" describes the response to income, not necessarily poor physical quality. The same good can be normal for a low-income consumer and inferior for a higher-income consumer (NCERT).

  2. Prices of related goods
    • Substitute goods can be used in place of one another (for example, tea and coffee). If the price of a substitute rises, demand for the given good rises; if it falls, demand falls (direct relationship).
    • Complementary goods are used together to satisfy a want (for example, tea and sugar; car and petrol). If the price of a complement rises, demand for the given good falls; if it falls, demand rises (inverse relationship).

    [Correction] The correct term is complementary goods (goods that complete each other), not "complimentary" (free of charge). This is a frequent spelling-and-meaning confusion.

  3. Tastes and preferences: a favourable change (fashion, advertising, health awareness) increases demand; an unfavourable change decreases it.
  4. [Additional] Other factors: expectations about future prices or income; size and composition of population (number of buyers); distribution of income; availability of credit; season and weather; government policy such as taxes on consumers.

5.4 Exceptions to the Law of Demand

  1. Giffen goods: a special type of inferior good whose quantity demanded rises when its price rises and falls when its price falls, so the demand curve slopes upward over that range.

    [Correction] Giffen goods are not defined as "very poor-quality" goods. The defining condition is economic: the good is inferior, and its negative income effect is strong enough to outweigh the substitution effect. This typically needs the good to be a staple taking a large share of a poor consumer's budget: when its price rises, the consumer can no longer afford better food and buys even more of the staple. Every Giffen good is inferior, but most inferior goods are not Giffen goods.

  2. Veblen goods: status or prestige goods bought for conspicuous consumption (show-off). A higher price increases their appeal as a status symbol, so quantity demanded may rise with price. Examples often cited: designer jewellery, luxury watches.

    [Correction] Not every luxury good is a Veblen good. Most luxuries still follow the law of demand; a Veblen good is one where the high price itself is the source of desirability. The term comes from Thorstein Veblen's The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899).

  3. [Additional] Other commonly listed exceptions: expectation of a further price rise (speculative buying); goods judged by price as a signal of quality (ignorance effect); emergencies such as war or natural disasters.

Common trap: buying more when prices rise because further rises are expected reflects a change in expectations - a non-price factor that shifts demand - not a true upward-sloping demand curve. Similarly, necessities (salt, life-saving medicines) whose quantity changes little with price have inelastic demand; that is not an exception to the law of demand.

6. Theory of Supply

Supply is the quantity of a commodity that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at a given price during a given period of time.

[Correction] Add "able" and "during a given period": supply is a flow per period, not "at a point of time". [Additional] Supply is not the same as stock: stock is the total quantity available with producers at a point of time; supply is the part of it actually offered for sale at a given price.

6.1 Law of Supply

According to the law of supply, other things remaining constant, when the price of a commodity rises, quantity supplied rises, and when the price falls, quantity supplied falls. Price and quantity supplied are directly (positively) related.

[Correction] "Supply curve: it is upward curve" should read: the supply curve is upward sloping from left to right (positive slope). It may be drawn as a straight line or a curve.

[Additional] Why the supply curve slopes upward: a higher price makes production more profitable and covers the rising marginal cost of producing extra units; existing firms produce more and new producers may enter.

6.2 Other Factors Affecting Supply

  1. Technology: improved technology lowers cost per unit, so supply increases; outdated technology reduces supply.
  2. Cost of raw materials (input prices): if input costs rise, supply falls; if they fall, supply rises.
  3. Taxes and subsidies on a product: a subsidy to producers lowers their effective cost, so supply increases (curve shifts right). A per-unit tax (for example, excise duty) raises the effective cost, so supply decreases (curve shifts left).

    [Correction] The rough note on subsidy and tax was incomplete ("if subsidy is"). It is completed above following the standard NCERT treatment of a unit tax: a tax shifts supply left, and a subsidy works in the opposite direction.

  4. [Additional] Other factors: prices of other goods the firm could produce with the same resources; number of firms in the market; expectations of future prices; natural factors such as weather (especially for agriculture); goals of the firm.

[Additional] As with demand: a change in the good's own price causes expansion / contraction of quantity supplied (movement along S); a change in any other factor causes increase / decrease in supply (shift of S).

7. Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium occurs at the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. This price is the equilibrium (market-clearing) price, and the quantity bought and sold is the equilibrium quantity. Graphically, it is the point where the demand and supply curves intersect.

[Correction] "Quality demanded" should be quantity demanded. [Additional] The standard model assumes a perfectly competitive market with flexible prices; the market reaches equilibrium through price adjustment, not by a decision of any single buyer or seller.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • PPC = maximum combinations of two goods with given resources and technology, fully and efficiently used. Also called PPF or transformation curve.
  • Slope of PPC = MOC = MRT. Normal PPC is concave to the origin (increasing MOC); straight line = constant MOC; convex = decreasing MOC.
  • Point inside PPC = unemployment / under-utilisation; outside = unattainable; outward shift = growth of resources or technology.
  • Law of demand: price and quantity demanded are inversely related, ceteris paribus. Law of supply: directly related.
  • Own-price change = movement (expansion / contraction). Non-price change = shift (increase / decrease).
  • Substitutes: price of Y up, demand for X up. Complements: price of Y up, demand for X down.
  • Normal good: income up, demand up. Inferior good: income up, demand down.
  • Giffen good = inferior good where negative income effect > substitution effect; upward-sloping demand. Veblen good = status good; high price itself adds appeal.
  • Per-unit tax shifts supply left; subsidy shifts supply right.
  • Equilibrium: Qd = Qs. Below P* = excess demand (shortage); above P* = excess supply (surplus).

Mains Points

  • PPC and policy choice: every allocation (defence vs welfare, consumption vs capital goods, food crops vs cash crops) involves an opportunity cost. Choosing more capital goods today can shift the PPC outward in future.
  • Growth vs utilisation: unemployment and idle capacity place an economy inside its PPC. Policies that improve utilisation raise output without new resources; policies on education, infrastructure and R&D shift the PPC outward.
  • Price signals: demand-supply analysis explains price spikes in perishable goods after supply shocks (weather, transport disruption) and why buffer stocks or imports are used to shift supply.
  • Taxes and subsidies: indirect taxes shift supply left, tending to raise prices; producer subsidies shift supply right. The final incidence on buyers and sellers depends on elasticities.
  • Limits of the model: Giffen and Veblen goods, expectations and imperfect information show that the law of demand rests on the ceteris paribus assumption.

30-Second Revision

PPC = maximum output combinations; slope = MOC; concave (rising MOC), straight (constant), convex (falling). On = efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable; growth shifts it out, destruction shifts it in. Demand: inverse price-quantity relation; own price moves along, income / related prices / tastes shift the curve; Giffen and Veblen are exceptions. Supply: direct relation; technology, input costs, taxes and subsidies shift it. Equilibrium where Qd = Qs; class example Rs 20 and 70 units.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021 (GS Paper I) - Determinants of demand

Consider the following statements:
Other things remaining unchanged, market demand for a good might increase if

  1. Price of its substitute increase
  2. Price of its complement increase
  3. The good is an inferior good and income of the consumers increases
  4. Its price falls

Which of the above statements are correct?

  1. 1 and 4 only
  2. 2, 3 and 4 only
  3. 1, 3 and 4 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3 only

Answer: (a) 1 and 4 only. A dearer substitute shifts demand for the good right (1 correct). A dearer complement shifts it left (2 wrong). For an inferior good, rising income reduces demand (3 wrong). A fall in own price raises quantity demanded along the curve (4 accepted; strictly this is an expansion of quantity demanded, see Section 5.2).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - Opportunity cost

If a commodity is provided free to the public by the Government, then

  1. the opportunity cost is zero.
  2. the opportunity cost is ignored.
  3. the opportunity cost is transferred from the consumers of the product to the tax-paying public.
  4. the opportunity cost is transferred from the consumers of the product to the Government.

Answer: (c). Resources used to provide the good still have alternative uses, so opportunity cost is not zero. It is financed by taxpayers rather than paid by consumers. This builds on the 22 September class on opportunity cost and the PPC idea that every output has a cost in forgone alternatives.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020 (GS Paper I) - Price determination

Which of the following factors/policies were affecting the price of rice in India in the recent past?

  1. Minimum Support Price
  2. Government's trading
  3. Government's stockpiling
  4. Consumer subsidies

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1, 2 and 4 only
  2. 1, 3 and 4 only
  3. 2 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2, 3 and 4

Answer: (d) 1, 2, 3 and 4. Market price comes from demand and supply. MSP and procurement/stockpiling reduce the supply available in the open market and put a floor under farm prices. Government sales (for example, open-market sales) add to supply. Consumer subsidies (public distribution) affect effective demand in the open market.

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 (GS Paper I) - Equilibrium applied to the money market

Supply of money remaining the same when there is an increase in demand for money, there will be

  1. a fall in the level of prices
  2. an increase in the rate of interest
  3. a decrease in the rate of interest
  4. an increase in the level of income and employment

Answer: (b). Same logic as market equilibrium: with supply fixed, a rightward shift in demand raises the equilibrium "price". In the money market that price is the rate of interest. (An application of the idea; the question itself is a macroeconomics question.)
