1. Economic Systems: Capitalism and Socialism

Every economy must solve the central problems: what to produce, how to produce and for whom to produce (NCERT, Introductory Microeconomics). Economic systems differ in who solves these problems - the market (capitalism), the State (socialism) or both (mixed economy).

1.1 Capitalism (Market Economy)

  1. All central problems are solved by market forces of demand and supply through the price mechanism.
  2. Private property dominates, and inheritance is allowed.
  3. The role of the government in the economy is restricted (laissez-faire).

[Additional] Other features: profit motive, consumer sovereignty and competition. Intellectual roots: Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) and the "invisible hand" - individuals pursuing self-interest are led, through prices, to an efficient use of resources. Even under capitalism the State provides defence, law and order and enforcement of contracts.

1.2 Socialism (Centrally Planned Economy)

  1. Central problems are solved through a planning mechanism by a central planning authority appointed by the government.
  2. Private property is restricted and inheritance is not allowed.
  3. The government controls all economic resources to ensure proper (equitable) allocation of resources.

[Correction] "Planning commission" is India's name for its planning body; in general terms it is a central planning authority (for example, Gosplan in the USSR). In classical socialism what is abolished is private ownership of the means of production (land, factories, capital) and their inheritance; personal property (house, household goods, savings) was generally allowed in socialist states, so "private property is restricted" is accurate but "inheritance not allowed" applies mainly to productive assets. "Laisoez-Faire" = laissez-faire; "contols" = controls.

After the 1991 reforms (LPG), India moved towards a more market-oriented mixed economy.

2. Inclusive Growth in a Market Economy

A market economy means that all decisions regarding production, consumption and distribution are taken through the market forces of demand and supply. Markets are efficient at generating growth but do not automatically distribute its gains fairly (see the 1 October model answer). A market economy can have inclusive growth under the following conditions:

2.1 Environmental Protection from Excessive Exploitation (E)

Markets treat clean air, water and forests as free goods, so firms tend to over-exploit them. The State must protect the environment from excessive exploitation.

[Additional] Environmental damage is a negative externality - a cost borne by third parties (often the poor, who depend most on natural resources) and not by the producer. Tools: regulation (Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; Environmental Impact Assessment), the polluter pays principle, pollution taxes and tradable permits. For listed companies, SEBI requires the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) - mandatory for the top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation from FY 2022-23 - which discloses ESG performance. The current phase-in of third-party assurance of "BRSR Core" indicators is [Verification Required].

2.2 Corporate Social Responsibility (S)

CSR implies that corporates in an economy should also take steps to support social upgradation and to promote social upliftment. It was made mandatory in India by Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013.

[Correction] CSR was not "introduced" in 2013 - voluntary frameworks existed earlier (MCA CSR Voluntary Guidelines, 2009; National Voluntary Guidelines on Social, Environmental and Economic Responsibilities of Business, 2011). The 2013 Act made CSR mandatory for qualifying companies; Section 135 came into force on 1 April 2014 (with the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014). India is often described as the first country to mandate CSR spending by law (National CSR Portal: "one of the world's" first) - use "among the first" in answers. Spellings: "coporates" = corporates; "it's" = its.

Who must spend on CSR

Under the Companies Act, registered companies with a turnover of Rs 1,000 crore, a net worth of Rs 500 crore and a profit of Rs 5 crore are required to contribute 2% of their profit for CSR activities.

[Correction] The three thresholds are alternatives ("or"), not cumulative ("and"). Section 135(1): every company (including a foreign company's Indian branch or project office) having net worth of Rs 500 crore or more, OR turnover of Rs 1,000 crore or more, OR net profit of Rs 5 crore or more during the immediately preceding financial year is covered. Meeting any one is enough. The Rs 5 crore profit test applies to the immediately preceding financial year, not to a three-year average; the three-year average is used only to calculate the amount (below). "Net worth" = paid-up share capital + reserves (broadly, total assets minus total outside liabilities), as defined in Section 2(57).

CSR obligation (Section 135(5))

Minimum CSR spend in year t = 2% × (NPt-1 + NPt-2 + NPt-3) / 3

where NP = net profit of the company computed under Section 198 (profit before income tax, excluding profits of overseas branches and certain dividends) in each of the three immediately preceding financial years. A company less than three years old uses the years it has completed.

Worked example: net profit Rs 90 crore, Rs 100 crore and Rs 110 crore in the last three years → average Rs 100 crore → minimum CSR spend = Rs 2 crore in the current year (a flow, nominal rupees).

[Correction] The 2% is of the average net profit (Section 198) of the three immediately preceding years - a pre-tax measure - not "profit after tax". [Additional] If a company spends more than 2%, the excess can be set off against the requirement of up to three succeeding financial years (Section 135(5) proviso read with the CSR Rules). If the CSR obligation exceeds Rs 50 lakh, a CSR Committee of the Board (at least three directors, at least one independent) must be formed; otherwise the Board itself performs its functions (Section 135(1), (9)). Companies with an average CSR obligation of Rs 10 crore or more must get an independent impact assessment of projects of Rs 1 crore or more (Rule 8; PIB, 10 February 2026).

Permitted CSR activities (Schedule VII)

The government has identified activities that count as CSR, for example promoting education, supporting healthcare, protecting ecology, supporting gender upliftment, promoting cleanliness, developing sports facilities, etc.

[Correction] The list is Schedule VII of the Companies Act and currently has 13 items, (i) to (xiii), not 11. It has been amended several times (for example, slum-area development, disaster management, PM CARES Fund and, most recently, Social Stock Exchange instruments were added). The activities must be undertaken in project mode in India, and the company should give preference to the local area where it operates (Section 135(5) proviso).

How CSR may be implemented

If a firm is unable to fulfil its CSR obligations on its own, it can hire an NGO, or it can contribute the amount to the PM relief fund.

[Correction] A company may implement CSR itself or through an implementing agency - a Section 8 company, registered public trust or registered society that is registered under Sections 12A and 80G of income-tax law (or a government-established entity). Since 1 April 2021 every implementing agency must register with the Central Government by filing Form CSR-1, and private agencies need a three-year track record in similar activities (Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2021). "Get certified" therefore means CSR-1 registration of the agency; in addition, the company's CFO certifies that funds were used as approved. Contributing to the PMNRF or PM CARES Fund is itself a permitted activity (item (viii)) - not merely a fallback.

If the company does not spend

If a firm is unable to meet its CSR obligations for 3 consecutive years, it has to provide an answer to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs.

[Correction] This describes the old "comply or explain" regime (2014-2020), under which the Board only had to give reasons for not spending in its report - every year, not after three years. Since the Companies (Amendment) Acts of 2019 and 2020 and the CSR Amendment Rules of 22 January 2021, unspent CSR money must be transferred: (a) amounts for an ongoing project go to an Unspent CSR Account in a scheduled bank within 30 days of the year-end and must be spent within three financial years, after which any balance goes to a Schedule VII fund (such as PM CARES) within 30 days; (b) other unspent amounts go to a Schedule VII fund within six months of the year-end. Default attracts a civil penalty (Section 135(7)): the company pays twice the amount required to be transferred or Rs 1 crore, whichever is less, and each officer in default pays one-tenth of that amount or Rs 2 lakh, whichever is less. The Board must still explain the shortfall in its report.

2.3 Corporate Governance (G)

Corporate governance implies setting rules for ethical decision-making by the Board of Directors. It also requires that the Board take decisions not only for the benefit of large shareholders and promoters but also for the benefit of small shareholders, creditors, workers and other stakeholders in the company. It was strengthened by the Companies Act, 2013.

[Correction] Corporate governance norms in India predate 2013: CII's voluntary code (1998); SEBI's Kumar Mangalam Birla Committee (1999) led to Clause 49 of the Listing Agreement (2000); the N. R. Narayana Murthy Committee (2003) tightened it. The 2013 Act (passed after scandals such as Satyam, 2009) gave these norms statutory force for companies generally; listed companies additionally follow SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015, revised after the Uday Kotak Committee (2017). Spelling: "fro" = for.

2.5 Promoting Competition in the Market

The government should take steps to improve ease of doing business so that there is more competition in the market, which will prevent firms from adopting unfair practices.

[Additional] Competition lowers prices, improves quality and widens consumer choice - benefits that matter most to poor consumers. India replaced the MRTP Act, 1969 with the Competition Act, 2002 (Raghavan Committee); the CCI prohibits anti-competitive agreements (cartels), abuse of dominant position and regulates combinations (mergers). The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 added a deal-value threshold (Rs 2,000 crore) for merger notification and settlement/commitment mechanisms. Ease-of-business steps: Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023 (decriminalised 183 provisions in 42 Central Acts), National Single Window System, DPIIT's Business Reforms Action Plan for States, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (exit of failed firms).

[Correction] Do not quote the World Bank "Doing Business" rank as current: India was 63rd of 190 in Doing Business 2020, but the report was discontinued in September 2021 after data irregularities. Its successor, B-READY, is being rolled out in phases; India's inclusion and score are [Verification Required].

3. Indian Economy After Independence: India at the Time of Independence

India inherited a stagnant, backward and colonial economy at Independence, reflected in the indicators below. The causes lay largely in colonial policy: de-industrialisation of handicrafts, the drain of wealth (Dadabhai Naoroji), exploitative land-revenue systems (zamindari), commercialisation of agriculture without investment, and infrastructure (railways, ports) built mainly to serve British trade (NCERT, Indian Economic Development, Chapter 1).

4. Five Year Plans in India - The Planning Commission

The government appointed the Planning Commission in 1950 to develop Five Year Plans for economic development through assessment and allocation of resources.

[Correction] Precise facts: set up on 15 March 1950 by a resolution of the Union Cabinet (PIB, 1 January 2015) - it was neither constitutional nor statutory. The Prime Minister (Jawaharlal Nehru) was its ex officio Chairman; the first Deputy Chairman was Gulzarilal Nanda. It was replaced by NITI Aayog on 1 January 2015. Its mandate included assessing the country's material, capital and human resources, formulating plans for their most effective and balanced use, and fixing priorities.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Central problems: what, how and for whom to produce. Capitalism: price mechanism, private property, laissez-faire. Socialism: central planning authority, State ownership of means of production.
  • "Socialist" added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976); "socialistic pattern of society" - Avadi, 1955; Industrial Policy Resolutions 1948 and 1956.
  • CSR: Section 135, Companies Act 2013; in force 1 April 2014; thresholds (any one, preceding FY): net worth Rs 500 crore, turnover Rs 1,000 crore, net profit Rs 5 crore.
  • Spend at least 2% of average net profit (Section 198) of the three preceding FYs; activities in Schedule VII (13 items); preference to local area.
  • Unspent: ongoing projects → Unspent CSR Account (30 days; spend in 3 FYs); others → Schedule VII fund within 6 months. Penalty: 2× amount or Rs 1 crore (lower); officers 1/10 or Rs 2 lakh (lower).
  • CSR Committee: 3+ directors incl. 1 independent (not needed if obligation is up to Rs 50 lakh). Implementing agencies register via Form CSR-1 (from 1 April 2021). Impact assessment if average obligation is Rs 10 crore+.
  • CSR spend: Rs 34,908.75 crore in FY 2023-24; Rs 1,44,159 crore over FY 2019-20 to FY 2023-24.
  • Independent directors: one-third of the Board in listed companies (Sec. 149(4)); max two terms of 5 years; must hold less than 2% of voting power with relatives.
  • Board meetings: at least 4 a year, gap at most 120 days (Sec. 173); AGM gap at most 15 months (Sec. 96).
  • Clause 49 (2000) from Kumar Mangalam Birla Committee; Narayana Murthy Committee (2003); Uday Kotak Committee (2017); SEBI LODR 2015; NFRA (2018).
  • Competition Act 2002 replaced MRTP Act 1969; CCI. Jan Vishwas Act 2023: 183 provisions, 42 Acts.
  • At Independence (NCERT): literacy less than 16% (female about 7%); life expectancy 32 years; IMR 218; 70-75% workforce in agriculture, 10% manufacturing. Census 1951 literacy 18.33%.
  • Planning Commission: 15 March 1950, Cabinet resolution, PM as Chairman; NDC: 6 August 1952; NITI Aayog: 1 January 2015.

Mains Points

  • Market + safeguards = inclusive growth: markets deliver efficiency; ESG norms, regulators and competition correct externalities, information asymmetry and market power so that gains are shared.
  • CSR as a bridge, not a substitute: mandatory CSR mobilises about Rs 35,000 crore a year, but it is small relative to public spending, concentrated in richer States and at risk of "tick-box" compliance; it complements, not replaces, the welfare State.
  • From "comply or explain" to "comply or pay": the 2021 regime (unspent accounts, transfers and civil penalties) improves enforcement but has been criticised as a "quasi-tax" on profits.
  • Corporate governance protects minority stakeholders: independent directors, audit committees, auditor rotation, disclosure and class-action suits reduce promoter dominance (principal-agent problem) and build investor trust - a precondition for capital formation.
  • Competition is pro-poor: lower prices and better quality benefit consumers; ease of doing business brings new entrants and jobs.
  • Colonial legacy explains planning: stagnation, a backward agriculture, missing capital-goods industry and poor human development led India to choose State-led planning in a mixed economy in 1950-51.

Q3. UPSC CSE Mains 2014, GS Paper III - Capitalism and inclusive growth

Q4. UPSC CSE Mains 2017, GS Paper IV - CSR

Approach: Define CSR and India's Section 135 mandate (Section 2.2). Sustainability: environmental stewardship, local supply chains, skilled workforce. Limits: "compliance" CSR, greenwashing, short-term costs; ethical value lies in stakeholder trust, not only profit. Corrections Made

  1. Socialism: central planning authority (Gosplan, Planning Commission); means of production vs personal property; laissez-faire spelling.
  2. CSR origin: voluntary guidelines 2009/2011; mandatory from 1 April 2014.
  3. CSR thresholds: any one (OR), immediately preceding FY; not cumulative; not a 3-year profit average.
  4. CSR amount: 2% of average net profit (Section 198, pre-tax), not profit after tax; CSR Committee; impact assessment.
  5. Schedule VII: 13 items, not 11.
  6. Implementation: CSR-1 registered agencies with track record; PMNRF/PM CARES is a Schedule VII item.
  7. Non-compliance: from "comply or explain" to mandatory transfer of unspent funds and civil penalty (2021).
  8. Corporate governance origin: Clause 49 (2000), Birla, Murthy and Kotak committees, LODR 2015.
  9. Independent directors: one-third for listed companies, 2 for specified public companies; 2% voting-power test.
  10. Filings: with the Registrar of Companies (AOC-4, MGT-7); auditor rotation.
  11. Meetings: AGM yearly (15-month gap); 4 Board meetings a year with a 120-day maximum gap.
  12. Shareholder disclosure: public holders above 1% in shareholding pattern (listed); SBO 10%; "2%" is the ID test.
  13. Ease of doing business: Doing Business discontinued 2021; last rank 63.
  14. Pre-Independence growth: NCERT figures (<2%, ~0.5% per capita); 0.7%/0% flagged.
  15. Agriculture: 70-75% workforce (NCERT); 53% GDP share flagged; foodgrains 50.8 MT (1950-51).
  16. Industry: 10% workforce (NCERT); 17% GDP share flagged; consumer-goods dominance, TISCO.
  17. Literacy: NCERT 16% / 7% vs Census 1951 18.33% / 8.86%.
  18. Health and poverty: life expectancy 32, IMR 218; "80% households, two meals" flagged.
  19. Spellings: Independence, at least.
  20. Planning Commission: 15 March 1950, Cabinet resolution, non-statutory; replaced by NITI Aayog 1 January 2015.
