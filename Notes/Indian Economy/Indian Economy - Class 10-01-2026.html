1. VB-G RAM G Act, 2025 - The Successor to MGNREGA

The Viksit Bharat - Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 came into force on 1 July 2026 and replaced MGNREGA. It is meant to be a more modern architecture of rural development that combines (i) livelihood security, (ii) durable asset creation, (iii) technology-enabled governance, (iv) convergence planning and (v) climate resilience. It is implemented by the Ministry of Rural Development and is aligned with the vision of Viksit Bharat @2047.

[Correction] Official name: Viksit Bharat - Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin), short form VB-G RAM G (not "Vikisit ... Rozgar Evam Ajeevika Mission - Grameen"). It is a statutory Act, not merely a scheme: the Bill was introduced in Lok Sabha on 16 December 2025, received Presidential assent on 21 December 2025, and MGNREGA stood repealed with effect from 1 July 2026 (national launch: 2 July 2026, Tirupati district, Andhra Pradesh). Each State must notify its own scheme consistent with the Act within six months of commencement - so "introduced from July 2026" should read "in force from 1 July 2026".

[Correction] Spellings: "MNREGA" = MGNREGA; "Vikisit" = Viksit; "livelyhood" = livelihood; "convergance" = convergence; "resiliance" = resilience; "guarenteed" = guaranteed; "payed" = paid; "aforestation" = afforestation.

1.1 Main Features

1. Guaranteed employment raised from 100 to 125 days

The number of days of guaranteed wage employment has been increased from 100 to 125 days in a financial year. This aims to strengthen income stability, support rural consumption and give greater economic resilience to vulnerable households.

[Correction] The guarantee is per rural household, not "per worker": "not less than 125 days of wage employment per rural household in each financial year to households whose adult members volunteer to undertake unskilled manual work" (Section 5(1)). The adult members of a household share the 125 days.

2. Smooth, worker-friendly transition

Existing verified job cards remain valid under VB-G RAM G until new cards are issued.

[Correction] Only MGNREGA job cards for which e-KYC has been completed remain valid, until the new Gramin Rozgar Guarantee Cards are issued ("till new cards are not issued" = until new cards are issued). [Additional] Ongoing MGNREGA works continue under the new Act and are prioritised for completion, so assets are not left half-built (PIB FAQ, 11 May 2026).

3. Registration of workers without cards

Households without a card can apply for registration, and they should not be denied the opportunity to work.

[Correction] Registration is by household: any rural household without an MGNREGA job card applies through any adult member to the Gram Panchayat, giving names, ages and address, for a Gramin Rozgar Guarantee Card. "Should not be denied" is a legal entitlement, not just an intention - the Union Minister's stated goal is that "no eligible rural worker should remain without work even for a day" (PIB, 30 June 2026).

4. Demand for work and unemployment allowance

Workers can demand work orally, in writing or digitally. If employment is not provided, they receive an unemployment allowance of not less than one-fourth of the notified wage rate for the first 30 days in a financial year, and not less than one-half of the notified wage rate after that.

[Correction] Add the trigger: employment must be provided within 15 days of the application; if not, the allowance becomes due. It is payable by the State Government. "Fiscal year" in India = financial year (April-March). Written demand uses the same Form 6 as under MGNREGA; demand can be made to the Gram Panchayat, the Programme Officer or any authorised person. [Additional] PIB states that the Act "removes earlier dis-entitlement provisions", restoring the allowance as a meaningful safeguard. Work should be given within 5 km of the village; beyond that (within the block) workers get an extra 10% of the wage for transport and living costs.

5. Timely, transparent and accountable wage payment

Wages continue to be paid through DBT. Wages are to be paid weekly and in any case not delayed beyond 15 days; if delayed, compensation is paid at 0.05% per day of the unpaid wages. Attendance is captured in real time using face authentication.

[Correction] Precise wording: wages are paid "weekly or in any case not later than a fortnight after closure of the muster roll" (Section 5(3)). The 0.05% a day is delay compensation under Schedule II on the unpaid wages of the worker - not "interest on the pending wage bill" of the government. Face-authentication attendance has an exception-handling mechanism for poor network, device failure or other genuine problems (PIB FAQ, 11 May 2026). The 0.05% rate is the same as under MGNREGA.

6. From full central wage funding to 60:40 cost sharing

The scheme has been changed from a central sector scheme to a Centrally Sponsored Scheme. The Centre and the States share expenditure in the ratio 60:40. Budget for FY 2026-27: Rs 95,692 crore.

[Correction] MGNREGA was not a Central Sector Scheme - it was already a Centrally Sponsored Scheme (one of the "core of the core" schemes in Budget documents), but the Centre bore 100% of unskilled wages and 75% of material and skilled-wage costs. The real change is the cost-sharing pattern: 60:40 (Centre:State) for wages, material and administrative costs; 90:10 for North-Eastern and Himalayan States; 100% Central funding for UTs without legislature. Funding now follows State-wise normative allocations fixed on objective parameters (Sections 4(5), 22(4)); the State bears any spending above its allocation, and also pays unemployment allowance and delay compensation (PRS bill summary). The figure is Rs 95,692.31 crore, the Union Budget Estimate for FY 2026-27 (1 February 2026) - the Centre's share only; total spending will be higher once State shares are added. PIB (30 June 2026) described the same amount as the interim allocation made to States and UTs for the rollout. "FY 2027" should be written FY 2026-27.

The Act expands the scope of work and aligns it with the goal of Viksit Bharat, on the idea of "employment as well as dignity". Works fall under four thematic domains (Section 4(2) read with Schedule I):

8. Seasonal pause for farm labour

Every State Government declares a pause period in the financial year so that a shortage of agricultural labour during sowing and harvesting can be avoided.

[Correction] The pause is "up to 60 days in aggregate" (it need not be one continuous 60-day block), notified in advance by the State (Section 6). The 125-day guarantee is not reduced - it must be provided in the remaining part of the year. [Additional] This responds to the long-debated criticism that MGNREGA drew labour away from farms in peak seasons (see 30 September notes).

9. Dignity, safety and social security at the worksite

The Act emphasises dignity, safety and social security. It legally guarantees worksite facilities - clean drinking water, rest areas (shade) and a first-aid kit, along with free medicine and treatment in case of accidents. Where many young children accompany women workers, one woman worker is deputed for child care.

[Correction] The garbled point "At workers with more than 5 children below 5 years" is corrected to the established rule: if five or more children below the age of six years accompany women workers at a site, one of the women workers is deputed to look after them (and is paid the wage). This was the rule in Schedule II of MGNREGA; the PIB FAQ (11 May 2026) confirms only "safe drinking water, shade for children and rest periods, and a first aid box" under the new Act, so the crèche rule and free treatment for worksite injury under VB-G RAM G are [Verification Required] against Schedule II of the 2025 Act.

10. Technology-enabled governance

Modern, technology-enabled governance through biometric authentication, mobile-based monitoring, real-time dashboards and AI-enabled analytics, along with a strong social audit mechanism.

[Correction] Statutory technology provisions (Sections 23-24; PRS): (i) biometric authentication for transactions, (ii) geospatial technology for planning and monitoring, (iii) mobile-application dashboards for real-time tracking, (iv) weekly public disclosure (digital and physical) with weekly Gram Panchayat disclosure meetings, and a "Janata Board" at every worksite. Social audits are conducted by Gram Sabhas (Section 20). "AI-enabled analytics" is not named in the PIB/PRS summaries - [Verification Required].

11. Relaxations during calamities

The Central Government can make temporary relaxations during calamities and extraordinary situations.

[Correction] The Centre may make special relaxations - expanding permissible works, enhancing wage employment and relaxing documentation norms - on the recommendation of the State Government during natural calamities or extraordinary circumstances (PIB FAQ, 11 May 2026). Nothing in the official summaries allows the Centre to suspend the Act; relaxations expand support. Do not confuse this with the State's seasonal pause (feature 8).

Inclusive health = availability + affordability + quality.

4. Question: Explain the Role of Financial Inclusion in Economic Development

Introduction. The Government has promoted financial inclusion through PMJDY, MUDRA, PM SVANidhi, subsidised insurance (PMJJBY, PMSBY, PMFBY) and pension schemes such as the Atal Pension Yojana, so that vulnerable groups have enough capacity to contribute to the goal of Viksit Bharat. Financial inclusion helps development through two routes - growth and inclusion.

A. It creates growth by

  1. Mobilising savings - PMJDY deposits of Rs 3.17 lakh crore (19 August 2026) can be lent for investment.
  2. Providing loans - MUDRA (57.79 crore loans, Rs 40.07 lakh crore sanctioned by March 2026) and PM SVANidhi (outlay Rs 7,332 crore up to March 2030) help small businesses start and expand.
  3. Providing insurance - PMFBY (4.19 crore farmer applications in 2024-25) lowers the risk of crop loss and encourages farmers to grow high-value crops.
  4. Mobilising long-term savings through pension and insurance funds, which can finance infrastructure projects.

B. It creates inclusion by

  1. Banking inclusion - financial identity, better-targeted subsidies (DBT) and safety of savings.
  2. Credit inclusion - livelihood sources, education finance and business development for vulnerable groups.
  3. Insurance inclusion - protects families from the sudden loss of a life or a health shock.
  4. Pension inclusion - reduces old-age vulnerability.

Conclusion. Policy focus on financial inclusion should be increased to achieve not only inclusion but also stable GDP growth - by deepening usage (credit, insurance) and financial literacy, not just account access.

[Correction] Data updates: PMJDY deposits are Rs 3.17 lakh crore (19 August 2026), not Rs 2.9 lakh crore; PM SVANidhi's Rs 7,332 crore is the total outlay of the restructured scheme up to 31 March 2030 (Cabinet, 27 August 2025), not an annual budget and not Rs 7,360 crore; PMFBY's "more than 4 crore beneficiaries" is 4.19 crore farmer applications in 2024-25, the highest since launch (PIB, 29 August 2026) - applications are not unique farmers, since one farmer can insure several crops or plots. "PM FBY" = PMFBY; "incurance" = insurance; "laons" = loans; "vulerable" = vulnerable; "Therefor" = Therefore. Scheme details are in the 30 September notes.

[Additional] Why insurance raises growth: without cover, a poor farmer chooses low-risk, low-return crops (risk aversion). Insurance shifts risk to a pool, so the farmer can invest in seeds, fertiliser and high-value crops - raising average output. Similarly, pension and insurance funds are long-term (patient) capital, matching the long gestation of roads, power and ports.

Financial inclusion
(bank + credit + insurance + pension)→Growth route: savings → investment; credit → enterprise; insurance → risk-taking; pension funds → infrastructure+Inclusion route: identity and DBT; livelihood credit; shock protection; old-age security→Stable, inclusive GDP growth

5. Question: Examine How Financial Inclusion Leads to Upliftment of the Standard of Living (150 words)

Introduction. Financial inclusion means affordable access to savings, credit, insurance and pensions for all. India now has 59 crore PMJDY accounts, and adult account ownership is about 89% (Global Findex 2025).

How it raises living standards

  1. Higher income: collateral-free credit (MUDRA, SVANidhi, KCC) funds self-employment and replaces costly moneylender loans.
  2. Full welfare transfers: DBT into accounts cuts leakages, so the poor receive full benefits.
  3. Consumption smoothing: savings and overdraft help families cope with lean seasons.
  4. Shock protection: PMJJBY, PMSBY and PM-JAY prevent distress sale of assets after death, accident or illness.
  5. Human capital: education loans and health cover improve children's prospects.
  6. Old-age security and empowerment: APY pensions; women's own accounts raise bargaining power.

Limits: dormant accounts, low credit usage, fraud and low literacy.

Conclusion. Financial inclusion turns access into security and opportunity; deepening usage will lift living standards sustainably.

Exam point: "Examine" needs mechanism + evidence + limits. Standard of living = income + consumption stability + security + capabilities; link each scheme to one of these.

6. Question: Is It Possible to Have Inclusive Growth in a Market Economy? (250 words)

Introduction. A market economy allocates resources through prices and private enterprise; inclusive growth is growth whose benefits and opportunities reach all sections. Markets generate growth efficiently, but they do not automatically distribute it fairly.

Why markets alone may exclude

  1. Unequal starting points: the poor lack assets, skills and collateral, so markets reward those already endowed.
  2. Market failures: information asymmetry (credit rationing), public goods (rural roads, vaccination) and externalities are under-supplied.
  3. Risk: no private insurance for the poorest; shocks push them back into poverty.

Why it is still possible

  1. Growth creates resources: higher tax revenue funds welfare - India's rapid growth financed DBT, PM-JAY and housing; about 24.82 crore people exited multidimensional poverty between 2013-14 and 2022-23 (NITI Aayog, 2024).
  2. Markets create jobs and cut prices: competition made mobile data and UPI payments cheap for the poor.
  3. State as enabler: public infrastructure (PMGSY, BharatNet), human capital (Samagra Shiksha, PMKVY), financial inclusion (PMJDY, MUDRA) and social protection (VB-G RAM G) let the poor participate in markets.
  4. Public rails, private innovation: DPI (Aadhaar, UPI, ONDC) lets private firms serve small users at low cost.

Challenges: jobless growth, informality, regional and gender gaps, rising inequality.

Conclusion. Inclusive growth is possible in a market economy when the State ensures equality of opportunity - markets for efficiency, the State for equity. The Nordic and East Asian experiences show this mix works.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • VB-G RAM G = Viksit Bharat - Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025; introduced 16 December 2025; assent 21 December 2025; in force 1 July 2026; MGNREGA repealed the same day.
  • 125 days per rural household per financial year (Section 5(1)); Gramin Rozgar Guarantee Card; e-KYC job cards valid meanwhile.
  • Work within 15 days and 5 km (else +10% wage beyond 5 km); unemployment allowance at least 1/4 of wage (first 30 days), 1/2 thereafter, paid by State.
  • Wages weekly or within a fortnight of muster-roll closure; delay compensation 0.05% per day; face-authentication attendance.
  • Funding 60:40; 90:10 NE and Himalayan; 100% Centre for UTs without legislature; normative allocation; admin ceiling 9% (was 6%); material at most 40%.
  • Four domains: water security; core rural infrastructure; livelihood infrastructure; extreme-weather mitigation. Plans: Viksit Gram Panchayat Plans + PM Gati Shakti.
  • Seasonal pause: up to 60 days in aggregate, notified by State. Social audit by Gram Sabha (Section 20).
  • FY 2026-27 BE: Rs 95,692.31 crore (Centre's share).
  • PMGSY 2000; Bharatmala Phase I 2017; Amrit Bharat Express 30 December 2023; DDUGJY 2014-15 (100% villages, 28 April 2018); Saubhagya 25 September 2017 (2.86 crore households); BharatNet 2.21 lakh GPs.
  • JJM: 15 August 2019; 16.7% → 82.09% rural tap coverage (July 2026); 55 lpcd.
  • Samagra Shiksha 2018; PM SHRI 2022 (14,500+ schools); EMRS for ST students, Classes VI-XII; PMKVY 15 July 2015 (MSDE).
  • UIP covers 12 vaccine-preventable diseases (9 national + 3 sub-national); Mission Indradhanush 25 December 2014.
  • Medical colleges 387 (2014) → 844 (2026); PMBJP: 20,149 Jan Aushadhi Kendras (30 June 2026).
  • PMFBY: 4.19 crore farmer applications in 2024-25 (record).

Mains Points

  • From safety net to development tool: VB-G RAM G keeps the rights-based core of MGNREGA but links employment to durable, climate-resilient assets and convergence planning.
  • Fiscal federalism trade-off: 60:40 sharing and normative allocation give predictability and fiscal discipline, but shift risk to States and may ration a demand-driven right.
  • Labour-market balance: the 60-day pause addresses farm-labour shortages but can weaken the bargaining power of landless workers.
  • Technology vs exclusion: face authentication and dashboards curb fraud, but need robust exception handling to avoid excluding genuine workers.
  • Inclusive growth strategy = access + capability + security: infrastructure gives access, HRD gives capability, financial inclusion and social protection give security.
  • Coverage vs quality: near-universal tap connections, electrification and accounts coexist with functionality, learning-quality and usage gaps - the next policy frontier.
  • Market + State: inclusive growth is possible in a market economy when the State supplies public goods, human capital and safety nets, and DPI lowers the cost of serving small users.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2011 (GS Paper I) - Inclusive growth

Inclusive growth as enunciated in the Eleventh Five Year Plan does not include one of the following:

  1. Reduction of poverty
  2. Extension of employment opportunities
  3. Strengthening of capital market
  4. Reduction of gender inequality

Answer: (c). Inclusive growth aims at poverty reduction, employment and equity (gender, social, regional); capital-market strengthening is a financial-sector goal (Section 6).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2011 (GS Paper I) - Instruments of inclusive growth

Which of the following can aid in furthering the Government's objective of inclusive growth?

  1. Promoting Self-Help Groups
  2. Promoting Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises
  3. Implementing the Right to Education Act
  1. 1 only
  2. 1 and 2 only
  3. 2 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (d). SHGs (financial inclusion), MSMEs (jobs, MUDRA) and RTE (human capital) all widen participation in growth (Section 3, Section 4).

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2016 (GS Paper I) - Mission Indradhanush

'Mission Indradhanush' launched by the Government of India pertains to

  1. immunization of children and pregnant women
  2. construction of smart cities across the country
  3. India's own search for the Earth-like planets in outer space
  4. New Educational Policy

Answer: (a). A catch-up immunisation drive under UIP (Section 3.B).

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - PMKVY

With reference to Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana, consider the following statements:

  1. It is the flagship scheme of the Ministry of Labour and Employment.
  2. It, among other things, will also impart training in soft skills, entrepreneurship, financial and digital literacy.
  3. It aims to align the competencies of the unregulated workforce of the country to the National Skill Qualification Framework.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 and 3 only
  2. 2 only
  3. 2 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (c). PMKVY belongs to the Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship, not Labour; RPL aligns informal skills to the NSQF (Section 3.A).

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2011 (GS Paper I) - Rural employment guarantee eligibility

Among the following who are eligible to benefit from the "Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act"?

  1. Adult members of only the scheduled caste and scheduled tribe households
  2. Adult members of below poverty line (BPL) households
  3. Adult members of households of all backward communities
  4. Adult members of any household

Answer: (d). The same universal, self-targeted eligibility (rural households whose adults volunteer for unskilled manual work) continues under VB-G RAM G (Section 1.2).
