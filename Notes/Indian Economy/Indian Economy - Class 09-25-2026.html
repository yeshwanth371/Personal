1. Applications of Market Equilibrium

Once equilibrium price is set where quantity demanded = quantity supplied (see 24 September notes), any factor that shifts the demand or supply curve creates excess demand or excess supply at the old price, and the price moves to a new equilibrium.

Four rules of market equilibrium (the other curve unchanged)
#FactorCurve shiftAt old priceEquilibrium price[Additional] Equilibrium quantity
1Factors that increase demand (higher income for a normal good, dearer substitute, favourable tastes)D shifts rightExcess demandRisesRises
2Factors that reduce demand (dearer complement, unfavourable tastes)D shifts leftExcess supplyFallsFalls
3Factors that reduce supply (higher input cost, per-unit tax, crop failure)S shifts leftExcess demandRisesFalls
4Factors that increase supply (better technology, subsidy, bumper harvest)S shifts rightExcess supplyFallsRises

[Correction] Rule 2 in the rough notes ("will deamand reducing price") should read: factors that reduce demand will reduce the price of the commodity. All four rules hold only ceteris paribus - the other curve does not shift and the curves have their normal slopes (D downward, S upward). Price is only half the result: demand shifts move price and quantity in the same direction, while supply shifts move them in opposite directions.

Figures 1a-1d: original qualitative schematics of the standard demand-supply model; no empirical data. Conceptual basis: NCERT, Introductory Microeconomics, Chapter 5.

Exam point: onion or tomato price spikes after unseasonal rain are supply-side (Rule 3: price up, quantity down). Festival-season price rises are demand-side (Rule 1: price up, quantity up). If both curves shift together, only one of price or quantity can be predicted without knowing the size of the shifts.

2. Economic Growth and GDP

2.1 Growth

Economic growth is the sustained increase in the real GDP (GDP at constant prices) of an economy over time.

Growth rate (%) = (Real GDPt - Real GDPt-1) / Real GDPt-1 x 100
t = current period; t-1 = the same period of the previous year (year-on-year comparison). Real GDP is valued at the prices of a base year (now 2022-23 in India), so changes reflect output (volume) and not prices.

[Correction] "Growth is the growth of the GDP" needs one qualifier: growth is measured by real GDP, not nominal GDP. Nominal GDP (current prices) can rise only because prices rise. Example (official): in Q1 (April-June) 2026-27, nominal GDP grew 10.3% but real GDP grew 7.8%; the gap reflects price change (implicit GDP deflator growth of about 2.3%) (MoSPI, 31 August 2026, base 2022-23).

2.2 Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during a given period (in India, the financial year, 1 April to 31 March), whether produced by residents or non-residents.

[Correction] Add "market value" (goods are added up in money terms) and "irrespective of who produces": GDP counts production by foreign-owned firms inside India, and excludes production by Indian residents abroad. The "accounting year" for India's national accounts is the financial year (April-March), not the calendar year. GDP is a flow, measured per period.

GDP = GVA at basic prices + Product taxes - Product subsidies (production approach)
GVA = gross value added by all producing units. Check with official data, Q1 2026-27 at constant prices: GVA Rs 73.82 lakh crore + net product taxes Rs 7.54 lakh crore = GDP Rs 81.36 lakh crore (MoSPI, 31 August 2026).
[Additional] Expenditure approach: GDP = PFCE + GFCE + GFCF + Change in stocks + Valuables + (Exports - Imports).

[Additional] GNP (or GNI) = GDP + Net factor income from abroad (NFIA). GDP is domestic (territory-based); GNP is national (residents-based). For India, NFIA is usually negative, so GNP is slightly lower than GDP.

2.3 Who Estimates GDP and When

GDP is estimated by the National Accounts Division of the National Statistics Office (NSO), Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI). Estimates are released both quarterly and annually.

[Correction] (i) Ministry name: Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. (ii) Early estimates are not "unreal": they are preliminary because they are built from partial indicator data (the benchmark-indicator method extrapolates the previous year's level using indicators such as IIP, GST, crop estimates and company results). They are revised as fuller data (company accounts, surveys, government accounts) arrive. (iii) "Actual GDP after 2 years" is imprecise: under the old 2011-12 series, the first, second and third revised estimates came in successive years, and the third revised (final) estimate came about three years after the financial year ended. [Verification Required] The exact revision calendar for the new 2022-23 series could not be confirmed from a MoSPI release calendar during preparation; the 31 August 2026 NAS release did update the revised estimates for 2022-23, 2023-24 and 2024-25 together.

2.4 Domestic Territory

For GDP, "domestic territory" (in SNA terms, economic territory) is wider than the political boundary. It includes:

Components of India's domestic territory
#IncludedClarification
1Political frontiers: land, territorial waters and airspaceTerritorial waters extend up to 12 nautical miles from the baseline.
2Ships and aircraft operated by residents between two or more countriesAn Air India aircraft flying Delhi-London is part of India's domestic territory.
3Fishing vessels, oil and natural-gas rigs and floating platforms operated by residents in international waters or where the country has exclusive rightsIncludes rigs in India's Exclusive Economic Zone operated by resident companies.
4India's embassies, consulates and military establishments located abroadEmbassy = mission in the host country's capital; consulate = mission in other cities of the host country.
ExcludedForeign embassies and consulates in India; offices of international organisations in India (for example, UN agencies, World Bank) - these are not part of India's domestic territory.

[Correction] (i) Residence is not decided by where a company is headquartered. A resident unit is one whose centre of economic interest lies in the country (ordinarily present or operating there for one year or more). A foreign-owned company registered and operating in India is a resident of India. (ii) Point 2 covers ships and aircraft operating between two or more countries. (iii) Point 4 refers to India's embassies and consulates located abroad, not missions in India's own capital; foreign embassies in New Delhi are excluded. Military establishments abroad are also included.

2.5 System of National Accounts (SNA)

The SNA is the United Nations' internationally agreed statistical standard of concepts, definitions, classifications and accounting rules for compiling GDP and other national accounts. Versions: 1953, 1968, 1993, 2008 and 2025.

[Correction] "Current rules are from before / on 2008 ... last met in 2025" should read: the 2008 SNA is the standard countries have been implementing; the United Nations Statistical Commission, at its 56th session (4-7 March 2025), adopted the 2025 SNA as the updated international standard (decision 56/101). Countries move to a new SNA only when they revise their national-accounts series, so implementation of 2025 SNA is a multi-year process. [Verification Required] The extent to which India's 2022-23 base series incorporates 2025 SNA recommendations was not confirmed from an official source.

[Additional] Areas highlighted in the 2025 SNA decision include measuring data as an asset, treatment of natural resources, and the net return to capital for non-market production; it was adopted alongside the IMF's new balance-of-payments manual (BPM7) for consistency.

3.1 Value Added Method and Double Counting

Value added (VA) = Value of output - Value of intermediate consumption
Total value added = Sum of value added by all producers = Value of final goods
[Additional] Net value added = Gross value added - Depreciation (consumption of fixed capital).
Class example: tea (hypothetical, Rs)
ProducerValue of outputIntermediate inputsValue added
Dairy farmer (milk)20020 - 0 = 20
Sugar producer10010 - 0 = 10
Tea stall (tea, final consumer good)10020 + 10 = 30100 - 30 = 70
Total13030100 = value of the final good (tea)

The rough-note arithmetic is correct: total value added = 20 + 10 + 70 = 100. Adding all outputs (130) would count milk and sugar twice - once on their own and again inside the price of tea. This is double counting.

[Additional] The example assumes the dairy farmer and sugar producer buy no inputs (a simplification). In reality every stage subtracts its own purchased inputs, but the result is the same: the sum of value added at all stages equals the value of final output.

4. Growth as a Quantitative Concept and the Trickle-Down Effect

Growth is a quantitative concept: it measures how much output increases, not how it is shared. It works through the accumulation of stock variables (capital stock, infrastructure, skills) by adding flow variables (investment each year).

Kt = Kt-1 + It - Dt
K = capital stock at the end of the year (stock); I = gross investment during the year (flow); D = depreciation during the year (flow). Net investment (I - D) is the net addition to the stock.

According to the traditional view, growth leads to faster industrialisation, urbanisation and commercialisation, which raise productivity and incomes, so living standards improve for all - the benefits "trickle down" to the poor.

GDP growth→Industrialisation, urbanisation, commercialisation→Higher productivity→Higher incomes→Better living standards for all?

[Correction] "Growth will automatically lead to improving everyone's standard of living" is the trickle-down hypothesis, not an established result. Experience, including India's, shows growth can be accompanied by rising inequality, jobless growth, regional imbalance and persistent deprivation. This gap is the reason planners moved to inclusive growth (Section 7).

[Correction] The examples are correct; one wording trap: saving (income not consumed during a period) is a flow, while accumulated savings or wealth (as on a date) is a stock. Similarly, fiscal deficit (flow) adds to public debt (stock), and investment (flow) adds to capital (stock).

6. Vicious Circle of Poverty vs Virtuous Cycle of Growth

[Correction] "Virtuous angle of growth Vs Vicious angle of poverty" should be the virtuous cycle of growth vs the vicious circle of poverty. The vicious-circle idea is associated with Ragnar Nurkse (Problems of Capital Formation in Underdeveloped Countries, 1953): "a country is poor because it is poor".

Exam point: the vicious circle can be broken from any link - raising saving (financial inclusion), investment (public capex, FDI), or productivity (education, health, technology). This is why human-capital spending is treated as an investment, not only welfare.

7. Inclusive Growth

Inclusive growth is a qualitative concept that focuses not only on the rate of growth but also on its distribution. It takes place when no section of society is left behind - growth creates opportunities for all and their benefits are widely shared.

[Additional] Inclusive growth became the central theme of India's Eleventh Five Year Plan (2007-12), "Towards Faster and More Inclusive Growth"; the Twelfth Plan (2012-17) was titled "Faster, More Inclusive and Sustainable Growth".

7.1 Equitable Distribution

Equity is different from equality. Equality gives everyone the same amount; equity gives each person what is fair given their needs and circumstances. Equitable distribution is achieved through equality of opportunity and freedom to make choices.

[Correction] "Equity implies distribution such that preferences are also considered" is better stated as distribution according to needs and circumstances (fairness), not preferences. Equity may require unequal treatment of unequals - for example, progressive income tax, reservations, targeted subsidies.

7.2 Removal of Poverty

Poverty means absolute deprivation of basic necessities such as food, shelter, education and health. Inclusive growth therefore needs direct policy targeting of poverty, not only reliance on growth.

[Additional] Absolute poverty = living below a fixed minimum standard (a poverty line). Relative poverty = being poor compared to others in the same society. Examples of direct targeting: the National Food Security Act 2013 (subsidised food grains), MGNREGA 2005 (guaranteed wage employment), and housing schemes such as PMAY.

7.3 Capability Enhancement / Removing Capability Deprivation

The capability approach was developed by Prof. Amartya Sen. Capabilities are the real freedoms people have to achieve what they value (being healthy, educated, adequately nourished, taking part in community life). Poverty is capability deprivation, not merely low income.

According to Sen, capabilities are enhanced when public services (education, health, nutrition, social security) are provided and discrimination on the basis of religion, caste, gender, race, region, language etc. is restricted. People can then participate fully in economic activity.

[Correction] "Novel prize" should be Nobel. Sen received the 1998 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel "for his contributions to welfare economics"; he was the first Indian to win it. [Additional] Indian-born Abhijit Banerjee shared the 2019 prize (with Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer) for an experimental approach to alleviating global poverty.

[Additional] Sen's ideas are set out in Development as Freedom (1999) and underpin UNDP's Human Development Index (first published 1990 under Mahbub ul Haq), which measures health, education and standard of living. In India, the Constitution's Article 15 prohibits discrimination by the State on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

7.4 Sustainable Development / Intergenerational Equity

[Correction] The rough notes stop at this heading; the point is completed below.

[Additional] Sustainable development is "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs" (Brundtland Commission, Our Common Future, 1987). Intergenerational equity means fairness between present and future generations: today's growth should not exhaust natural resources, degrade the environment or leave unsustainable debt for future generations. It has three pillars - economic, social and environmental - and is operationalised globally through the 17 Sustainable Development Goals of the UN 2030 Agenda (adopted 2015). Intra-generational equity (fairness within the present generation) is the other side of inclusive growth.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Demand up: P up, Q up. Demand down: P down, Q down. Supply down: P up, Q down. Supply up: P down, Q up (other curve unchanged).
  • GDP = market value of final goods and services produced within the domestic territory in a period; a flow. GNP = GDP + NFIA.
  • GDP estimated by NSO, MoSPI; new series base year 2022-23 (released 27 February 2026); double deflation for manufacturing.
  • Estimate sequence: First Advance (Jan) - Second Advance (Feb) - Provisional (May/June) - Revised estimates. Quarterly estimates about two months after each quarter.
  • Domestic territory includes resident-operated ships/aircraft between countries, resident-operated rigs, and India's embassies, consulates and military bases abroad; excludes foreign embassies and international organisations in India.
  • SNA versions: 1953, 1968, 1993, 2008, 2025 (adopted by UN Statistical Commission, March 2025).
  • Final goods = consumer + capital goods. Intermediate goods are excluded to avoid double counting. Final vs intermediate depends on end use.
  • Value added = output - intermediate consumption; NVA = GVA - depreciation.
  • Stock: at a point of time (capital, wealth, money supply, public debt). Flow: over a period (GDP, saving, investment, fiscal deficit).
  • Vicious circle of poverty: Ragnar Nurkse. Capability approach: Amartya Sen (Economics prize 1998). Sustainable development definition: Brundtland Report, 1987.
  • Inclusive growth: theme of the 11th Plan (2007-12); 12th Plan added "sustainable".

Mains Points

  • Growth vs inclusion: high GDP growth is necessary but not sufficient; jobless growth, regional disparity and informal employment show trickle-down is not automatic. Inclusive growth requires deliberate policy - employment, human capital, financial inclusion, social protection.
  • Capability lens: spending on health, nutrition and education enlarges capabilities and productivity; anti-discrimination measures widen participation, especially for women and marginalised groups.
  • Data quality: GDP estimates are revised as data improve; the 2022-23 base revision (double deflation, new PPI, updated administrative data) aims to better capture the current economic structure. Policy should read early estimates with caution.
  • Sustainability: growth that depletes natural capital or piles up public debt transfers costs to future generations (intergenerational inequity); the 2025 SNA's treatment of natural resources supports "greener" measurement.
  • Price management: buffer stocks, imports and export curbs work by shifting supply to limit price spikes (Rules 3 and 4).

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 (GS Paper I) - Definition of national income

The national income of a country for a given period is equal to the

  1. total value of goods and services produced by the nationals
  2. sum of total expenditure
  3. sum of personal income of all individuals
  4. money value of final goods and services produced

Answer: (d). National income / GDP counts only the money (market) value of final goods and services produced in a period. Intermediate goods are left out to avoid double counting (Section 3, tea example).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2015 (GS Paper I) - Real vs nominal GDP

With reference to Indian economy, consider the following statements:

  1. The rate of growth of Real Gross Domestic Product has steadily increased in the last decade.
  2. The Gross Domestic Product at market prices (in rupees) has steadily increased in the last decade.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (b) 2 only. The level of nominal GDP rises almost every year because both output and prices rise. The growth rate of real GDP goes up and down with the business cycle (for example, it slowed after the 2008 global financial crisis), so it did not rise "steadily". This tests the difference between level and growth rate and between nominal and real (Section 2.1, Figure 2).

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 (GS Paper I) - Source of growth

Economic growth in country X will occur if

  1. there is technical progress in the world economy
  2. there is population growth in X
  3. there is capital formation in X
  4. the volume of trade grows in the world economy

Answer: (c). Investment (a flow) adds to the capital stock (a stock) of country X and raises its productive capacity: Kt = Kt-1 + It - Dt (Section 4). The other options happen outside X, or (population growth) do not by themselves raise output.

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 (GS Paper I) - Growth vs development

Increase in absolute and per capita real GNP do not connote a higher level of economic development, if

  1. industrial output fails to keep pace with agricultural output.
  2. agricultural output fails to keep pace with industrial output.
  3. poverty and unemployment increase.
  4. imports grow faster than exports.

Answer: (c). Real GNP can grow while poverty and unemployment rise. The gains then have not reached everyone, so trickle-down has failed. This is exactly the case for inclusive growth (Section 4, Section 7).

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014 (GS Paper I) - Plan objective

The main objective of the 12th Five-Year Plan is

  1. inclusive growth and poverty reduction
  2. inclusive growth and sustainable growth
  3. sustainable and inclusive growth to reduce unemployment
  4. faster, sustainable and more inclusive growth

Answer: (d). The Twelfth Plan (2012-17) was titled "Faster, More Inclusive and Sustainable Growth". It built on the Eleventh Plan's theme "Towards Faster and More Inclusive Growth" and added sustainability (Section 7, Section 7.4).

Q6. UPSC CSE Prelims 2012 (GS Paper I) - Instruments of inclusive growth

Which of the following can aid in furthering the Government's objective of inclusive growth?

  1. Promoting Self-Help Groups
  2. Promoting Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises
  3. Implementing the Right to Education Act

Select the correct answer using the codes given below:

  1. 1 only
  2. 1 and 2 only
  3. 2 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (d) 1, 2 and 3. SHGs improve financial inclusion and women's participation. MSMEs create jobs on a large scale. The Right to Education builds capabilities (Section 7.3).   7. Final vs intermediate goods: "for resale"; capital goods are final; end-use principle.
  8. Trickle-down: a hypothesis, not automatic.
  9. Stock and flow: saving (flow) vs savings (stock).
  10. Virtuous cycle / vicious circle: terminology; Nurkse.
  11. Equity: according to needs and circumstances, not preferences.
  12. Amartya Sen: "Nobel"; exact prize name and year.
  13. Sustainable development: incomplete point completed.
