1. British Land Revenue Policies

1.1 The Three Systems at a Glance

After the Diwani (1765) the Company needed a dependable land revenue, which was its biggest source of income. After the failed experiment of revenue farming (Ijarah) under Warren Hastings, three systems emerged, one for each part of British India: the Permanent Settlement (settlement with the zamindar), Ryotwari (settlement with the cultivator) and Mahalwari (settlement with the village / estate). 1.2 Permanent Settlement (Zamindari Settlement), 1793

  • Who: introduced by Cornwallis (Governor-General 1786-93), on the advice of John Shore (see c01).
  • When and where: 1793, in Bengal, Bihar, Odisha and the Banaras division of present-day Uttar Pradesh (see c02).
  • Extent: about 19% of British India (textbook figure; see c02).
  • Why: the reasons are in the next table; the consequences are in section 1.6.

[Correction] Name, office and date. The notes' "corn wallis" is Charles Cornwallis (Earl, later Marquess, Cornwallis), Governor-General of Bengal 1786-93 (the post of "Governor-General of India" arose only in 1833; see the 10-25 file). The settlement was first made for ten years in 1790 (the "decennial settlement") and declared permanent in 1793; Wikipedia gives the Court of Directors' proposal as 1786, the decennial settlement as 1790 and the permanent one as 1793, and says the regulations were dated 1 May 1793 (Permanent Settlement, Wikipedia); many textbooks give the proclamation as 22 March 1793 [Verification Required] on the exact day. John Shore (later Governor-General 1793-98) was a Company revenue official who drafted the settlement, but argued for testing it for ten years first, because he doubted that zamindars would trust "permanent" rates; Cornwallis wanted it made permanent at once (Wikipedia; some exam guides say it was "designed by Shore and implemented by Cornwallis": Vajiram and Ravi). The notes' "on the advice of John Shore" is therefore correct in part. [Additional] The settlement was one part of the Cornwallis Code (1793), which separated the Company's service into revenue, judicial and commercial branches.

[Correction] Areas and share. The notes' "Banaras division of UP" is not "UP" of 1793: the United Provinces did not exist until 1902 (Banaras division was in the Bengal Presidency and later in the North-Western Provinces). The settlement was extended to Banaras (c. 1795, Jonathan Duncan) and to the Northern Circars of Madras (c. 1802) [Verification Required] on both dates (Wikipedia says "later in Varanasi and also the northern districts of Madras"). "Odisha" is also not simple: the Company took Cuttack only in 1803, so the settlement covered Midnapore / parts of Orissa and other later arrangements [Verification Required]. The figure 19% (with 51% Ryotwari and 30% Mahalwari) is a standard exam figure for the share of British India's area; secondary study sites repeat it (for example GovtExamsStudy) but a primary or NCERT source was not opened [Verification Required].

Reasons for the introduction

Reasons for the Permanent Settlement (from the notes, reorganised)
#ReasonNote
1Motive is disputed. One view: Cornwallis mistakenly believed that zamindars were the owners of the land and so settled with them. Another view: he wanted to create private ownership of land and shift Indian agriculture to capitalist lines, so he settled with zamindars.There is no consensus; present as two interpretations, not a settled fact (c03).
2Administrative ease: it was easier for the Company to collect revenue from a few zamindars than from millions of peasants; revenue would also be regular and predictable.Followed the failure of annual and five-year farming (10-25 file).
3A loyal social base: the Company wanted to create a class of landholders loyal to the British.Bentinck (1829) openly praised this aim (see the [Additional] in c03).
4Incentive to improve production: because the Company's demand was fixed, any surplus above the fixed amount would remain with the landholder, who would then invest in agriculture and raise output.The notes' wording was garbled (c04).

[Correction] Motive: two views, not one fact. In the rough notes points 1 and 3 repeat the same two ideas (mistaken ownership; creating private property). They are merged above. Historians have interpreted the settlement differently: (a) an agrarian-capitalist / "improving landlord" model - Cornwallis "had in mind improving landlords in their own country, such as Coke of Norfolk" and stated that when "the demand of government is fixed, an opportunity is afforded to the landholder of increasing his profits" (Wikipedia), often linked to the Physiocratic idea that land is the source of wealth (a point asked in the 2024 Mains History optional); (b) a "rule of property" argument associated with Ranajit Guha (A Rule of Property for Bengal; the 1996 edition is cited by Wikipedia); (c) the view that officials misread the zamindars, who under the Mughals were revenue-collecting agents and not owners (NCERT, Themes in Indian History III, ch. 10, as cited by Wikipedia, which says zamindars "were not the landowners but rather revenue collector agents of the state"). [Additional] Contemporary official views: Bentinck (1829) said the settlement had "created a vast body of rich landed proprietors deeply interested in the continuance of the British Dominion"; Lord Hastings (the Marquess of Hastings, 1819) wrote that it had "subjected almost the whole of the lower classes throughout these provinces to most grievous oppression" (both quoted in Wikipedia, citing Keith, Speeches and Documents on Indian Policy). These are official / colonial assessments, to be set against the nationalist critique (R. C. Dutt) in the impact section.

[Correction] "Could not claim any amount above the surplus production". The sentence in the notes is garbled. The intended meaning: the state demand was fixed for ever, so the Company could not claim more even if cultivation expanded or prices rose, and the increase in output went to the zamindar. The Company hoped this would encourage investment and produce more revenue indirectly through trade (Wikipedia). In practice the opposite happened for the Company: its income stayed fixed while its expenses rose over time (Wikipedia).

Characteristic features

  1. Settlement with zamindars, who were treated as the owners of land though they had previously been revenue collectors; hence also called the zamindari settlement.
  2. The right was hereditary; zamindars could transfer, sell or mortgage their land.
  3. Sunset Law: the revenue had to be paid before sunset on a fixed date; otherwise the zamindari was sold by auction and the ownership lost.
  4. The Company / state could not demand more than the amount fixed (the demand was permanent, not revised).
  5. The zamindar kept 1/11 of the rental and paid 10/11 to the Company.

[Correction] Ownership, the 10/11 share and Sunset Law. (a) Under the Mughals the zamindars (and rajas, taluqdars) were hereditary revenue collectors; the 1793 Act made them proprietors in the sense that they could now sell and mortgage the land; the cultivators below them lost their earlier status as occupants and became tenants (Wikipedia). (b) 10/11 to the Company and 1/11 to the zamindar is the standard textbook split of the rental collected from the ryots (Wikipedia: "state demand was fixed at 89% of the rent and 11% was to be retained by the zamindar"); a coaching site states the fixed demand as about 45% of the produce [Verification Required] (Vajiram and Ravi). (c) The "Sunset Law" is a textbook name for the rule that the zamindari was auctioned if the dues were not paid by sunset on the due date; the Company's collectors would not allow for flood or drought (Wikipedia). It is the basis of a UPSC Prelims 2024 question. [Additional] The zamindars lost their armed forces and their courts (the judicial work went to Company courts) and were required to issue pattas (title deeds) to their tenants - an obligation that was often neglected (Wikipedia).

1.3 Ryotwari System

  • Who: devised by Captain Alexander Read and Thomas Munro; introduced across Madras by Munro as Governor (1820-27).
  • Where: Madras (Chennai) and Bombay (Mumbai) Presidencies, and later Sindh, Berar and parts of Assam. Share: about 51% of British India (textbook figure).

[Correction] Names, dates and areas. The notes' "alexander reed" is Alexander Read. Britannica: the system "was devised by Capt. Alexander Read and Thomas (later Sir Thomas) Munro at the end of the 18th century and introduced by the latter when he was governor (1820-27) of Madras" (Britannica, Ryotwari system). Read first tried it in the Baramahal region (after the 1792 Treaty of Seringapatam took the area from Mysore); Munro worked in the Ceded Districts from 1800 [Verification Required]; he became Governor of Madras in May 1820 (Wikipedia). So "1820" is the date of its general introduction in Madras, not of its invention. It was extended to Bombay later - the survey settlement under Wingate is dated 1827-35 in Dutt's account cited by Wikipedia; Goldsmid and Wingate's Bombay survey is more systematic than Madras's [Verification Required]. Sindh (annexed 1843), Berar (assigned by the Nizam to the Company in 1853) and Assam (taken after 1826) are dated here from general knowledge [Verification Required]. Madras also had zamindari and other tenures (for example in the Northern Circars), so "Madras = Ryotwari" is a generalisation.

Characteristic features

  1. Settlement with the peasants, called ryots - hence ryotwari (Britannica: from Arabic ra'iyyah, a peasant; the system removed the intermediary).
  2. Each ryot was given a patta, a document confirming his ownership (a heritable, transferable right as long as the revenue was paid).
  3. The rate of revenue was very high; in Madras it was up to about 50% of the standard produce (see c07).
  4. It was not fixed for ever; it was revised every 20-30 years.
  5. The Company retained the right to raise the revenue at each revision.

[Correction] How high the rate was, and what the official view said. The notes' "50% of standard production" is in line with study guides that give the initial Madras demand as about 45-55% of the estimated produce (GovtExamsStudy, secondary) [Verification Required]; R. C. Dutt's Economic History of India (cited by Wikipedia) says Munro reduced the rate over time but even the reduced tax was excessive (the exact fraction is truncated in the text read here [Verification Required]). The revision period of 20-30 years is the usual textbook range (30 years in Bombay) [Verification Required]. Official description (J. S. Mill, 1857): every registered holder is "recognised as its proprietor", may sublet, gift, sell or mortgage, "cannot be ejected so long as he pays the fixed assessment", and remissions are granted "in unfavourable seasons". Practice: peasants were not guaranteed relief in bad years, and the Madras Torture Commission (1854-55) recorded the use of torture to collect arrears (as noted by Vajiram and Ravi, in explaining the 2024 Prelims answer). Keep the official and critical accounts apart. [Additional] The cost of measuring every holding and the power given to petty revenue officials were drawbacks noted by Britannica.

1.4 Mahalwari System

  • Who: Holt Mackenzie and R. M. Bird, in 1822.
  • Where: western Uttar Pradesh (Agra region), later Central India and Punjab. Share: about 30% of British India (textbook figure).
  • How: several villages were grouped into a mahal; an individual was made responsible for collecting the revenue of the mahal.
  • Almost similar to the zamindari settlement, but the revenue was not fixed for ever; hence called the temporary zamindari settlement.

[Correction] Names, dates and stages. Holt Mackenzie (a Company civil servant) framed the scheme in Regulation VII of 1822; Robert Merttins Bird refined it under Governor-General William Bentinck by a new regulation in 1833 (the regulation number is [Verification Required]). Under the 1833 scheme the state demand was fixed at 66% of the rental value, for 30 years, and the work was completed under James Thomason (spelt "Thompson" in the Wikipedia text read here) [Verification Required]; the 66% proved heavy and the Saharanpur Rules (1855) under Dalhousie lowered it to 50%, though officers paid little attention (Mahalwari, Wikipedia). So "1822" is the first stage and 1833 the stage that made it workable. The "Agra region" is the North-Western Provinces (the Ganga-Yamuna Doab, Rohilkhand and Bundelkhand), taken from the Nawab of Awadh and the Marathas in 1801-03; Awadh (Oudh) itself had a separate talukdari settlement after annexation (1856) [Verification Required]. Punjab came to the Company after the Second Anglo-Sikh War (annexed 1849).

[Correction] "An individual was given the responsibility". The responsibility was collective: Wikipedia says the settlement was made with landlords or lambardars (village headmen, also called numberdars) who represented villages or groups of villages, "the village communities, the landlords were jointly responsible", and "individual responsibility was not assigned" (Mahalwari, Wikipedia). Britannica: for revenue purposes the name applied to "any compact area containing one or more villages, called estates"; where a zamindar held the whole estate the settlement was with him, otherwise with individual cultivators (Britannica, Mahalwari system). The notes' "an individual" is best read as the lambardar / headman acting for the village community. [Additional] The lambardar kept the records, surveyed, helped assess and collected the revenue; some regions saw the system break down because the state demand was large and rigid (Wikipedia, citing a coaching source [Verification Required]).

1.6 Impact of the Revenue Policies

The notes' summary: the British land revenue policies were exploitative and made the cultivators' lives miserable. The details follow.

(a) Absentee landlordism (Permanent Settlement areas)

  • After a few years the actual zamindar began to transfer the right of collecting revenue to middlemen and himself moved to the city for a comfortable life.
  • Over the years this spread, and many layers of middlemen came between the zamindar and the peasant.
  • This produced the Bengal phenomenon known as absentee landlordism.

[Correction] Sub-infeudation and causes. The layered middlemen are called sub-infeudation (zamindar → patnidars / dar-patnidars → jotedars → sharecroppers); the lease called patni was recognised by law in 1819 [Verification Required]. A main cause was the high fixed demand and the Sunset Law: many old zamindars lost their estates at auction and the buyers were often Company officials, merchants and bankers, who managed land through agents; B. S. Cohn notes a shift from "lineages and local chiefs" to "civil servants and their descendants, and to merchants and bankers", often absentee (Wikipedia). So the notes' picture (the zamindar himself leaves) is one cause; auction was another. [Additional] This "commercialisation of land" - land becoming a thing to buy, sell and mortgage - is the point Cohn makes and the notes make for Ryotwari areas (next).

(b) Debt trap and land alienation (Ryotwari areas)

High revenue
(cash)→Peasant fails

land never recovered→Land passes to

  • Because the rate was too high, when a peasant failed to pay he had to mortgage his land; once he did so he was caught in a vicious cycle of debt and could never recover the land.
  • Thus land passed from cultivators to non-cultivators (moneylenders, traders).
  • The policies made land a saleable commodity that could be freely bought and sold in the market.

[Correction] Scope of the claim. The same "land as a commodity" effect is attributed to all the systems, not only Ryotwari: Wikipedia notes that cash payment (introduced so the Company could become the exclusive buyer of Indian goods) "frequently proved economically untenable for cultivators, exposing them to the exorbitant demands of moneylenders when crops failed" (Ryotwari, Wikipedia). [Additional] Later exam-relevant results: the Deccan Riots (1875) against moneylenders in Ryotwari-settled Bombay districts, and the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879); the collapse of the Deccan cotton boom after the American Civil War (1861-65) fed this (LotusArise, secondary; these belong to a later class). R. C. Dutt (Economic History of India, 1902-04) is the nationalist economic historian who linked heavy revenue demand to poverty and famine.

2. Commercialisation of Indian Agriculture

Meaning: a change from cultivation for home consumption to cultivation for the market.

Revenue
preferred in cash→Peasants grow
cash crops→Industrial Revolution:
demand for raw materials→Dependence on
merchants→Exposure to
market swings
  • As the British preferred revenue in cash, peasants started to produce crops that could be sold in the market at a higher price: cotton, jute, sugarcane, etc. (see Map 2).
  • After the Industrial Revolution in Britain, demand for such crops rose because they served as inputs for British industries.
  • The British government in India therefore forced the peasants to cultivate cash crops; industrialisation accelerated commercialisation.
  • Peasants depended on merchants to sell their produce; the merchants exploited them by paying less.
  • They were also exposed to the erratic behaviour of the market.

[Correction] "Forced", timing and the wider picture. (a) "The British government forced the peasants" states one interpretation (the nationalist / Marxist view, which calls commercialisation artificial and forced; a UPSC history optional question of 2022 quotes this view: below). Others stress market incentives, railways, the American Civil War cotton boom (1861-65) and debt, and Sekhar Bandyopadhyay finds it hard to link cash crops directly to famine; Tirthankar Roy argues the rich "may have become richer" but "total income had increased" (as summarised in LotusArise, secondary). (b) Commercial crops were not new: Wikipedia notes the Mughal revenue system (zabt) favoured cash crops such as cotton, indigo, sugarcane and opium (Economic history of India). What changed was scale, direction (British industry and export markets) and coercion. (c) The notes' "cash crops like cotton, jute, sugarcane" are correct but incomplete; [Additional] indigo (Bengal, Bihar: the Indigo Revolt 1859-60, later Champaran 1917), opium (for the China trade), tea (Assam) and wheat (Punjab) were also important. LotusArise places the main surge after 1813 and especially around 1860. (d) The Permanent Settlement era also saw zamindars and officials push tenants to grow indigo and cotton instead of rice or wheat (Wikipedia).

3.1 Phase 1: Commercial Capitalism (1600-1800)

  • The EIC acted as a trading corporation. It brought bullion (gold and silver) and exchanged it for Indian goods (the notes' "pieces of metals").
  • Despite tough competition from rival companies, the EIC made a decent profit.
  • After the Battle of Plassey (1757) and the Battle of Buxar (1764) the Company stopped bringing bullion and bought Indian goods with Indian wealth. The wealth came from loot and plunder, taxes on internal trade, and the Diwani (revenue rights, 1765).
  • The purchase of Indian goods with Indian wealth is called the Company's "investment".
  • With the political control of Bengal, it tried to establish a monopoly: it fixed the prices of Indian goods, usually 35% below the market price.
  • It bought raw cotton and sold it to the artisans at a higher price, so the artisans suffered a double loss, as buyers (of raw material) and as sellers (of cloth).

[Correction] When the bullion stopped; names and figures. The notes link the end of bullion import to "Plassey, Buxar". The usual reading is that it stopped after the Company gained the Diwani in 1765, which made Bengal's revenue available to buy goods (the Battles of Plassey, 23 June 1757, and Buxar, 22 October 1764, made that possible). Wikipedia: "immediately following the East India Company gaining the right to collect revenue... the Company largely ceased a century and a half practice of importing gold and silver". Dates of the two battles: see the 10-24 file. The "rival companies" were chiefly the Dutch, French and independent British buyers, who competed for the same Bengal weavers; after 1765 the Company forced local merchants to fulfil its orders before others and dictated the price of yarn (Economic history of India, citing Om Prakash). The figure "35% lower than the market price" and the specific claim that the Company sold raw cotton to weavers at a higher price are textbook claims I could not trace to a source [Verification Required]. [Additional] Bullion before 1757: an estimated 28,000 tonnes of bullion flowed into the subcontinent between 1600 and 1800 to pay for its exports (Parthasarathi, cited in Wikipedia); the same article notes that Britain protected its own weavers by the Calico Acts (1685-1774), which put tariffs on imported Indian textiles. "Drain of wealth" (Dadabhai Naoroji, R. C. Dutt) is the later name for the one-way flow that "investment" began.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Permanent Settlement: 1793, Cornwallis (John Shore's plan); Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Banaras, Northern Circars; zamindar as proprietor; revenue fixed for ever; 10/11 to the Company, 1/11 to the zamindar; Sunset Law; c. 19% of British India; decennial settlement 1790.
  • Ryotwari: Alexander Read (Baramahal, 1792) and Thomas Munro (Governor of Madras 1820-27); Madras, Bombay, later Sindh, Berar, Assam; ryot gets a patta; revised every 20-30 years; high rate (c. 50% in Madras); c. 51%.
  • Mahalwari: Holt Mackenzie (Regulation VII, 1822), R. M. Bird (1833); NW Provinces / Agra, Central India, Punjab; village (mahal) with lambardar; temporary; 66% of rental, 50% after the Saharanpur Rules (1855); c. 30%.
  • Absentee landlordism (Permanent Settlement areas); debt trap and land alienation (Ryotwari areas); land became a commodity.
  • Commercialisation: cash revenue → cash crops (cotton, jute, sugarcane; also indigo, opium, tea); Industrial Revolution demand; dependence on merchants.
  • Commercial policy (class periods): 1600-1800 commercial capitalism; 1800-60 industrial capitalism; 1860-1947 finance capitalism. After the Diwani (1765) no more bullion; "investment" = buying Indian goods with Indian revenue.

Mains Points

  • Why three systems? A single model was not applied: the Company settled with whoever already held a recognised right in each region (zamindars in the east; cultivators in the south, where large zamindars were few; village communities in the north-west). The aims were the same: maximum, regular cash revenue.
  • Permanent Settlement: a "rule of property" that gave security of tenure to landlords, a loyal social base and predictable revenue, but at the cost of tenants' rights, sub-infeudation and absentee landlordism, and eventually a falling real value of the Company's income. Debate: Physiocratic / improving-landlord model vs misreading of zamindars as owners.
  • Ryotwari and Mahalwari: in theory more equitable (no middlemen; community-based) but with revisable, heavy demands, leading to debt, land transfer to moneylenders and, later, agrarian unrest (Deccan 1875).
  • Commercialisation: the product of cash revenue, new markets, railways and British industry; benefits went mainly to planters, merchants and moneylenders; peasants were tied to global price swings. Debate: forced vs market-driven.
  • Link to the economy as a whole: revenue policy → commercialisation → colonial economy (raw materials out, manufactures in) → the drain of wealth critique of the nationalists.

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Cornwallis - Governor-General 1786-93; Permanent Settlement and Cornwallis Code. John Shore - revenue official who drafted the settlement (Governor-General 1793-98). Zamindar - hereditary revenue collector made landlord in 1793. Sunset Law - auction if dues unpaid by sunset.
  • Alexander Read - first tried Ryotwari (Baramahal). Thomas Munro - Governor of Madras 1820-27. Ryot - cultivator. Patta - title deed. Wingate; Goldsmid - Bombay survey officers.
  • Holt Mackenzie - Regulation VII of 1822. Robert Merttins Bird - 1833 revision. James Thomason - completed the 1833 scheme. Mahal - village or estate. Lambardar - village headman. Saharanpur Rules - 1855 cut to 50%.
  • Absentee landlordism; sub-infeudation (patni) - rent-collecting rights passed down layers. Commercialisation of agriculture - production for the market. Investment - buying Indian goods with Indian revenue.
  • B. S. Cohn; Ranajit Guha; R. C. Dutt - historians cited on land and revenue. Physiocrats - French school that saw land as the source of wealth.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Zamindar vs ryot vs village: Permanent Settlement = zamindar; Ryotwari = ryot; Mahalwari = village (mahal) through the lambardar.
  • Permanent vs temporary: only the Permanent Settlement was fixed for ever; Ryotwari and Mahalwari were revised (Mahalwari is the "temporary zamindari settlement").
  • Who owned the land: Mughal zamindars were collectors; 1793 made them owners. Ryotwari did not mean the peasant paid no revenue in bad years (2024 Prelims).
  • 1820 vs 1792: Ryotwari general introduction (Munro, Madras) vs the Baramahal trial (Read). 1822 vs 1833: Mackenzie's Mahalwari vs Bird's revision.
  • Cornwallis vs John Shore: Cornwallis introduced; Shore designed and urged a trial. Cornwallis was Governor-General of Bengal (the office "of India" began 1833).
  • Revenue farming (Hastings) was not the Permanent Settlement; it preceded it.
  • Percentages (19, 51, 30) are shares of area, from textbooks. 10/11 - 1/11 is the share of the rental in the Permanent Settlement.
  • Bullion: it stopped after the Diwani (1765), not "at Plassey".

30-Second Revision

Permanent Settlement 1793: Cornwallis (Shore); Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Banaras; zamindar = owner; fixed for ever; 10/11 : 1/11; Sunset Law; c. 19%; leads to absentee landlordism. Ryotwari: Read, Munro (Madras 1820); Madras, Bombay (later Sindh, Berar, Assam); ryot with patta; revised every 20-30 years; high rate (c. 50%); debt trap; c. 51%. Mahalwari: Holt Mackenzie 1822, Bird 1833; NW Provinces, Central India, Punjab; mahal through lambardar; temporary; 66% to 50% (1855); c. 30%. Commercialisation: cash revenue, cash crops (cotton, jute, sugarcane), Industrial Revolution, dependence on merchants. Commercial phases: 1600-1800 commercial capitalism (bullion for goods; after 1765 Indian revenue buys goods: "investment"; monopoly, low prices); 1800-60 industrial (blank); 1860-1947 finance.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2024 - General Studies Paper I

With reference to revenue collection by Cornwallis, consider the following statements:
1. Under the Ryotwari Settlement of revenue collection, the peasants were exempted from revenue payment in case of bad harvests or natural calamities.
2. Under the Permanent Settlement in Bengal, if the Zamindar failed to pay his revenues to the state, on or before the fixed date, he would be removed from his Zamindari.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Correct answer: (b) 2 only. Statement 1 is wrong: Ryotwari gave only occasional remissions and no blanket exemption (and Ryotwari was not Cornwallis's system; see c07). Statement 2 is right: the Sunset Law meant the zamindari was forfeited and auctioned if dues were unpaid on time (c05, features). Sources: Testbook (says it was asked in UPSC CSE Prelims 2024) and Vajiram and Ravi.

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2012 - General Studies Paper I (Q37)

With reference to Ryotwari Settlement, consider the following statements:
1. The rent was paid directly by the peasants to the Government.
2. The Government gave Pattas to the Ryots.
3. The lands were surveyed and assessed before being taxed.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 1 and 2 only
  3. 1, 2 and 3
  4. None
Correct answer: (c) 1, 2 and 3 (option lettering as printed on the cited page; the answer is "all three"). The ryot paid the state directly, held a patta, and holdings were measured and assessed (section 1.3, c06, c07). Source: deepmentor, Prelims 2012 Q37; Superkalam also lists it as a 2012 question.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2024 - History (Optional), Paper II

"The pace of commercialisation of agriculture increased as a result of British revenue policies in India." Critically examine.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): define commercialisation; show how cash revenue and the three systems pushed cash crops (section 1.6, section 2); balance with other drivers - Industrial Revolution demand, railways, the American Civil War cotton boom, moneylenders; mention that commercial crops pre-dated the British (c12); close with the forced-vs-market debate. Source for the question: ExamDay (lists it as UPSC Mains 2024 History Paper II, 20 marks) and the 2023-24 list on LotusArise.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2024 - History (Optional), Paper II

"Do you agree that the idea of permanent fixation of land revenue of 1793 in Bengal was highly influenced by the Physiocratic school of thinking? Discuss."

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): explain the Physiocrats (land as the source of wealth), Cornwallis's "improving landlord" model and the fixed demand; then give competing explanations - revenue stability, a loyal class, and misreading of zamindars as owners; weigh the evidence (c03, section 1.2). Source for the question: LotusArise PYQ list (year given as 2024).

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2022 - History (Optional), Paper II

"Colonialism had a twisted logic of its own for commercialization. It emerges on analysis to have been often an artificial and forced process." Critically examine.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): explain "artificial and forced" with cash revenue, indigo and opium coercion, regional specialisation and debt; cite the counter-view that peasants responded to prices and that income grew for some; conclude with the colonial purpose (c12, section 2). Source for the question: LotusArise PYQ list (year given as 2022).
