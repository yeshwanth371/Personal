1. National Movement / National Awakening (continued): Factors 5-7

Factor 5. The socio-religious reform movements

  • They criticised prevailing superstitions and inequality in society and attacked the caste system, thereby promoting equality and strengthening democratic values (c01).
  • By promoting equality they helped forge a national identity; over the years Indians gradually realised that they belonged to one country / nation.
  • [Additional] Other links to nationalism: pride in India's past (the Arya Samaj, Theosophists), self-respect against missionary and colonial criticism, and the training of a reform-minded, English-educated leadership. The limits - narrow social base, religious revivalism - are in the 31 October note, c05.

[Correction] "Strengthen democracy". No democracy existed in colonial India in the 1870s-80s; the reformers spread the values on which democracy rests (equality, individual dignity, reason). Also, not every reform body attacked caste equally - the Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samajes and Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj did; the Muslim revivalist bodies were not concerned with caste. Write "most reform movements".

A. Rise of Indian industry and the abolition of cotton import duties

  • In the second half of the 19th century there was a slow beginning of Indian industries and factories. Cowasjee Nanabhoy Davar (1815-73) founded the Bombay Spinning and Weaving Company at Tardeo, Bombay, on 7 July 1854; it began production on 7 February 1856 (Wikipedia) (Map 1, no. 1; c03).
  • In 1855 George Acland and the Bengali financier Babu Bysumber Sen set up the first jute mill at Rishra, on the Hooghly north of Calcutta (Map 1, no. 4; c04).
  • Indian mills began to compete with the industrial goods coming from England. Under pressure from the Lancashire industrialists, the Government abolished the customs duties on British cotton goods entering India - partly by Lytton in 1879, and the rest in March 1882 under Ripon (c05; Map 2).

[Correction] "In 1853 Cowsjee Nanabhai established first cotton mill at Bombay". (a) Name: Cowasjee (Cowaszee) Nanabhoy Davar, a Parsi broker and banker. (b) Date: the company was founded on 7 July 1854 and produced from February 1856 (Wikipedia: Bombay Spinning and Weaving Company; Cowaszee Nanabhoy Davar). "1853" is not supported by the sources opened. (c) "First": it was the first mill in Bombay and is usually called the first successful Indian-owned cotton mill; Wikipedia notes that a mill at Broach (James Landon's) started production in October 1855, and the earlier Fort Gloster mill near Calcutta (c. 1818) had failed [Verification Required]. Write "first cotton mill of Bombay, 1854" to be safe.

[Correction] "Bysumbher Sen and George Acland ... at Rishar". The place is Rishra (Hooghly district, West Bengal). The Acland Mill (1855) was founded by George Acland, an English entrepreneur, with Babu Bysumber Sen (Wikipedia); Banglapedia names the partner Shyamsunder Sen - the sources differ on the name. The mill burnt down in 1858 and was refounded in 1862. Its machinery and overseer came from Dundee (Scotland), the rival jute centre.

[Correction] Abolition of customs duties. (a) Sequence: in 1879 Lytton remitted the import duties on the coarser cotton goods, overruling the majority of his own Council - Hansard (1 May 1879) records Sir George Campbell's protest at this; a Commons motion of 4 April 1879 (Mr. Briggs) called the cotton duty "unjust alike to the Indian consumer and the English producer". The remaining import duties (except on salt and liquors) were abolished in March 1882 by Ripon's Finance Member Evelyn Baring (R. C. Dutt). (b) Cause: Lancashire pressure (a depressed cotton trade) plus the free-trade doctrine of the time; Indian competition was real but mostly in coarse goods. Lytton did this in a year of famine, war and deficit, which is why nationalists saw it as sacrificing Indian revenue to British interests. (c) [Additional] Duties returned in 1894, and an equal excise on Indian cotton cloth (1896) followed - a lasting nationalist grievance [Verification Required] for the 1896 date.

B. Royal Titles Act, 1876 and the Delhi Durbar, 1877

  • The British Parliament passed the Royal Titles Act, 1876, giving Queen Victoria (reigned 1837-1901) the title Empress of India, rendered as Kaisar-i-Hind.
  • Pomp and show: the title was proclaimed at the Delhi Durbar (the "Imperial Assemblage") on 1 January 1877 in Delhi (Map 1, no. 5). The Library of Congress gives about 68,000 people and 15,000 troops, with 63 princes and dignitaries over 14 days (c06).
  • Though (contrast with the famine): the Durbar was held while the Great Famine of 1876-78 devastated southern and western India. In 1878 Lytton constituted a commission headed by Sir Richard Strachey - the (First) Famine Commission, or Strachey Commission (c07).

Recommendations of the Strachey Commission (report 1880):

  1. [Additional] (the notes left point 1 blank) Famine relief to be treated as a duty of the state, organised by Famine Codes: relief works for the able-bodied at wages enough for subsistence, gratuitous relief for those unable to work, suspension and remission of land revenue (Rau's IAS); provisional Famine Codes followed in 1883.
  2. Construction and railway projects to be undertaken - protective railways and irrigation works.
  3. [Additional] A Famine Relief and Insurance Fund (GKToday: Rs 1.5 crore a year) [Verification Required]; cost of relief to be borne mainly by the provinces, with central help when necessary.

[Correction] Royal Titles Act. The notes are right: an Act of the British Parliament (Disraeli's government), 1876. Precision: the Act is of 1876, the proclamation of 1 January 1877. The Persian/Urdu form used was Qaisar-i-Hind (Library of Congress); "Kaiser-i-Hind" is the usual exam spelling. The Durbar's expense during famine is what made it a nationalist grievance.

[Correction] Famine Commission: who and when. The blank in the notes is Lytton. The commission is often dated 1880 (the year of its report); the sources opened give 1878-80 (GKToday) and "In 1880, Lytton appointed" (Rau's IAS). Write "Strachey Famine Commission, 1878-80". The earlier Campbell Commission (1866) followed the Orissa famine; later ones were Lyall (1897-98) and MacDonnell (1901) [Additional].

C. Maximum age for the civil services reduced to 19

  • The maximum age for the Indian Civil Service (ICS) open examination, held only in London, was cut from 21 to 19, making it still harder for Indians to compete.
  • The Indian Association (founded 26 July 1876, Calcutta) made this its first all-India campaign, demanding a higher age limit and simultaneous examinations in India and England; Surendranath Banerjea toured northern India from 26 May 1877 with the petition (India Today) (c08).
  • [Additional] Lytton also set up the Statutory Civil Service (1879), by which a few posts (one-sixth of covenanted posts, per GKToday) were filled by nominated Indians of "good family" [Verification Required].

[Correction] "Which led to formation of Indian Association". The Indian Association was formed in July 1876 for broader aims (31 October note, section 3.5); the age reduction did not cause its formation but became its first national agitation (1877). The cut was proposed by Lord Salisbury, Secretary of State for India, and debated in the Commons on 28 June 1877 (UnlockIAS citing Hansard), during Lytton's viceroyalty. Sources date it 1876 or 1877. PIB notes that a Public Service Commission (appointed after 1885) recommended raising the limit again.

D. Arms Act, 1878

  • It made it an offence for Indians to keep, carry or trade in arms without a licence.
  • [Additional] Europeans and Anglo-Indians (and some officials) were exempted, so the Act was openly racial (GKToday; IASPOINT) (c09).

[Correction] Arms Act. The notes omit the point that made it a grievance: it was discriminatory, not merely a licensing law. It is the Indian Arms Act (Act XI of 1878) [Verification Required] for the Act number. Its repeal became a standing Moderate demand.

E. Vernacular Press Act, 1878

  • Officially "An Act for the better control of publications in Oriental languages", passed by the Viceroy's Council on 14 March 1878 (Wikipedia); popularly the Gagging Act.
  • It applied only to Indian-language newspapers, not English ones. A paper could be made to give a bond; after a warning, its press, paper and security could be seized; courts could not review such action - the only appeal was to the Governor-General in Council (Britannica).
  • It hit the vernacular press of Bengal hardest. The Amrita Bazar Patrika escaped it by turning from Bengali into an English paper overnight (Britannica). Proceedings were brought against Som Prakash, Bharat Mihir, Dacca Prakash and Samachar (Wikipedia) (c10).

[Correction] "Primarily targeted amrit bharat publication". The paper is the Amrita Bazar Patrika (Sisir Kumar Ghosh and brothers, 1868). Strictly, the Act targeted the whole vernacular press (it was prompted by criticism of the famine, the Durbar and the Afghan War); the Patrika is famous for evading it, not for being its only target. Repeal: by Ripon; Britannica and Wikipedia say 1881, Indian exam books usually say 1882 - sources differ; write "repealed by Ripon (1881-82)".

Factor 7. The Ilbert Bill controversy (1883-84)

  • [Additional] What: a bill drafted by Sir Courtenay Ilbert, Law Member of the Viceroy's Council, introduced on 9 February 1883 under Ripon (Wikipedia), to let senior Indian district magistrates and sessions judges try European British subjects in criminal cases outside the Presidency towns. Why: Indians such as Behari Lal Gupta were now senior enough to hold such posts. Reaction: a furious "white mutiny" of the Calcutta European business community and the indigo and tea planters (Britannica); a European and Anglo-Indian Defence Association was formed. Outcome: a compromise Act, passed on 25 January 1884 (Britannica), let a European defendant claim a jury of which at least half were Europeans or Americans - so the aim was largely defeated (c11).
  • Consequence: educated Indians saw that equality could not be expected from the Raj and learned the value of organised, all-India agitation; Britannica calls the controversy a prelude to the Congress (1885).

Class question: "The Ilbert Bill was by no means an isolated expression of white racism." Comment.

Answer as given in class (cleaned up):

  • British administration rested on racial discrimination; the British ruled believing in their racial superiority and a "civilising mission" - later summed up as the "White Man's Burden" (c11).
  • This racism was not seen only in the Ilbert Bill case; there were numerous instances of racial prejudice against Indians.
  • From the beginning of the Company's rule there was discrimination in the employment of Indians: no Indian could rise above the rank of daroga in the police or subedar in the army (c12).

[Additional] Completing the answer (structure for 150-250 words): (1) Introduction: the Ilbert Bill (1883) exposed European opposition to Indian judges trying Europeans; it was one link in a long chain. (2) Administration: Cornwallis reserved the higher civil posts for Europeans; Indians were kept to subordinate ranks (daroga, subedar). The Charter Act of 1833 and the Queen's Proclamation of 1858 promised that race would be no bar, but the ICS examination was held only in London and the age limit was cut to 19 (1876-77). (3) Law: Europeans in the mofussil could be tried only by European magistrates (exempted from Indian magistrates in 1873 - Britannica); the Arms Act (1878) exempted Europeans; the Vernacular Press Act (1878) spared the English press. (4) Society and economy: separate clubs and railway compartments, the indigo planters' impunity, and the Anglo-Indian press's abuse of Indians during the Ilbert agitation. (5) Conclusion: racism was structural, not occasional; the Indian response - the National Conference (1883) and the Congress (1885) - grew from this experience.

[Correction] "Illbert bill"; "whitemen birds theory". (a) The spelling is Ilbert, after Sir Courtenay Peregrine Ilbert. (b) The phrase meant is "White Man's Burden" - the title of Rudyard Kipling's poem of 1899, so it is a later label for the attitude of 1883 [Verification Required] for the year against a literary source; in the answer, describe it as "the belief in racial superiority / civilising mission, later called the White Man's Burden". (c) The bill was not withdrawn; it was passed in diluted form on 25 January 1884 (Britannica), a common exam trap.

[Correction] "No Indian could rise above the daroga and subedar in police". The two ranks belong to different services: daroga (head of a police station, under Cornwallis's police system) in the police, and subedar (the highest Indian rank in a sepoy company) in the army. Indians could not hold the King's commission until the 20th century. Treat the exact rank ceilings as the textbook view [Verification Required].

2. Ripon's Measures and the Factory Acts (marked important in class)

2.1 First Factory Act, 1881 (Ripon)

  • The Indian Factories Act, 1881 (Act XV of 1881) received assent on 15 March 1881 and came into force on 1 July 1881 for the whole of British India (text of the Act).
  • It applied to factories using mechanical power and employing a minimum of 100 workers (c13).
  • It prohibited the employment of children below 7 years.
  • Children of 7-12 years were not to be employed for more than 9 hours a day, and were to get 4 holidays a month (and a daily rest interval).
  • Dangerous machinery was to be properly fenced (c13).
  • Inspectors were to be appointed to supervise factories (sections 3-4 of the Act).

2.2 Other measures of Ripon

  • He repealed the Vernacular Press Act (1881-82; c10).
  • He started the first regular (synchronous) census of India in 1881 (Census Commissioner W. C. Plowden), although a beginning had been made in 1872 under Lord Mayo (c14).
  • [Additional] The Resolution on Local Self-Government (1882) - hence "father of local self-government" - and the Hunter Education Commission (1882); the abolition of the remaining import duties (March 1882); the Ilbert Bill (1883) [Verification Required] for the 1882 dates.

[Correction] Factory Act, 1881. (a) "Applicable on Indian factories only": it applied to all factories in British India (whatever the owner) that used mechanical power and employed 100 or more people; plantations (tea, coffee, indigo) were outside it. (b) "Dangerous factories / mines should be fenced": the Act required dangerous machinery to be fenced; mines were not covered. (c) It set no limit for adult men or women and said nothing on wages or unions (2017 PYQ below). (d) [Additional] Pressure came both from Lancashire manufacturers (who saw cheap Indian labour as unfair competition) and from reformers such as Sorabjee Shapoorjee Bengallee and N. M. Lokhande; nationalists of the time were divided on it.

[Correction] Census: "begun in 1872 by Lord ___". The blank is Lord Mayo; the 1872 count (taken between 1867 and 1872) was non-synchronous - regions were counted at different times. 1881 was the first synchronous census and began the unbroken decennial series (Anantam IAS; LTP). "First regular census" in the notes is right in that sense.

2.3 Second Factory Act, 1891 (Lansdowne)

  • Passed under Lord Lansdowne, after the Factory Commission of 1890; it applied to factories with 50 or more workers (GKToday).
  • It prohibited the employment of children below 9 years.
  • Children of 9-14 years were not to be employed for more than 7 hours a day (c15).
  • All workers got a weekly holiday.
  • Working hours for women were fixed at 11 hours a day (with a 1.5-hour interval); hours for men were left unregulated.

4. The Moderate Phase (1885-1905)

  • Congress politics during the first 20 years of its history is called the Moderate phase; its leaders believed in liberalism.
  • They were called "Moderates" to distinguish them from the more assertive nationalists of the early 20th century, who were called "Extremists". The Moderates believed in gradualism and constitutionalism (c20).
  • Their methods of demanding reforms and concessions have been dubbed the 3 Ps: petitions, prayers and propaganda (also given as "prayers, petitions and protests").
  • The Congress was hardly a full-fledged political party; it met once a year, and it was anglicised in nature.
  • The Moderates were successful people in their personal lives (lawyers, journalists, teachers, businessmen) and had little commitment to full-time politics.
  • They represented the elite, educated upper class of Indians (c20).
  • To them British rule was an act of divine providence, destined to modernise India.
  • They wanted British rule to continue until Indians were trained in the art of self-government.

[Correction] Wording in the rough notes. (a) "Who were referred to as extremists they believed in gradualism and constitutionalism" reads as if the Extremists were gradualists; the intended meaning is that the Moderates believed in gradualism and constitutionalism. (b) "They rep athority class of indian" is read as "they represented the elite / upper class of Indians" - the point Dufferin made in 1888 by calling them a "microscopic minority" [Verification Required] for the teacher's exact word. (c) "Act of providence of the god which was determined in modernisation of India" = British rule was a providential dispensation meant to modernise India - a view expressed by leaders such as Ranade and Gokhale.

4.1 Demands of the Moderates

The notes list four heads: 1. Constitutional, 2. Administrative, 3. Military, 4. Economic.

1. Constitutional

  1. Self-rule / dominion status (c21).
  2. Abolition of the India Council (the Secretary of State's council in London).
  3. Elections to be introduced for the legislatures (elected Indian members in the legislative councils).
  4. Two Indian members in the Viceroy's Executive Council [Verification Required].
  5. The budget to be referred to the legislature, with discussion and voting ("no taxation without representation").

2. Administrative

  1. Separation of the executive from the judiciary.
  2. The rough notes end at "b." [Additional] The usual list continues: Indianisation of the services through simultaneous ICS examinations in India and England and a higher age limit; repeal of the Arms Act; extension of trial by jury; more spending on education, health, sanitation and irrigation; protection of Indian workers abroad.

3. Military [Additional]

  • Reduction of military expenditure; Indians to be admitted to commissioned ranks; the Indian army not to be used, at India's cost, in Britain's wars outside India.

4. Economic [Additional]

  • An end to the drain of wealth (Naoroji, R. C. Dutt); reduction of land revenue and extension of the Permanent Settlement; abolition of the salt tax; protection of Indian industries (against the cotton-duty policy of section 1, A); agricultural banks for peasants.

The [Additional] items follow standard lists (Vajiram & Ravi; Wikipedia, History of the Indian National Congress); check them against the next class.

[Correction] "Self-rule / dominion status" and the order of demands. (a) In the early years the central constitutional demand was the expansion and reform of the legislative councils with elected Indians (partly met by the Indian Councils Act, 1892). The demand for self-government on the colonial model (like Canada and Australia) came at the end of the phase - Gokhale (Benares, 1905) and Naoroji's "Swaraj" (Calcutta, 1906). (b) "Dominion status" is a later term (formally from 1907; a Congress goal in the 1920s); use "self-government within the Empire" for the Moderates. (c) "Two Indian members in the Executive Council" could not be confirmed; the first Indian member was S. P. Sinha (1909) under the Morley-Minto reforms [Verification Required].

  • Ilbert Bill: introduced 9 Feb 1883 (Ripon; Law Member C. P. Ilbert); diluted Act 25 Jan 1884 - jury half European.
  • Factory Act 1881 (Act XV, Ripon): 100+ workers, min age 7, 7-12 yrs max 9 hrs, 4 holidays/month, fencing, inspectors. Factory Act 1891 (Lansdowne): 50+ workers, min age 9, 9-14 yrs max 7 hrs, weekly holiday, women 11 hrs.
  • Census: 1872 Mayo (non-synchronous); 1881 first synchronous (Ripon; Plowden).
  • INC: 28 Dec 1885, Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay, 72 delegates, Hume, W. C. Bonnerjee. 1886 Calcutta Naoroji; 1887 Madras Tyabji; 1888 Allahabad Yule; 1889 Bombay Wedderburn.
  • Safety valve - Wedderburn (1913), Lajpat Rai (1916); lightning conductor - Bipan Chandra.

Mains Points

  • Lytton as catalyst: his measures were racial (Arms Act, ICS age), economic (cotton duties), repressive (VPA) and insensitive (Durbar in famine); they united grievances across provinces and gave the Indian Association its first national campaigns (Figure 2).
  • Racism as structure: use the Ilbert Bill as the climax of a chain - employment ceilings, ICS rules, legal privilege, Arms Act, VPA (class answer).
  • Labour laws and imperial interest: the Factory Acts were humanitarian in form but pushed by Lancashire; compare with the one-way free trade of the cotton duties (c05, c13).
  • Origin of the Congress: evaluate safety valve vs lightning conductor; stress the Indian pre-history (associations, 1883 conference) (table).
  • Moderates: methods (3 Ps), demands (councils, services, military, economic drain), achievements (Indian Councils Act 1892, economic critique, political education) and limits (narrow base, faith in British justice) - see the 2021 Mains PYQ.

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Lytton - Viceroy 1876-80. Ripon - Viceroy 1880-84, liberal reformer. Dufferin - Viceroy 1884-88. Lansdowne - Viceroy 1888-94. Elgin II - 1894-99. Curzon - 1899-1905. Mayo - Viceroy 1869-72 (1872 census). Salisbury - Secretary of State who cut the ICS age.
  • Cowasjee Nanabhoy Davar - Bombay's first cotton mill. George Acland, Bysumber Sen - Rishra jute mill. Lancashire - English cotton county (Manchester). Evelyn Baring - Finance Member who abolished import duties (1882).
  • Royal Titles Act - 1876 Act making Victoria Empress of India. Kaisar-i-Hind - "Emperor/Empress of India". Imperial Assemblage - the 1877 Delhi Durbar. Richard Strachey - head of the 1878-80 Famine Commission. Famine Code - rules for famine relief (1883).
  • Gagging Act - Vernacular Press Act. Amrita Bazar Patrika - Ghosh brothers' paper that turned English. Statutory Civil Service - 1879 nominated Indian posts.
  • Courtenay Ilbert - Law Member, Ilbert Bill. Behari Lal Gupta - Indian ICS judge whose case prompted it. Daroga - police station officer. Subedar - senior Indian rank in a sepoy company. White Man's Burden - Kipling's phrase (1899) for imperial racial paternalism.
  • W. C. Plowden - Census Commissioner, 1881. N. M. Lokhande - labour pioneer, Bombay Mill Hands' Association (1890). Sorabjee Shapoorjee Bengallee - factory-law reformer.
  • A. O. Hume - retired ICS, Congress founder. W. C. Bonnerjee - first Congress president. Dadabhai Naoroji, Badruddin Tyabji, George Yule, William Wedderburn - presidents 1886-89. Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College - venue of 1885. Lala Lajpat Rai - Extremist; safety-valve critic. R. P. Dutt - Marxist author of India Today. Bipan Chandra - historian; lightning-conductor thesis.
  • Moderates / Extremists - the two early Congress tendencies. 3 Ps - petitions, prayers, propaganda (protests). India Council - Secretary of State's council in London. Dominion status - later term for self-government within the Empire. Drain of wealth - Naoroji's theory of economic loss to Britain.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Royal Titles Act 1876 vs proclamation / Durbar 1 Jan 1877 (c06).
  • Vernacular Press Act (Lytton, 1878) vs its repeal (Ripon, 1881-82); the Patrika evaded it by switching to English (c10).
  • The Ilbert Bill was passed (diluted, 1884), not withdrawn (c11).
  • The Indian Association was not created by the ICS age issue; it agitated on it (c08).
  • 1881 Act: 100 workers, 7 / 7-12 / 9 hrs; 1891 Act: 50 workers, 9 / 9-14 / 7 hrs; neither fixed wages or allowed unions (c15).
  • Census 1872 (Mayo, non-synchronous) vs 1881 (Ripon, synchronous) (c14).
  • 1889 Congress = Bombay (Wedderburn); 1910 = Allahabad (Wedderburn again) (c17).
  • Daroga = police; subedar = army (c12).
  • Moderate phase from 1885, not 1888; "dominion status" is a later term (c19, c21).
  • Strachey Famine Commission (Richard Strachey) vs John Strachey (Lytton's Finance Member) - different brothers [Verification Required].

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Question wording and answer keys were taken from the cited pages; check the official UPSC key before relying on any answer. No question was invented.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 (GS Paper 1)

The Ilbert Bill controversy was related to the

  1. imposition of certain restrictions to carry arms by the Indians
  2. imposition of restrictions on newspapers and magazines published in Indian languages
  3. removal of disqualifications imposed on the Indian magistrates with regard to the trial of the Europeans
  4. removal of a duty on imported cotton cloth
Answer: (c). The bill would let Indian magistrates and judges try Europeans (factor 7). The distractors are the other Lytton-Ripon measures of today's class: (a) Arms Act 1878 (1-D), (b) Vernacular Press Act 1878 (1-E), (d) cotton duties 1879-82 (1-A). Source: PMF IAS: Ilbert Bill, PYQ 2013.

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017 (GS Paper 1)

Consider the following statements:
1. The Factories Act, 1881 was passed with a view to fix the wages of industrial workers and to allow the workers to form trade unions.
2. N.M. Lokhande was a pioneer in organizing the labour movement in British India.
Which of the above statements is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b) 2 only. The 1881 Act dealt mainly with child labour, fencing and inspection, not wages or unions (2.1, c13); Lokhande founded the Bombay Mill Hands' Association (1890). Source: PMF IAS: Factories Act 1881, PYQ 2017.

3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2011 (GS Paper 1)

What was the purpose with which Sir William Wedderburn and W.S. Caine had set up the Indian Parliamentary Committee in 1893?

  1. To agitate for Indian political reforms in the House of Commons
  2. To campaign for the entry of Indians into the Imperial Judiciary
  3. To facilitate a discussion on India's Independence in the British Parliament
  4. To agitate for the entry of eminent Indians into the British Parliament
Answer: (a). Wedderburn (Congress president 1889; sessions table) worked through British Parliament - the Moderates' "propaganda in England" method (section 4). Source: PMF IAS: Indian Parliamentary Committee, PYQ 2011 (note: that page wrongly places Wedderburn's 1889 session at Allahabad; see c17).

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2021 (GS Paper 1, 15 marks)

To what extent did the role of the moderates prepare a base for the wider freedom movement? Comment.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official marking scheme): introduce the Moderate phase (1885-1905) and its leaders; base prepared - an all-India platform (INC), political education and the 3 Ps, the economic critique (drain of wealth), civil-liberties campaigns (against the VPA and Arms Act), Indianisation demands, the Indian Councils Act 1892; limits - narrow elite base, faith in British justice, no mass mobilisation (section 4, table); conclude that they laid the foundation that Extremists and Gandhi widened. Source: GKToday: Mains 2021 question.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2021 (GS Paper 1, 10 marks)

Trace the rise and growth of socio-religious reform movements with special reference to Young Bengal and Brahmo Samaj.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance): causes (Western education, missionary criticism, social evils); Young Bengal (Derozio, Hindu College, radical rationalism); Brahmo Samaj (Rammohun Roy 1828, Debendranath Tagore, Keshub Chandra Sen, splits); conclude with their contribution to national awakening (factor 5; 29 October note). Source: SuperKalam: Mains 2021 question.

In 1877 the entrance age to ICS was reduced from 21 to ..............
