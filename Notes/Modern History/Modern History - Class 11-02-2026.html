1. Demands of the Moderates (continued)

Numbering continues from the 1 November note: 1. Constitutional (done) - 2. Administrative - 3. Military - 4. Economic.

2. Administrative

  1. Separation of the executive from the judiciary - the district collector (executive) also acted as magistrate, so the same officer arrested, prosecuted and tried. [Additional] The demand survives as a Directive Principle: Article 50 of the Constitution of India.
  2. Indianisation of the higher services - more Indians in the covenanted civil service (ICS), the police, forests, medical and engineering services. The Moderates gave an economic reason (European salaries and pensions were remitted abroad - part of the drain, see 4) and a political one (an Indian bureaucracy would be more responsive to Indians).
  3. Increase in the maximum age for the ICS examination - Lytton had cut it to 19 in 1876-78 (1 November note), shutting out most Indians, who had to finish a degree and travel to London.
  4. Conducting the ICS examination in India - i.e. simultaneous examinations in England and India.

Charles Aitchison Commission (Public Service Commission), 1886-87 - who: appointed by Viceroy Lord Dufferin in 1886, chaired by Sir Charles Umpherston Aitchison, Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab; why: to examine how Indians could be given higher employment in the public services; report: 1887.

  • Recommended raising the maximum age from 19 to 23 (as in the rough notes).
  • [Additional] Replaced the covenanted/uncovenanted division with three tiers - Imperial (recruited in London), Provincial (recruited in each province) and Subordinate services; abolished Lytton's Statutory Civil Service (1879); rejected simultaneous examinations - so demand (d) was refused.
  • The recommendations were implemented from 1892 (c01).
  • [Additional] In 1893 the House of Commons passed a resolution in favour of simultaneous examinations, but it was not implemented; the Secretary of State (Kimberley) held that an adequate number of Europeans must always be in the service. Simultaneous examinations came only after the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms - the ICS examination was held in India from 1922 [Verification Required].

[Correction] "Implemented from 1892 (Indian Council Act 1892)". The Aitchison recommendations (age 23, three-tier services, end of the Statutory Civil Service) were implemented in 1892 by executive decision of the Government of India and the Secretary of State. The Indian Councils Act, 1892 is a different measure of the same year: it enlarged the legislative councils and allowed discussion of the budget (the Moderates' constitutional demand, 1 November note). Do not link the age change to the Councils Act. Note also that the Commission was appointed in 1886 but reported in 1887.

3. Military

  • Reduction in defence (military) expenditure, which took the largest share of Indian revenue.
  • Appointment of Indians to commissioned ranks - Indians could then rise only to "Viceroy's commissioned" ranks (subedar, risaldar) below the most junior British officer.
  • [Additional] The Moderates also objected to the Indian army being used, at India's cost, in wars beyond India's frontiers (Afghanistan, Burma, Africa, China).

4. Economic - the economic critique of colonialism

The most important contribution of the Moderates was their economic critique of colonialism. Through articles, journals and books they showed that British rule, far from being a blessing, was the main cause of Indian poverty.

The dominant themes: poverty, drain of wealth and deindustrialisation

  • One-way free trade: Britain imposed free trade on India (no protective duties on British manufactures, see the cotton duties in the 1 November note) while India's own goods had no protection. India was converted from an exporter of finished goods (textiles) into an exporter of raw materials (cotton, jute, indigo, food grains) and an importer of manufactured goods.
  • India was thus reduced to a dependent agrarian economy and a field for British capital investment (railways, plantations, mines).
  • Indian capital, not foreign capital: they argued that India should develop through Indian capital, because profits on British investment were sent home - another channel of the drain.
  • The absence of protective tariffs had destroyed Indian industries (deindustrialisation).
  • [Additional] Channels of the drain (Naoroji): Home Charges (interest on debt, pensions, India Office costs), salaries and savings of British officials remitted home, profits of foreign capital, and the export surplus that brought no return. Naoroji put the drain at roughly £12 million a year in his early estimates [figures varied across his writings].

[Additional] Why this mattered. The critique turned nationalism from a demand for jobs and councils into a case against the economic basis of colonial rule, using the government's own statistics. Because the Moderates wrote for the educated public, it reached the masses only later - through the Extremists, who drew the political conclusion (Swadeshi and boycott). Contrast: the colonial view held that India's poverty came from over-population and backward agriculture, and that railways and law and order were benefits; later scholarship debates the size of the drain but accepts that Home Charges and the export surplus were real transfers.

2. The Extremist Phase (1905-1918)

  • Towards the end of the 19th century a reaction set in within the Congress against the failure of the Moderates. This rising trend is known as Extremism (also called militant or assertive nationalism; its leaders called themselves "Nationalists").
  • Contrary to the Moderates, the Extremists had faith in the capacity of the masses and wanted to involve them in the national struggle.
  • They rejected the Moderates' methods (constitutional agitation - prayers, petitions, protests) and supported the doctrine of passive resistance: boycott of foreign goods, government schools, courts and offices; Swadeshi; national education. [Additional] Aurobindo Ghose set this out in a series of articles, The Doctrine of Passive Resistance (in Bande Mataram, 1907).
  • [Additional] Leaders: Lal-Bal-Pal - Lala Lajpat Rai (Punjab), Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Maharashtra), Bipin Chandra Pal (Bengal) - and Aurobindo Ghose. Goal: Swaraj; Tilak: "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it" (popularised 1916).

2.1 Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1856-1920)

  • Born 23 July 1856 in Ratnagiri district, Maharashtra (Bombay Presidency) - Map 1, place 1.
  • Made Poona (Pune) his political base.
  • A gifted writer who popularised nationalism through his newspapers Mahratta (English) and Kesari (Marathi), both founded in 1881 with G. G. Agarkar and Vishnushastri Chiplunkar; Tilak took them over in 1888 (c05).
  • An eloquent speaker who popularised the public Ganapati festival (1893) and the Shivaji festival (1896) among the youth (c05).
  • Went to jail several times and set the trend of courting imprisonment for later nationalists. [Additional] 1882 (defamation, the Kolhapur case, with Agarkar) [Verification Required]; 1897 - 18 months' rigorous imprisonment for sedition (Section 124A) after the Rand murder (factor 3); 1908 - six years in Mandalay (Burma), which set off a six-day political strike of Bombay mill workers.
  • [Additional] Also: the anti-excise campaign of 1896 (boycott of foreign cloth in Maharashtra); Gita Rahasya and The Arctic Home in the Vedas; Home Rule League (April 1916); called the "Father of Indian Unrest" by Valentine Chirol; died 1 August 1920.

[Additional] Interpretations. Nationalist writers see the festivals as a way to take politics beyond the English-educated to ordinary people. Critics - then and later - note that the use of Hindu religious symbols (and Tilak's opposition to the Age of Consent Act, 1891) narrowed the movement's appeal to Muslims and social reformers. Both views appear in standard texts; neither should be written as settled fact.

[Correction] Tilak's papers and festivals. (a) The names are Mahratta (English weekly) and Kesari (Marathi), both started in 1881 by a group that included Tilak, Agarkar and Chiplunkar. (b) The public Ganapati festival dates from 1893 (as noted). The Shivaji festival is usually dated 1896; Tilak's campaign for the Shivaji memorial at Raigad began in 1895, which explains the "1895" in the notes. (c) "Fects" = festivals.

3. Factors Leading to the Rise of Extremism

1 True nature of British rule exposed+2 Dissatisfaction with Moderates+3 Famines and plague+4 Reaction against Westernisation+5 International events+6 Curzon's policies→Extremism → Partition of Bengal (1905) as the immediate trigger → Swadeshi

Figure 2 - Factors 1-5 are long-term causes; factor 6, ending in the partition of Bengal, supplied the immediate cause. [Additional]

Factor 1. Recognition of the true nature of British rule

  • The Moderates, through their writings, had exposed British rule before the public:
    • Dadabhai Naoroji - Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901).
    • M. G. Ranade - his articles were compiled as Essays on Indian Economics (1898) (c02).
    • R. C. Dutt - The Economic History of India in two volumes, 1902 and 1904 (c03).
  • The Moderates took great pains to provide statistical data and empirical evidence of how wealth was drained from India. They were not popular among the masses, but they contributed immensely to exposing the economic drain - and the Extremists drew the conclusion that a regime of this nature would not reform itself.

[Correction] Ranade's book. "Essays on colonialism" is not a title. Ranade's economic essays were collected as Essays on Indian Economics (1898). The year comes from standard references and was not checked against a first edition [Verification Required].

[Correction] R. C. Dutt's two volumes were The Economic History of India under Early British Rule (1902; Plassey to 1837) and The Economic History of India in the Victorian Age (1904; 1837-1900) - not "1902-3".

Factor 2. Dissatisfaction with the achievements of the Moderates

  • The Extremists were unhappy with the slow progress of the Congress in its first 15-20 years; the only constitutional gain was the limited Indian Councils Act, 1892.
  • They criticised the Moderates' methods and dubbed the 3 Ps (prayers, petitions, protests) "political mendicancy" (begging).
  • They wanted the Congress to work throughout the year (not meet only once a year) and to press its demands forcefully.

Factor 3. Worsening economic situation: famines and plague

  • The adverse economic policies of the British led to more frequent famines in the second half of the 19th century, with a heavy toll on human life.
  • 1876-78 - the Great Famine (Deccan and South), which coincided with Lytton's Delhi Durbar of 1 January 1877.
  • 1896-97 - famine, which coincided with bubonic plague (Bombay from 1896; Poona in 1897); the government's relief was seen as lukewarm (c06).
  • 1899-1900 - famine at the start of Curzon's viceroyalty (Bombay Presidency, Central Provinces, Rajputana, Hyderabad).
  • Poona plague: a Special Plague Committee under Walter Charles Rand, ICS, was set up to fight the epidemic; soldiers entered houses forcibly, examined occupants (including women) in public, removed people to camps and damaged property - which caused deep anger. Tilak attacked the measures in Kesari and Mahratta.
  • The Chapekar brothers - Damodar Hari and Balkrishna Hari - shot Rand and his escort Lt. Charles Egerton Ayerst on 22 June 1897 (the night of Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee celebrations); Ayerst died on the spot and Rand on 3 July 1897 (c07).

[Correction] Famine years. "1896-99" joins two famines: 1896-97 and 1899-1900. Bubonic plague reached Bombay in 1896 and Poona in early 1897; it was the plague (not the famine) that brought Rand's committee to Poona. "Cold lukewarm response" is read as the government's inadequate relief response.

[Correction] Rand and the "plague commission". (a) Rand was appointed plague commissioner of Poona on 19 February 1897 and chaired the Special Plague Committee, acting under the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897. His job was plague control (search, segregation, disinfection) - "relief" in a broad sense only. He did not head a "Plague Commission"; the all-India Indian Plague Commission was a separate inquiry of 1898-99 [Verification Required]. (b) His name was Walter Charles Rand, not "Charles Rand". (c) Damodar shot Rand; Balkrishna fired on Ayerst. Damodar was hanged on 18 April 1898; Balkrishna and the third brother, Vasudeo, were hanged in May 1899 (sources differ on the exact days).

[Additional] Consequence and views. The killing was one of the first political assassinations of British officials in modern India. The government charged Tilak with sedition (1897) for articles in Kesari, which made him a national figure ("Lokmanya"). The colonial account stressed the public-health need for strict measures; nationalist accounts stressed the humiliation of Indians. Both are part of the record.

Factor 4. Reaction against increasing Westernisation

  • The Extremists feared that Westernisation, based on individualism, would dominate Indian culture and that India would lose its distinct cultural identity.
  • The Moderates looked to the West and were inspired by Western liberal thinkers (c04).
  • The Extremists looked within India and were inspired by Swami Dayananda Saraswati, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee and Swami Vivekananda.

[Correction] "Hord lock rousseau". The intended names are probably John Locke and Jean-Jacques Rousseau; "Hord" cannot be identified with confidence - it may stand for another liberal thinker such as J. S. Mill, Edmund Burke or Herbert Spencer, who are the names usually given [Verification Required] - check with the teacher.

Factor 5. Contemporary international influences

  • Nationalist and revolutionary movements in the Ottoman and Russian empires inspired the Extremists. [Additional] Also cited: Ireland, Egypt, Persia, China; the Russian Revolution of 1905.
  • The humiliation of Indians in South Africa (racial laws in Natal and the Transvaal; Gandhi's struggle there from 1893) angered Indians.
  • The defeat of Italy by Ethiopia at Adwa (1 March 1896) and the defeat of Russia by Japan in the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05) exploded the myth of European invincibility (c08).

[Correction] Dates of the international events. The Russo-Japanese War lasted from February 1904 to September 1905 (Port Arthur fell in January 1905, Tsushima on 27-29 May 1905, Treaty of Portsmouth 5 September 1905), so "1905" is the year of victory, not of the whole war. "Repulsion of Italians by Ethiopians (1896)" = the Battle of Adwa, 1 March 1896, which ended the First Italo-Ethiopian War (1895-96). Japan was the first Asian power in modern times to defeat a European power.

Factor 6. The conservative policies of Lord Curzon (Viceroy 1899-1905)

  1. Calcutta Corporation (Municipal) Act, 1899 - Curzon's government amended the law to reduce the elected element in the Calcutta Corporation and its committees, so that official and nominated members controlled it. Indians resented this (c09).
  2. Delhi Durbar - held on 1 January 1903 to celebrate the accession and coronation of Edward VII; its great cost, so soon after the famine of 1899-1900, drew bitter criticism (c10).
  3. In 1902 Curzon appointed the Indian Universities Commission under Sir Thomas Raleigh; on its recommendations he enacted the Indian Universities Act, 1904, which reduced the autonomy of Indian universities (c11).
  4. Indian Official Secrets (Amendment) Act, 1904 - extended the 1889 Act to civil matters and was seen as a curb on the freedom of the press. [Additional] It was opposed by the Indian and Anglo-Indian press alike (Hansard, 24 March 1904).
  5. Partition of Bengal, 1905 - Curzon divided Bengal into two provinces, officially for administrative convenience; nationalists saw it as a move to weaken nationalism in Bengal, its main centre (c12).

[Correction] Calcutta Municipal Act, 1899 - what changed. The rough notes say the nominated members were increased. Before 1899 the corporation had 75 commissioners, 50 of them elected. The Act cut the total to 50 - 25 elected and 25 appointed, gave the government-appointed chairman the executive power and moved real power to a small general committee in which the elected members were a minority. The net effect was an official/nominated majority. 28 elected commissioners resigned in protest (Banglapedia). [Additional] Curzon did not draft the bill but strongly shaped it.

[Correction] Delhi Durbar: date and cost. (a) It was held on 1 January 1903 (celebrations ran from 29 December 1902 for about ten days), not in 1902. (b) It marked the accession (22 January 1901) and coronation (9 August 1902) of Edward VII as King-Emperor. (c) Cost: the Government of India's estimate was £233,000 and its net imperial cost was later stated as £299,000 (Hansard, 16 March 1911) - about Rs 45 lakh at the fixed rate of Rs 15 = £1. The figure "more than 2 million rupees" in the notes understates this; provincial and princely spending was extra.

[Correction] Names and effect of the university reform. The chairman was Sir Thomas Raleigh (not "Relaigh"). [Additional] The 1904 Act increased official control: more nominated fellows in the senates, government power to veto senate regulations and approve affiliations, and stricter conditions for affiliated colleges. Curzon's defence was "efficiency"; nationalists (Gokhale opposed it in the Council) saw an attack on Indian-run higher education.

[Correction] "East and west Bengal". The new provinces were Eastern Bengal and Assam (Dacca, Chittagong and Rajshahi divisions, Hill Tippera, Malda and Assam; capital Dacca) and Bengal (western Bengal with Bihar and Orissa; capital Calcutta). Dates: decision made public December 1903; partition announced 19 July 1905; in force 16 October 1905 (approximate zones in the Map 1 inset); annulled at the Delhi Durbar of 12 December 1911. Interpretations: the official reason was that Bengal (about 78 million people) was too large to administer; nationalists, and most later historians, read it as "divide and rule" - dividing Bengali speakers and creating a Muslim-majority province. Official notes of Home Secretary H. H. Risley ("Bengal united is a power; Bengal divided will pull in several different ways") are often cited for this view [Verification Required].

Curzon gave utmost importance to efficiency in administration and worked tirelessly to revamp the administrative machinery (apparatus). His method was to appoint a commission, then legislate on its report.

5. Significance of the Swadeshi Movement

5.1 Background of the Swadeshi movement [Additional - added on request]

Added after class on 2 November 2026. This part was not in the class notes, so all of it is [Additional].

A. Long-term background

  • The ideas already existed. The Moderates' economic critique (section 1-4) had shown how one-way free trade ruined Indian industry; buying Indian goods had been urged earlier, e.g. Tilak's boycott of foreign cloth against the cotton excise in 1896 (2.1).
  • Constructive groundwork in Bengal before 1905: Satish Chandra Mukherjee's Dawn Society (1902) and journal Dawn; Rabindranath Tagore's Swadeshi Samaj address (1904), a blueprint for village self-help (atmashakti); swadeshi enterprises such as P. C. Ray's Bengal Chemicals (c19).
  • Political frustration: the failure of Moderate methods (factor 2) and Curzon's measures (factor 6) had already angered educated Bengal.

B. Immediate background: the partition of Bengal

  • December 1903 - the proposal to partition Bengal was made public (Home Secretary H. H. Risley's letter of 3 December 1903 is usually cited; exact date [Verification Required]).
  • Official reason: Bengal, with about 78 million people, was too large to administer. Nationalist reading: to weaken Bengal, the nerve centre of nationalism, by dividing Bengali speakers and creating a Muslim-majority province (c12).
  • December 1903 - July 1905: Moderate protest. Press campaigns (Bengalee, Hitabadi, Sanjivani), petitions and memorials (many from Dacca and Mymensingh districts) and big conferences at the Calcutta Town Hall in March 1904 and January 1905 (Sumit Sarkar). They failed to move the government.
  • 13 July 1905 - Krishna Kumar Mitra's weekly Sanjivani first proposed a boycott of British goods.
  • 19 July 1905 - partition announced.
  • 7 August 1905 - at the Calcutta Town Hall the Boycott Resolution was passed and the Swadeshi movement formally proclaimed; established leaders such as Surendranath Banerjea accepted boycott "after considerable hesitation" (Sarkar). Boycott of Manchester cloth and Liverpool salt began.
  • 16 October 1905 - partition came into force and was kept as a day of mourning: hearths were left unlit (arandhan), people bathed in the Ganga, tied rakhis on one another as a sign of unity (the idea of Tagore and Ramendrasundar Trivedi), and sang Amar Sonar Bangla; Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose addressed huge meetings.
  • Government response: the Carlyle Circular (October 1905; dated 10 October in some accounts, published 22 October in Sarkar) threatened to withdraw grants, scholarships and affiliation from institutions whose students picketed - which pushed the movement towards national education.

C. How the movement developed (1905-1908)

  • Congress: the Benares session (1905), president G. K. Gokhale, supported the anti-partition and boycott movement in Bengal; the Calcutta session (1906), president Dadabhai Naoroji, declared Swaraj the goal and passed resolutions on Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott and National Education.
  • Moderates vs Extremists: the Moderates wanted boycott only of British goods, only in Bengal, and only to undo the partition; the Extremists wanted to extend it to schools, courts, titles and government service and across India, as passive resistance for Swaraj - the dispute behind the Surat split (1907).
  • Four trends (Sumit Sarkar): the Moderate trend; constructive swadeshi (self-help, national schools, village work); political Extremism (passive resistance; papers such as Bande Mataram, New India, Sandhya, Yugantar); and revolutionary terrorism.
  • Spread outside Bengal: Tilak in Poona and Bombay; Lala Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh in the Punjab; Syed Haider Raza in Delhi; V. O. Chidambaram Pillai and Subramania Siva at Tuticorin (Madras Presidency).
  • Repression and decline: police broke up the Barisal provincial conference (April 1906); Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh deported (1907); Seditious Meetings Act (1907); Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act (1908); Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908) against samitis; Tilak's six-year sentence (1908); Bengal leaders including Ashwini Kumar Dutt and K. K. Mitra deported (December 1908). With the Surat split and the arrests, the open movement faded by 1908. Exact dates in this list come from standard references, not primary documents [Verification Required].

5.2 Significance of the Swadeshi movement (class notes)

  1. The national movement took a leap forward. A large number of students and women, from urban and rural areas, joined the national movement for the first time; the movement thus made a beginning towards a mass movement, though on a limited scale (largely Bengal; weak among peasants and Muslims).
  2. Boycott was an effective strategy: women stopped using foreign utensils and wearing foreign bangles; cobblers refused to repair foreign shoes (washermen refused to wash foreign clothes; priests refused rites using foreign goods) - i.e. social boycott.
  3. Self-reliance (atmashakti) - restoring pride and confidence among Indians:
    • Education: the National Council of Education was set up on 15 August 1906; the Bengal National College opened with Aurobindo Ghose as principal (c20).
    • Economy: indigenous enterprises - e.g. P. C. Ray's Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works (c19) and V. O. Chidambaram Pillai's Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company at Tuticorin (1906), which challenged the British India Steam Navigation Company on the Tuticorin-Colombo route. Also swadeshi textile mills, soap and match factories, tanneries, banks and insurance companies.
  4. Samitis (volunteer corps): the movement produced many volunteers organised into samitis - e.g. the Swadesh Bandhab Samiti of Ashwini Kumar Dutt in Barisal (c21). Samiti members toured villages, popularised swadeshi songs, gave relief during famine and epidemics, and set up arbitration courts (salishi) to settle disputes outside British courts. [Additional] Other samitis: Dacca Anushilan (Pulin Behari Das), Brati, Sadhana, Suhrid.
  5. Hartals and labour unrest: the period saw many hartals and strikes, caused by price rise and racial discrimination. Two new features emerged: (a) professional agitators (nationalist lawyers and journalists who led workers) and (b) the power of union/organisation. Strikes took place in foreign-owned concerns - the Clive Jute Mills, Burn and Co. (iron works, Howrah) and the Government of India Press (September 1905), and later the East Indian Railway (1906). One of the earliest trade unions, the Printers' Union, was formed in Calcutta in 1905 (c22).

[Correction] Bengal Chemicals was not "opened" by the Swadeshi movement. P. C. Ray began the Bengal Chemical Works in 1892; it became Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works Ltd on 12 April 1901 - before the movement. It is a correct example of swadeshi enterprise and grew during the boycott, but write "expanded" or "was promoted", not "opened". The Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company (V. O. Chidambaram Pillai, Tuticorin, 1906) did arise during the movement.

[Correction] National education dates. The National Council of Education was founded on 15 August 1906 (Rashbehari Ghosh first president; Satish Chandra Mukherjee and Raja Subodh Chandra Mullick prominent; Mullick gave Rs 1 lakh). The Bengal National College is dated 14 or 15 August 1906 in different sources. Aurobindo resigned as principal on 2 August 1907, before his arrest in the Bande Mataram sedition case. "Principle" = principal.

[Correction] "Swadesh bhandavya samitis" = the Swadesh Bandhab Samiti ("society of friends of the motherland") of Barisal, founded by Ashwini Kumar Dutt (1856-1923) in 1905 (6 August 1905 in one source [Verification Required]); it had about 159 branches and was the most rural of the samitis. Dutt was deported in 1908.

[Correction] The Printers' Union and the strikes. (a) Sources agree that the Printers' Union was formed in Calcutta in 1905, out of the Government of India Press strike (from September 1905), with nationalist lawyers such as Aswini Coomar Banerjee; the date "October 1905" in the notes could not be confirmed [Verification Required]. Call it "one of the earliest trade unions", not "one of the oldest" - the Bombay Mill Hands' Association (N. M. Lokhande, 1890) is older. (b) "Burn iron company" = Burn and Co., Howrah; "govt press" = the Government of India Press, Calcutta.

[Additional] Limits of the movement (for balanced answers): swadeshi goods were dearer than foreign goods; the leaders did not take up peasant demands such as rent; the use of Hindu symbols and the government's courting of Muslim landlords kept most Muslim peasants of East Bengal away; repression (Carlyle Circular, 1905; Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1908), the Surat split (1907) and the lack of a single organisation ended it by 1908. Yet it gave the national movement its later methods - boycott, swadeshi, national education, volunteer corps.

5.3 Impact of the Swadeshi movement [Additional - added on request]

Impact of the Swadeshi movement, by field
FieldImpact
PoliticalNationalism moved from petitions to mass action; Swaraj became the Congress goal (Calcutta, 1906); the Moderate-Extremist rift became the Surat split (1907); the movement spread beyond Bengal to Maharashtra, the Punjab, Delhi and Madras.
MethodsIntroduced boycott, swadeshi, passive resistance, picketing, national education, volunteer corps and arbitration courts - methods later taken up and widened by Gandhi (non-cooperation, constructive programme).
EconomicRevival of handloom, silk weaving and artisan crafts; swadeshi textile mills, soap and match factories, tanneries, banks and insurance companies; Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company (1906). Imports of British cloth fell in 1905-08 [Verification Required] for exact figures.
EducationNational Council of Education and Bengal National College (1906); Bengal Technical Institute (1906), whose successor college became Jadavpur University (1955); teaching in the mother tongue; technical education.
CulturalPatriotic songs - Tagore's Amar Sonar Bangla (now Bangladesh's national anthem), songs of Rajanikanta Sen, D. L. Roy and Mukunda Das; "Vande Mataram" as a slogan; Abanindranath Tagore's Bharat Mata and the Bengal School of art; the scientific work of J. C. Bose and P. C. Ray hailed as national achievement.
SocialFirst large-scale participation of students and women; social boycott as a weapon; but little peasant participation and growing distance of Muslims in East Bengal.
LabourFirst politically linked strikes and early trade unions (Printers' Union 1905; railway and jute strikes 1906-07; Tuticorin Coral Mills strike 1908; Bombay strike for Tilak 1908).
RevolutionaryAfter repression some youth turned to revolutionary terrorism - Anushilan Samiti, the Jugantar group, the Muzaffarpur bomb (April 1908) and the Alipore conspiracy case (1908-09).
Communal and constitutionalThe government courted Muslim support for the new province; the All-India Muslim League was founded at Dacca (December 1906); the Simla deputation (October 1906) and separate electorates in the Morley-Minto reforms (1909) followed - read by nationalists as "rally the Moderates, divide the communities".
OutcomeThe partition was annulled at the Delhi Durbar of 12 December 1911; at the same time the capital was moved from Calcutta to Delhi, and Bihar and Orissa and Assam were made separate provinces (from 1912).

[Additional] Assessment. Bipan Chandra treats the movement as a major leap forward: it took nationalism to new social groups and gave it new methods, even though it did not become a peasant movement. Sumit Sarkar's study stresses its limits - a largely upper-caste, Hindu, middle-class base and the failure to reach Muslim peasants. Both readings are standard; use both in a Mains answer.

  • Aitchison Commission (Public Service Commission): appointed 1886 by Dufferin; report 1887; max age 23; three tiers (Imperial, Provincial, Subordinate); Statutory Civil Service abolished; simultaneous exams rejected; implemented 1892.
  • Article 50 - separation of judiciary from executive (Moderates' demand).
  • Economic critics: Naoroji (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901), R. C. Dutt (Economic History of India, 1902 and 1904), Ranade (Essays on Indian Economics), G. Subramania Iyer (The Hindu, Swadesamitran), G. V. Joshi, Gokhale.
  • Tilak: born Ratnagiri 1856; Kesari (Marathi) and Mahratta (English), 1881; Ganapati 1893; Shivaji 1896; jailed 1897 (18 months) and 1908 (Mandalay, 6 years).
  • Rand murder: 22 June 1897, Poona; Chapekar brothers; Rand died 3 July 1897.
  • Adwa 1 March 1896 (Ethiopia beat Italy); Russo-Japanese War 1904-05 (Tsushima May 1905).
  • Curzon (1899-1905): Calcutta Municipal Act 1899; Delhi Durbar 1 Jan 1903; Raleigh Commission 1902 → Indian Universities Act 1904; Official Secrets Act 1904; Ancient Monuments Preservation Act, 18 March 1904; Railway Board 1905; partition of Bengal 16 Oct 1905.
  • Commissions: Famine - MacDonnell (1900-01); Irrigation - Scott-Moncrieff (1901-03; Rs 44 crore); Police - Andrew Fraser (1902-03; CID).
  • Pusa Institute: Phipps gift £30,000; foundation 1 April 1904; work from 1905; to Delhi 1936.

Mains Points

  • Moderates' lasting gain: the economic critique of colonialism undermined the moral claim of British rule and supplied the logic of Swadeshi.
  • Why Extremism rose: combine long-term (economic critique, Moderate failure, famine and plague, cultural revival, international events) and immediate (Curzon, partition) causes; show that Extremism was a continuation, not a rejection, of the Moderates' analysis.
  • Curzon's paradox: administrative efficiency (commissions, irrigation, police, railways, archaeology) combined with political illiberalism (Calcutta Corporation, universities, secrets law, partition) - "efficiency" without consent produced the strongest agitation yet.
  • Swadeshi significance: first mass participation of students and women; atmashakti in education and industry; samitis; labour unrest; cultural creativity - but limited reach among peasants and Muslims.
  • Background and impact of Swadeshi: long-term roots (economic critique, constructive swadeshi) → immediate cause (partition, failure of petitions) → programme → impact by field (impact table) → limits → legacy for the Gandhian movements.
  • Historiography: nationalist (Bipan Chandra) vs Cambridge-school readings of Extremism; colonial vs nationalist views of plague measures and of the partition.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Aitchison Commission (1886, Dufferin) vs Islington Commission (1912) vs Lee Commission (1924) - all on the services; only Lee recommended a Public Service Commission.
  • Age to 23 in 1892 ≠ Indian Councils Act 1892 (c01).
  • Lytton's Durbar 1 Jan 1877 (Victoria, Empress) vs Curzon's 1 Jan 1903 (Edward VII) vs Hardinge's 12 Dec 1911 (George V present; partition annulled; capital to Delhi).
  • Kesari = Marathi; Mahratta = English.
  • Rand headed a Special Plague Committee, not the Indian Plague Commission.
  • Bengal Chemicals (1892/1901) predates Swadeshi; Swadeshi Steam Navigation (1906) belongs to it.
  • Eastern Bengal and Assam capital Dacca; residual Bengal included Bihar and Orissa.
  • Ancient Monuments Preservation Act 1904 vs Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958.
  • Commission ≠ Act: Raleigh Commission 1902 → Universities Act 1904.
  • Extremists were not all "revolutionary terrorists": Tilak and Aurobindo argued for passive resistance; the Chapekars acted independently.

30-Second Revision

Moderates demanded administrative (separate judiciary, Indianisation, age 23, exams in India - Aitchison 1886 gave age 23 in 1892 but refused simultaneous exams), military (less expenditure, Indian officers) and economic reforms; their great legacy was the economic critique (Naoroji, Dutt, Ranade, Iyer, Joshi, Gokhale). Their failure produced Extremism - faith in masses, passive resistance - led by Tilak (Ratnagiri, Poona, Kesari/Mahratta, Ganapati 1893, Shivaji 1896). Causes: exposure of colonial economics, Moderate failure, famines and the Poona plague (Rand, 22 June 1897), cultural revival, Adwa 1896 and Japan 1905, and Curzon (Calcutta 1899, Durbar 1903, Universities Act 1904, Secrets Act 1904, partition 1905) - though Curzon was also an efficient administrator (Famine, Irrigation, Police Commissions; Railway Board; Pusa; Monuments Act). Swadeshi brought students and women in, practised boycott and atmashakti (NCE 1906, Bengal National College, VOC's shipping company), built samitis (Barisal) and saw the first political strikes and unions (Printers' Union 1905).
