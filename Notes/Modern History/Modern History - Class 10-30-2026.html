1.1 Arya Samaj and Dayanand Saraswati (1824-83)

  • Origin (as in the notes): the Arya Samaj emerged as a reaction against Western cultural influences. It was founded by Dayanand Saraswati, born as Mool Shankar in a traditional Brahmin family (c01).
  • Early life: he left home early and wandered as an ascetic; in these years he learnt yoga; he finally met his teacher Swami Virajananda (at Mathura), who taught him the Vedas; after this he established the Arya Samaj in 1875.
  • Born / died: born 1824 at Tankara, Gujarat (Map 1, no. 1); died 30 October 1883 at Ajmer (no. 6) (Britannica; c05). Britannica: he wandered 1845-60 in search of religious truth and then became Virajananda's disciple; the guru took as his fee a promise to spend his life reinstating the Vedic Hinduism of pre-Buddhist India.
  • Founding: the first meeting that established the Arya Samaj was held at Bombay on 10 April 1875 (Britannica; Wikipedia) (Map 1, no. 2). He first drew public attention through a debate with orthodox scholars at Benares, presided over by the maharaja of Benares (Britannica). [Additional] The "ten principles" were finally settled at Lahore in 1877 (Wikipedia) - the basis of the notes' statement that the headquarters moved to Lahore (c02).
  • Works: Satyarth Prakash ("Light of Truth", the Samaj's main book) and the Vedic commentaries; a pamphlet on cow protection (Gokarunanidhi) (c02).
  • Beliefs: the Samaj believed in the infallibility of the Vedas and treated them as a "reservoir of all true knowledge". Notes' judgement: by giving supreme authority to the Vedas, the Samaj gave a secondary status to the human mind (a critical reading; the Samaj itself stressed reason in rejecting later "accretions" - Britannica). [Additional] Britannica: Dayanand claimed that modern technology, such as electricity, could be found in the Vedas; the Samaj also upholds karma and samsara (c03).
  • Criticised: child marriage, idol worship, caste based on birth and priestcraft (Britannica); the notes add polygamy [Verification Required] (c03). It accepted social division based on merit, not birth (Britannica: it opposes "basing caste upon birth rather than upon merit").
  • Women and education: it supported education for girls but, as the notes say, was against co-education [Verification Required]; Britannica: it furthers female education, intercaste marriage and widow remarriage (c04).

[Correction] Name, guru and learning. (a) "Modshankar" is Mool Shankar (Britannica: also "Mula Sankara"); "Dayanand Saraswati" was the name taken at initiation as an ascetic. (b) The teacher is Swami Virajananda (the notes' "Virijanand Saraswati"). Mathura as the place of the meeting is the standard account but is not confirmed in a source opened [Verification Required]. (c) "It was his teacher who taught him the four Vedas": Britannica says he became Virajananda's disciple after 1845-60 of wandering and promised to revive pre-Buddhist Vedic Hinduism; the detail that Virajananda taught the four Vedas is the usual account and is kept, but not independently confirmed [Verification Required]. (d) "After this only ... 1875": the Samaj was founded about 15 years after the end of his wanderings (1860), not immediately.

[Correction] Dates and nature of his works; the move to Lahore. (a) The notes say he "wrote Satyarth Prakash, interpretation of Vedas" in 1875. Satyarth Prakash is an exposition of the Samaj's teaching (and a critique of other faiths), not a commentary on the Vedas; his Vedic commentaries are separate works (the Rigvedadi Bhashya Bhumika, about 1876, [Verification Required]). The first edition of Satyarth Prakash is dated about 1874-75 (a revised edition followed in 1884) [Verification Required]; the notes' 1875 is retained. (b) "Gaukarunanidhi in 1871": the pamphlet is Gokarunanidhi ("Ocean of Mercy to the Cow"), usually dated 1881, with the Cow Protection Society (Gaurakshini Sabha) about 1882 [Verification Required]; 1871 appears to be a slip for 1881 and is not accepted. (c) "In 1877 the HQ shifted to Lahore from Bombay": the Lahore Arya Samaj was set up in 1877 and the principles were finalised there (Wikipedia); that the all-India headquarters moved is not confirmed - the Samaj grew strongest in Punjab, and Britannica says its largest following has always been in western and northern India [Verification Required].

[Correction] Beliefs: the Vedas, "secondary status to the mind", merit and polygamy. (a) "Infallibility of the Vedas" is accurate (Britannica; Wikipedia: the Vedas are "repositories of all of true knowledge"). (b) "Gave secondary status to the human mind" is the class's critical judgement, not a statement of the Samaj: the Samaj itself claimed to use reason in rejecting what it called later "accretions" to the Vedas (Britannica), so it is kept as one interpretation. (c) "Accepted the division of society on merit": Britannica says it opposes caste based on birth rather than merit; the notes' wording is retained and read as varna by merit, not birth. (d) Polygamy: the notes list it among the criticised evils, but Britannica's list (idol worship, animal sacrifice, shraddha, birth-based caste, untouchability, child marriage, pilgrimages, priestly craft, temple offerings) does not mention it [Verification Required]. (e) Against co-education: not confirmed in a source opened [Verification Required].

[Correction] "The British contribution for Arya Samaj in the field of education". The sentence is garbled. The following sentences (girls' education; DAV schools; Gurukul) show that the intended meaning is "the Arya Samaj's contribution in the field of education", and it is read that way here. The British role was only indirect: the "Anglo-Vedic" schools taught English and modern subjects alongside Vedic studies (see section 1.1.1).

[Correction] Dayanand's death. The notes say he was "poisoned to death at Ajmer". Britannica: he died on 30 October 1883 at Ajmer after vigorous public criticism of a princely ruler, "under circumstances suggesting that he might have been poisoned by one of the maharaja's supporters, but the accusation was never proved in court". Wikipedia says "assassinated". Treat poisoning as the traditional account, not a proved fact. The detail that he was staying as a guest of the Maharaja of Jodhpur is the usual account but was not confirmed here [Verification Required]. [Additional] His death day coincided with Diwali (Wikipedia, Arya Samaj).

[Correction] Lekh Ram, Munshi Ram, the 1893 split and the Gurukul. (a) Pandit Lekh Ram lived 1858-1897 (Wikipedia), so he cannot have founded a gurukul in 1902; the Gurukul at Kangri is associated with Munshi Ram (later Swami Shraddhanand, 1856-1926) [Verification Required] on the founding year and the exact site (some books give 1900-02; Haridwar). Lekh Ram was a well-known Arya preacher in disputes with Muslim leaders such as Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (Wikipedia). (b) The 1893 division was over vegetarianism (the "Mahatma" and "Cultured" parties), usually read as Gurukul vs College factions; the labels "progressive" and "conservative" in the notes are a textbook shorthand, not terms the Samaj used. (c) Lajpat Rai is described in the notes as a "progressive"; Wikipedia says he belonged to the Samaj and was active in its campaigning (Lajpat Rai's own 1915 book on the Samaj is a primary source).

[Correction] Shuddhi, cow protection and the "rift". (a) The notes tie these to "the conservatives" after Dayanand's death and place cow protection in the 1890s. The Gaurakshini Sabha is usually dated to Dayanand's own time (about 1882, c02), so cow protection was part of his programme, not only a later conservative one. (b) Shuddhi in its large-scale form (the 1900s onwards, and notably the 1920s under Shraddhanand) is later than the 1890s; the idea of re-admitting converts goes back to Dayanand [Verification Required] on the earliest instances. (c) Interpretation: "the Samaj widened the Hindu-Muslim gulf" is one scholarly view: Wikipedia says "some authors claim" the Samaj's activities increased antagonism; Britannica says the Samaj has been criticised as "overly dogmatic and militant" with "aggressive intolerance toward both Christianity and Islam", but also notes its contribution to Indian nationalism. The statement that the conservatives "spread hatred against Muslims" is not accepted as a settled fact; it is kept as the class's reading. The Samaj was also opposed by Sikh (Singh Sabha) and Muslim (Ahmadiyya) groups and by orthodox Hindus (Wikipedia).

[Additional] Arya Samaj vs Brahmo Samaj (exam favourite; see table in section 3). Both opposed idolatry and caste by birth; but the Brahmo Samaj rejected the authority of the Vedas (Debendranath, 1850), while the Arya Samaj held the Vedas infallible. Wikipedia notes that Dayanand met Debendranath Tagore and others at Calcutta, studied the Brahmo Dharma, and parted on good terms with the Brahmos.

1.2 Theosophical Society

  • Founded by Madame H. P. Blavatsky and H. S. Olcott in 1875 at New York; the notes say it later shifted its headquarters to Adyar, Chennai (c08).
  • Unique feature (as in the notes): it was established by foreigners inspired by Indian culture (c09).
  • Beliefs: faith in ancient Indian traditions and culture and in the doctrine of the transmigration of the soul (reincarnation and karma).
  • Universal brotherhood: it preached universal brotherhood among all men; the notes infer that this "promoted equality and strengthened democracy" (c09). [Additional] Its "Three Objects" (Britannica): a nucleus of the universal brotherhood of humanity without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour; the study of comparative religion, philosophy and science; and the investigation of unexplained laws of nature and latent human powers.

[Correction] Founders and the move to India. (a) Helena Petrovna Blavatsky (1831-91, Russian-born) and Henry Steel Olcott (1832-1907, American) founded the Society with William Quan Judge (1851-96) in New York in 1875 (Britannica). The day, 17 November 1875, is the usual date [Verification Required]. (b) Britannica: Blavatsky and Olcott moved to India in 1878, "eventually settling in Adyar (near Madras)", which still serves as the international headquarters. The usual chronology is Bombay (1879) and then Adyar (1882) [Verification Required]. So the headquarters moved New York → Bombay → Adyar, not directly to Adyar. (c) [Additional] In 1884 a former employee accused Blavatsky of faking messages from the "Masters"; an investigation by the Society for Psychical Research concluded the accusation was true, which hurt the Society (Britannica) - a limit of the movement.

[Correction] "Inspired from Indian culture" and "strengthened democracy". (a) Britannica describes the Society's doctrine as a spiritual evolutionism drawn from both Eastern and Western esoteric traditions; Indian ideas (reincarnation, karma) were central, but it was not only Indian in inspiration. (b) The "universal brotherhood" object is in the Society's own statement, but the claim that it "promoted equality and strengthened democracy" is a textbook inference, not a stated aim, and Britannica also notes Besant's early defence of a varna-based social order and of women's seclusion in India, which she later changed (c. 1900 onwards). It is therefore classed as revivalist in its defence of Hindu tradition, with a reformist side after 1913 (see section 3).

Annie Besant (1847-1933)

  • Born on 1 October 1847 in London, of mainly Irish descent (Britannica); died 20 September 1933 at Adyar (c10). In early life she was a committed Christian; she turned to atheism in the early 1870s (Britannica).
  • Joined the Theosophical Society in May 1889; first visited India in 1893; settled at Benares in 1895. She became its international president in 1907, succeeding Olcott (after Olcott's death) (c10).
  • Education: she founded the Central Hindu College at Benares in 1898 (the notes: "central Hindu school") and the Central Hindu Girls' School in 1904; the College was absorbed into Banaras Hindu University in 1916 (Britannica) (Map 1, no. 9).
  • Politics: delegate to the Congress in 1914; founded the weekly Commonweal and renamed a bought newspaper New India (1914); founded the All-India Home Rule League in 1916; was interned for 94 days in 1917; and in December 1917 became president of the 32nd session of the Indian National Congress at Calcutta, the first woman to do so (Britannica).

[Correction] Besant: origin, blanks and journals. (a) The notes call her "an Irish woman": she was born in London of "predominantly Irish descent" (Britannica); she was British-born. (b) The blank after "death of ...": Henry Steel Olcott (died 1907); Besant was elected in 1907 and held the office until her death (Britannica). (c) The blank journal: New India (Britannica: in 1914 she founded the weekly Commonweal and bought the Madras Standard, renaming it New India); the notes' "Common weal" is Commonweal. (d) "She joined the INC in 1914": Britannica lists 1914 as the year she served as a delegate to the Congress; her presidency followed in 1917 (the notes' 1917 and Calcutta are correct). (e) "In India she opened Central Hindu school at Banaras": the Central Hindu College (1898); the Girls' School followed in 1904. (f) [Additional] Her 1916 Home Rule League and the Lucknow Pact of December 1916 are mentioned here only because they bear on her career; both are not covered in this series yet.

1.3 Ramakrishna Mission

  • Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (1836-86) was one of the greatest Hindu saints of the 19th century; he believed in renunciation, meditation and devotion to God for religious salvation. Britannica: from his own experience of various disciplines, including Christianity and Islam, he endorsed the tenet that all religions are paths to the same goal (c11).
  • Vivekananda (12 January 1863 - 4 July 1902), born Narendranath Datta in Calcutta, was Ramakrishna's chief disciple; he met Ramakrishna in 1881 and was initiated into monkhood before Ramakrishna's death in 1886 (Britannica) (c11).
  • Message: the essential oneness of all religions; he travelled over India and popularised his teacher's message (about 1890 onwards, as a wandering monk, Britannica).
  • Chicago, 1893: he went to the World's Parliament of Religions at Chicago (Map 3, no. 2); his final address said "Help and not fight", "Assimilation and not destruction", "Harmony and peace and not dissension" (Britannica); he then toured the United States and visited London, and founded the first Vedanta Societies at New York (1894) and San Francisco (1900) (Britannica).
  • Mission: after returning to India in 1897 he founded the Ramakrishna Mission (notes: 1 May 1897), with a twofold purpose: to spread Vedanta as embodied in Ramakrishna's life and to improve the social conditions of the Indian people (Britannica); the Ramakrishna Math at Belur (near Calcutta, on the Ganga) was consecrated in 1898 (Map 1, no. 7) (c12).
  • Work: schools, medical help for the poor, homes for orphans, relief work and publications (the notes; Britannica: educational and philanthropic work in India).
  • Teaching: he popularised service to humanity is service to God; Britannica: labour for the benefit of humanity, "especially the poor and the needy", was the noblest endeavour and a means of divine realisation. He wanted to inculcate liberty, equality and free thinking (notes), criticised the neglect of the masses (the notes: neglect of Western knowledge had made Indian society stagnant) and defended polytheism and image worship (c13).

[Correction] Ramakrishna and Vivekananda: names and dates. (a) The blank after "vivekanand (1863 - 19..)" is 1902 (died 4 July 1902, aged 39; Britannica). (b) "Vivekananda" is the monastic name, not his birth name: he was born Narendranath Datta; Britannica says the name was proposed by Raja Ajit Singh of Khetri before he set out for Chicago. (c) [Additional] Ramakrishna's priesthood at the Dakshineswar Kali temple and his birth name Gadadhar are the usual account but were not confirmed here [Verification Required]; Britannica calls him a "Kali devotee" and gives 1836-86. (d) The notes' "popularised the message ... after" - Ramakrishna chose Narendranath as his spiritual successor (Britannica).

[Correction] Chicago and the founding of the Mission. (a) The event was the World's Parliament of Religions (the notes' "world conference of religions"), Chicago, 1893 (11-27 September is the usual dating) [Verification Required] on the exact days. (b) Britannica: after his return to India with a small group of Western disciples in 1897 he founded the Ramakrishna Mission Association, the forerunner of the Mission, to give structure to the religious and humanitarian work of the disciples; the Math at Belur was consecrated in 1898. The 1 May 1897 date is the traditional day of the Mission's founding and is not confirmed in a source opened [Verification Required]. (c) Note the two bodies: the Ramakrishna Math (monastic order) and the Ramakrishna Mission (service body) (Britannica). (d) [Additional] His lectures on his return journey are collected as Lectures from Colombo to Almora (1897) - see the 2006 PYQ.

[Correction] "Keeping Indians away from the materialistic influences of the West" vs "criticised the neglect of Western knowledge". The two statements in the notes pull in different directions. Britannica stresses Vivekananda's humanistic Vedanta, service to the poor, training the body as well as the mind ("football would bring a person closer to God than the study of the Bhagavadgita"), and rejection of "dogma and superstition". The usual textbook reading is a synthesis: India to learn Western science and organisation, the West to learn Indian spirituality [Verification Required] against NCERT or Bipan Chandra. The notes' line that the Mission kept Indians "away from" Western materialism is therefore read as an emphasis on spiritual values, not a rejection of modern knowledge. [Additional] The notes' "criticised the Indians" refers to his attack on the neglect of the masses and on "touch-me-not" practices [Verification Required]; the defence of image worship follows Ramakrishna's devotion to Kali (Britannica) [Verification Required] on Vivekananda's own words.

2. Muslim Movements

2.1 The Wahabi Movement

  • Origin (as in the notes): a revivalist movement founded by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in the 18th century in Arabia (Najd; Map 2, no. 1) (c14). Britannica: he began preaching about 1740, was expelled from two towns, and in 1744 reached al-Diriyah, where the emir Muhammad ibn Saud encouraged him; thereafter the movement's fortunes were allied to the Saudi dynasty. It stressed tawhid (the oneness of God) and rejected tomb-visiting and saint-veneration as shirk.
  • In India: the notes credit Shah Waliullah (1702-62) with advocating the philosophy; his ideas were propagated by Shah Abdul Aziz and Syed Ahmad Barelvi (c15).
  • Political colour: "however, they gave political colour to his ideas" - they wanted to turn India from Dar-ul-Harb to Dar-ul-Islam (c16).
  • Against whom: initially the movement was against the Sikhs in Punjab; later it turned against the British when Punjab was annexed in 1849 (c17).

[Correction] "Mohd Ibn Abdul Wahab ... Saudi Arab". The name is Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab. His movement began in Najd, central Arabia, about 1740, and was adopted by the Saudi family in 1744 (Britannica). The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia was created only in 1932, so "Saudi Arab" is anachronistic for the 18th century. His life dates (about 1703-92) are the usual figures and were not confirmed here [Verification Required]. [Additional] Britannica: "Wahhabi" is a term used mainly by outsiders; followers call themselves muwahhidun ("unitarians") or salafis. The first Wahhabi state occupied Mecca and Medina and attacked Karbala; the Ottoman sultan ended it in 1818 (Britannica) - this is the link to the Ottoman background in section 2.3.

[Correction] Shah Waliullah, Shah Abdul Aziz, and "Syed Ahmed Bareilvy". (a) Shah Waliullah Dehlvi (usually 1703-62; the notes' 1702) was a Delhi scholar and reformer, a contemporary of the Arabian Wahhab; he did not "advocate Wahhabism". Wikipedia: Barelvi was a follower of Waliullah's spiritual path, and his opponents labelled him a "Wahhabi" though he did not consider himself one. So the Indian movement is better named Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya (the Muhammadan Way), and "Wahabi" is the British / opponents' label (as with Britannica's note that the term is used by outsiders). The exact years (1703-62) [Verification Required]. (b) Shah Abdul Aziz was Waliullah's son and Barelvi's teacher (Wikipedia); his dates (1746-1824) are the usual figures [Verification Required]. (c) "Syed Ahmed Bareilvy" is Syed Ahmad Barelvi (from Rae Bareli), not to be confused with Sir Saiyid Ahmad Khan (Wikipedia's hatnote). (d) Differences from Arabia (Wikipedia): unlike the Najd Wahhabis, Barelvi's followers did not oppose Sufism; Barelvi himself was a Sufi murshid of the Hanafi school.

[Correction] Dar-ul-Harb and Dar-ul-Islam. The notes gloss them as "land of infidels" and "land of Islam". Dar al-Harb (the "abode of war") is a land not governed by Islamic law or without a peace arrangement with Muslims (Wikipedia: "a land without a peace treaty with Muslims", the view of Barelvi and the Faraizi followers), for which jihad was held obligatory; Dar al-Islam is a land under Islamic rule and law. The fatwa declaring India Dar al-Harb is traditionally attributed to Shah Abdul Aziz (about 1803) [Verification Required]. Context: Barelvi's first declared enemy was the Sikh Empire of Ranjit Singh in the Punjab and Peshawar valley; the aim was a base for later action against the British (Wikipedia).

[Correction] "Against the Sikhs, then against the British after 1849". The sequence is broadly right but compressed. (a) The jihad against the Sikhs was fought in 1826-31 and ended with Balakot (6 May 1831); the annexation of the Punjab by the East India Company (1849, under Dalhousie) came 18 years later. (b) Wikipedia: the purpose from the start was an Islamic state in the Peshawar valley as a base for later campaigns against the British; so the British were the long-term target, not a new enemy after 1849. (c) After Balakot, the followers kept up the movement on the north-west frontier and through networks in Bengal and Bihar; the British prosecuted "Wahabi" cases in the 1860s and the term became the colonial label for Muslim militancy [Verification Required] (no source opened for the trials). (d) [Additional] Legacy: Wikipedia says Barelvi's teaching influenced the Ahl-i-Hadith and Deobandi movements; the 1849 annexation and later "Wahabi" events were not opened in a primary source here.

2.2 Saiyid Ahmad Khan (1817-98) and the Aligarh School

[Inline request from the class] The rough notes insert here: "Add background of Mohammad to the ottoman and britain war to the impact on muslims of India". The wording is unclear (a person's name appears to be missing). It is read as "the background of the Ottoman-British war and its impact on the Muslims of India" and answered in section 2.3 immediately below this section's opening, as the class asked (c18).
  • Born on 17 October 1817 at Delhi; died 27 March 1898 at Aligarh (Britannica) (Map 1, no. 13). He belonged to a high-class Muslim family close to the Mughal royal establishment; his maternal grandfather had twice been prime minister of the Mughal emperor and had also held posts under the East India Company (Britannica) (c20).
  • Career: after early education he joined the Company's service (a clerk from 1838; a sub-judge three years later, Britannica); at the time of the revolt of 1857 he was posted at Bijnor, Uttar Pradesh (the notes) (c20).
  • "Reform movements in Islam emerged late, only after 1857" (as in the notes) - see c19.

2.2 (continued) After 1857: the Loyalist, the College and the Journal

  • After the revolt: the British government began prosecuting the Muslims; at this time Sir Saiyid wrote The Loyal Muhammadans of India, giving the message that Muslims too had saved the British (c20). [Additional] His pamphlet "The Causes of the Indian Revolt" (Urdu, 1859) laid bare the weaknesses and errors of the British administration and had considerable influence on policy (Britannica).
  • 1869: he went to England; after returning he established the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College in 1875, later the Aligarh Muslim University (1920) (c21). Britannica: during the 1869-70 visit he planned "a Muslim Cambridge".
  • Aligarh school: the liberal, social-reform movement with the college as its centre is known as the Aligarh school (or movement). [Additional] In 1886 he organised the All-India Muhammadan Educational Conference, the main national centre of Indian Islam until the Muslim League was founded in 1906 (Britannica).
  • Religious views (as in the notes): he criticised polygamy and the purdah system and was critical of the ulema (Islamic theologians); he said that only the Quran should be accepted as [authority] and not even the hadis (c22). Britannica: he wrote a modernist commentary on the Quran, seeking to harmonise Islamic faith with the scientific and progressive ideas of his time.
  • Journal: Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq (Britannica: Tahdhib al-Akhlaq, "Social Reform"), published for the "uplift and reform of the Muslim", started after his return from England (c21).
  • Politics (as in the notes): in early life he was a secular leader; after the formation of the Congress he dubbed it a Hindu party; he began saying that the Muslims constituted a separate Qaum (nation); "he could be held responsible for the spread of communalism" (c23).

[Correction] "Reform movements in Islam emerged late, only after 1857". The movement led by Syed Ahmad Barelvi (Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya) began about 1818 and ended in battle in 1831, so reform movements among Indian Muslims predate 1857 (section 2.1). What came after 1857 was the modernist and educational reform of the Aligarh school (and the Deoband seminary, usually dated 1866 [Verification Required]; not recorded today). The notes' statement is read as "modern reform among the Muslims began after 1857".

[Correction] Early career, Bijnor and the "Loyal Muhammadans". (a) Britannica: he started as a clerk in 1838 and qualified as sub-judge in 1841; his posting at Bijnor in 1857 and his rescue of Europeans there are the usual account but were not confirmed in a source opened [Verification Required]. (b) The Loyal Muhammadans of India (a tract in parts, usually dated 1860-61) [Verification Required] on the date and form; the Causes of the Indian Revolt (1859) is the better-documented work (Britannica) and is not in the notes. (c) "After the revolt the British began prosecuting the Muslims": the harsh treatment of Muslims after 1857 is the standard background but the exact nature was not verified [Verification Required]. (d) The notes' summary that he "joined the services of the company's government" is correct; "the reform movement started by him" is the Aligarh movement, not a political party.

[Correction] College dates; the journal's language; "Aligarh Muslim College". (a) England visit: 1869-70 (Britannica). (b) MAO: a Muslim school was established at Aligarh in May 1875 (the exact day, 24 May, is the usual date [Verification Required]); after his retirement in 1876 he enlarged it into a college, whose foundation stone was laid by the Viceroy in January 1877 (Britannica). So 1875 is the school's date; the college dates from 1877. (c) Aligarh Muslim University (not "Aligarh Muslim College") dates from 1920 (examday.ai; the Act and day [Verification Required]). (d) Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq was published in Urdu, not Persian: Britannica says his authorship was "in Urdu" and that he "started an influential journal, Tahdhib al-Akhlaq" on his return; the usual date is 1870 [Verification Required]. The English meaning "improvement in manners and morals" is the notes'; Britannica translates it "Social Reform".

[Correction] Polygamy, purdah, hadis and the ulema. (a) Ulema: Britannica notes conservative opposition to his projects; examday.ai says he criticised the ulema's rigid interpretations (consistent with the notes). (b) Purdah: the notes say he criticised it; examday.ai says he held conservative views on purdah; sources disagree and nothing was found in Britannica [Verification Required]. (c) Polygamy: not confirmed in a source opened [Verification Required]. (d) Quran vs hadis: "only the Quran, not even the hadis" is a strong version of his position; he wrote a rationalist commentary on the Quran (Britannica) and applied critical tests to the hadis, rather than rejecting all hadis [Verification Required]. The blank in the notes is filled as "[authority / source of guidance]".

[Correction] "Secular, then Hindu party; separate Qaum; responsible for communalism". (a) Turning point: Britannica dates his sense that the paths of Hindus and Muslims "must diverge" to the late 1860s (the Hindi-Urdu controversy at Benares, from 1867), before the Congress (1885) - so the break is earlier than "after the formation of Congress". (b) On the Congress: Britannica says that when some Muslims joined it he "came out strongly against" it and its aim of parliamentary democracy, arguing that in a country of communal divisions, with education and political organisation confined to a few classes, parliamentary democracy would work inequitably; Muslims followed his advice and stayed out of politics for several years. The phrase "a Hindu party" was not confirmed in a source opened [Verification Required]. (c) Qaum in Urdu can mean "community" or "nation"; his usage is a matter of interpretation [Verification Required]. (d) Interpretation: that he is "responsible for communalism" is one view (the "Two-Nation Theory precursor" reading, examday.ai); other readings stress his educational aims and loyalty to the Raj. Present as a contested historiographical judgement, not a fact. The notes' contrast of an early "secular" Saiyid is supported by Britannica: his early institutions (the Scientific Society, schools at Moradabad, 1858, and Ghazipur, 1863) were jointly operated by Hindus and Muslims.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Dayanand Saraswati: b. 1824 Tankara; d. 30 Oct 1883 Ajmer; Arya Samaj 10 Apr 1875, Bombay; ten principles settled at Lahore 1877; Satyarth Prakash; Vedas infallible; "go back to the Vedas"; DAV (Lajpat Rai, Hansraj) vs Gurukul (Munshi Ram, 1902); 1893 split over vegetarianism; Shuddhi.

Mains Points

  • Revivalism and nationalism: the Arya Samaj, the Ramakrishna Mission and the Theosophical Society restored pride in Indian tradition against Western and missionary criticism (Britannica on Besant), and the Samaj contributed to nationalism - but they also hardened religious boundaries (c07).
  • Two readings of the Arya Samaj: reformer (anti-idolatry, merit-based caste, education, widow remarriage) vs a militant, exclusivist movement (Britannica's criticism); the Vedic authority claim limited its rationalism (c07).
  • Vivekananda: a synthesis of Vedantic spirituality with service to the poor; shift from individual salvation to social service (c13).
  • Muslim responses: revivalist jihad (Barelvi) vs modernist education and loyalty (Sir Saiyid); the choice of loyalty and non-participation in politics shaped later Muslim politics (c23).
  • Pan-Islam: the caliphate linked Indian Muslims to the Ottoman Empire, which made the Ottoman-British war of 1914 a loyalty dilemma and led to the Khilafat movement (section 2.3).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Syed Ahmad Khan (Aligarh, 1817-98) vs Syed Ahmad Barelvi (jihad, 1786-1831) (c15). Arabian Wahhabism vs the Indian Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya: the second did not oppose Sufism.
  • Annie Besant was not the founder of the Theosophical Society (2013 PYQ); she was its president from 1907 and Congress president in 1917, born in London (c10).
  • Arya Samaj 1875, Bombay (not 1835); Vedas infallible, unlike the Brahmo Samaj; Lajpat Rai supported the Vedic appeal (2001 PYQ).
  • Vivekananda (1863-1902) vs Ramakrishna (1836-86); Ramakrishna Math vs Mission (c12).
  • Lekh Ram (d. 1897) vs Munshi Ram (Gurukul, 1902) (c06); DAV vs Gurukul.
  • Tahdhib al-Akhlaq in Urdu, not Persian; MAO school 1875 vs college 1877 vs AMU 1920 (c21).
  • Ottoman-British war began in 1914, not in Sir Saiyid's lifetime (c18).
