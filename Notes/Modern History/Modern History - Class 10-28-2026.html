4. Political
  • The British policy of conquest and annexation reached its peak under Dalhousie, who annexed several kingdoms in his eight years in office (see c01).
  • His Doctrine of Lapse was hated by Indian rulers, who lost the right to pass on the throne to adopted sons.
  • His policies produced a class of dissatisfied rulers whose participation gave the revolt legitimacy (see c02).
  • [Additional] Dalhousie was Governor-General from 1848 to 1856 (Britannica). Satara, Jaitpur, Sambalpur, Jhansi, Nagpur and others: 10-24 table. Oudh was annexed on 11 February 1856 for "misgovernment", not under Lapse.
5. Religious
  • Christian missionary activity created fear among Indians, and the government also promoted them.
  • Dalhousie's government enacted the Religious Disabilities Act (Lex Loci Act), 1850, giving a convert to Christianity the right to inherit ancestral property (see c03).
  • The religious and social sentiments of conservative Indians were hurt by "humanitarian" measures: the abolition of sati (Regulation XVII of 1829, Bentinck); legalising widow remarriage (Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856); "Cannine class" (unclear, see c05); and the promotion of girls' education. These were seen as undue interference by the government (see c04).
6. Military
  • While recruiting sepoys, the Company allowed them ample freedom to follow caste- or religion-based identity. Later, the government wanted uniformity and these privileges were abolished; the sepoys reacted.
  • Dalhousie's government passed the Post Office Act, 1854 (the notes say "Indian Post Office Act"), which charged fees for letters sent by sepoys.
  • His successor Canning enacted the General Service Enlistment Act (25 July 1856): it removed allowances (bhatta) and made it compulsory for sepoys to serve anywhere (see c06).
Triggering factorIn January 1857 the old Brown Bess was replaced by the Enfield rifle, whose greased cartridges were said to contain a mixture of beef and pork fat; this was the spark that ignited the revolt (see c07).

1.1 Political Cause: Dalhousie and the Doctrine of Lapse

[Correction] Conquest, annexation and "no code of conduct". Dalhousie was Governor-General from 1848 to 1856 (Britannica: "tenure as governor-general of India (1848-56)"), so "eight years" is right. His additions of territory came by different routes, not one: conquest (the Punjab, after the Second Anglo-Sikh War, 1849), the Doctrine of Lapse (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur etc.), and "misgovernment" (Oudh, 1856; Britannica notes the Doctrine of Lapse "could not be enforced" there). Britannica says Dalhousie "believed that Western rule was preferable to Eastern rule and should be enforced where possible". "Followed no code of conduct" is a critical (nationalist) judgement, not a fact; present it as: annexation by stated principles that Indians saw as unjust (Britannica; Wikipedia).

[Correction] Lapse and "dissatisfied rulers legitimised the revolt". The Doctrine applied to dependent Hindu states: Dalhousie asserted the British right to approve adoptions and to annex states with no direct natural or approved heir (Britannica); it did not abolish adoption as such. On "legitimised": Britannica says that, except the Mughal emperor, his sons and Nana Sahib, none of the important Indian princes joined the rebels; Wikipedia adds that rulers in Indore, Sagar and Gwalior (Scindia) stayed loyal. Nana Sahib and Rani Lakshmibai had "initially been willing to accept British supremacy" and rebelled when their claims were rejected. So aggrieved rulers gave leadership in some regions, and Bahadur Shah II's name gave symbolic legitimacy, but the claim that rulers as a class legitimised the revolt is the notes' interpretation, not a settled fact.

[Correction] Name and scope of the 1850 Act. The textbook names "Religious Disabilities Act" and "Lex Loci Act" are used in exam books, but the statute's title is the Caste Disabilities Removal Act, 1850 (Act XXI of 1850), enacted on 11 April 1850. It extended to all Company territories the principle of Regulation VII of 1832 (Bengal Code) that no law or usage should deprive a person of property or inheritance for renouncing a religion or for being "excluded from the communion" of a religion or deprived of caste. So it protected any person who changed religion or lost caste (a Hindu convert to Islam or Christianity), not only a convert to Christianity. Britannica puts the effect as "converts to Christianity were to share with their Hindu relatives in the property of the family estate"; the Christian-convert case is the one that alarmed conservatives (Wikipedia, with the Act's text).

[Correction] The "humanitarian" measures: who, when, and whom they affected. (a) The Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act was enacted in 1856; Britannica says Dalhousie "introduced a bill" on it, but Dalhousie's term ended in early 1856 (Canning succeeded him), so the Act itself belongs to Canning's time; the notes' place for it under Dalhousie's "religious" policy is therefore a shorthand. (b) Sati was abolished in 1829 under Bentinck, long before Dalhousie. (c) These measures are best read as grievances of conservative civilians and the religious elite; Wikipedia notes that "there is little evidence that the sepoys' allegiance was affected" by social reform, and that sepoys were more affected by service conditions (c06) and by missionary fears (Wikipedia). The present list also overlaps the 10-27 compilation, cause 5.

[Correction] "Cannine class" is unclear. The rough notes place the words "Cannine class" between widow remarriage and girls' education. No Act, measure or social class with this name is known in this context, and it may be a stray or incomplete entry. It has not been guessed or expanded; it is [Verification Required] against the teacher's notes.

(d) The loss of bhatta is a separate grievance, linked to the annexation of Oudh and Punjab, not a clause of the Enlistment Act.

1.4 The Triggering Factor: the Enfield Rifle

  • What: the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle-musket was introduced into the Bengal Army, replacing the smoothbore "Brown Bess". Its paper cartridges were pre-greased and were traditionally torn open with the teeth.
  • Why it mattered: a rumour said the grease was cow tallow (offensive to Hindus) and pig lard (offensive to Muslims). Britannica: there is "no conclusive evidence" that either was used, but the belief fed the larger suspicion that the British meant to undermine Indian traditional society.
  • When and where: rumours circulated in early 1857; British officials learnt of them through disputes at depots such as Dum Dum and Barrackpore. The Company then ordered that cartridges be issued ungreased and allowed sepoys to apply their own grease, which did not calm fears (Wikipedia). The 19th Bengal Native Infantry refused the cartridges on 26 February 1857; Mangal Pandey attacked officers at Barrackpore on 29 March; the Meerut troopers who refused cartridges were jailed and the general rising followed on 10 May 1857 (10-27, section 4.3).
  • Consequence: historians now see the cartridge affair as a catalyst that turned widespread discontent into open rebellion, not as the only cause.

[Correction] "Jan 1857", "gun", and "beef and pork". (a) The Enfield was a rifle-musket (the Brown Bess was a smoothbore musket), and its introduction began before 1857; what happened in early 1857 was the spread of the cartridge rumour and the first refusals. The exact January date of the Dum Dum incident is [Verification Required]. (b) The animal fats alleged were cow tallow and pig lard ("beef and pork" fat); the point is the rumour, because the presence of such fat in cartridges issued to the troops is not conclusively proved (Britannica; Kim Wagner in Wikipedia: "little direct evidence" that such cartridges were issued before the revolt). (c) The "spark which ignited the fire" is a metaphor: the underlying causes (sections 1.1-1.3 and 10-27) were already present.

5. Consequences: the Government of India Act 1858

What / who / when / where / why. Parliament in London, on 2 August 1858 (royal assent), passed "An Act for the better Government of India" (21 & 22 Vict. c. 106), in force from 1 November 1858, because the revolt compelled the government to act (Wikipedia: Palmerston's earlier bill cited the "grave defects" of the existing system; Lord Derby's bill was the one passed). The notes say the Act was passed "even before the revolt was completely suppressed": that is correct, because fighting went on until June 1858 (Gwalior) and peace was declared on 8 July 1859 (see 10-27 c13). Consequence: the end of Company rule and the start of direct Crown rule (the British Raj).

[Correction] Act, "Act for betterment of India" and Queen's Proclamation are not the same thing. The notes write "act for betterment of India / govt of India act 1858 / queen's proclamation" as one item. They are two documents: (a) the Government of India Act, 1858, the long title being "An Act for the better Government of India" - a statute of the UK Parliament, royal assent 2 August 1858; (b) the Queen's Proclamation to the "Princes, Chiefs, and People of India", dated 1 November 1858 - a declaration of policy issued a few months after the Act (Wikipedia: "The act was followed a few months later by Queen Victoria's proclamation"). The long title says "better Government" (administration), not "betterment" (welfare) (Wikipedia; Act on legislation.gov.uk).

5.1 Provisions of the Act of 1858 (as in the class) and the Structure after 1858

  1. End of Company rule: the Company's territories passed to the Crown; India was to be governed in the Queen's name.
  2. In England: the Board of Control and the Court of Directors were abolished; the office of Secretary of State for India was created, ending the "double government" (see c13). The Secretary of State was a British Cabinet minister assisted by a Council of 15 members, the India Council / Council of India.
  3. In India: the Governor-General took the additional title of Viceroy (representative of the Crown) and had an Executive Council of 4 members, his official advisers and heads of departments.
  4. End of annexation: an end to further conquest and annexation, and the dignity of the rulers would be maintained (see c13: this is the Proclamation).

6. Question: How was the Revolt of 1857 a Watershed in British Policy towards India?

[Additional] This is UPSC Civil Services Mains 2016, General Studies Paper I (see the PYQ section). Britannica uses the same idea: the immediate result was "a general housecleaning of the Indian administration". The class answer follows.

Introduction. The revolt had shaken the foundations of British rule. Afterwards the government wanted policies to prevent another revolt.

  • Divide and rule: "martial" and "non-martial" races. Castes and communities that helped the British in suppressing the revolt (Gurkhas, Sikhs) were classed as martial races; those that fought against were classed non-martial. The notes call this a sinister motive to keep Indians divided (see c14).
  • Strict ratio of British to Indian soldiers: 1:2 in the Bengal Army and 1:3 in Bombay and Madras (see c14).
  • Caution and conservatism: the government became slow in making social laws. It took 73 years after the Widow Remarriage Act (1856) to pass the Sarda Act, 1929, fixing marriage ages at 14 (girls) and 18 (boys) (see c15).
  • Princes: the dignity of rulers was to be respected and annexation was stopped.
  • Indian Councils Act 1861: gave representation to Indians, but they came from the ruling class (see c16).
  • Control from England: after 1858 India was remotely controlled by the Secretary of State, and the Viceroy's power decreased; the voice of Indians could not be heard.

Conclusion. There is no denying that British policies changed after 1857, but they were designed for better control of India, not for its betterment.

[Correction] "Martial races", the ratios and the sequence. (a) The martial race designation was created by army officials after 1857, and it was popularised later (Frederick Roberts) and made part of the army's recruiting manuals; it was not a single order issued at once after the revolt (Wikipedia). (b) The notes' rule that those who helped were "martial" and those who fought were "non-martial" is the broad logic of the policy: after the revolt the Bengal Army diminished recruitment from its old areas and favoured groups that had stayed loyal (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims, Pathans, Rajputs, Jats and others), while high-caste Hindus of Awadh and Bihar were discouraged (Wikipedia). It was also tied to a belief that such groups were politically docile. Calling the whole policy a "sinister" design to divide is the nationalist critique; Richard Schultz is cited by Wikipedia as saying it was a British effort to "divide and rule", while others link it to a military view of who made good soldiers. (c) Ratios: Wikipedia says only that the British increased the ratio of British to Indian soldiers; the 1:2 (Bengal) and 1:3 (Bombay and Madras) figures are the textbook numbers (also attributed to the Peel Commission, 1858-59) and are [Verification Required].

[Correction] "73 years" and "slow in enacting social laws". The arithmetic is right: 1856 + 73 = 1929. The Child Marriage Restraint Act (Sarda Act) was passed on 28 September 1929, came into force on 1 April 1930, and fixed the minimum age at 14 for girls and 18 for boys (Wikipedia). But it was not true that no social law was passed in between: the Age of Consent Act, 1891 (a related Act listed with the Sarda Act on Wikipedia) is one example; the exact age and the other laws are [Verification Required]. The Act was also weakly enforced; Nehru, quoted on Wikipedia, blamed government reluctance to antagonise communal elements. Hence "slow and cautious" is a fair judgement, "no social laws" is not.

[Correction] Indian Councils Act 1861: "representation" and the Viceroy's power. (a) The Act received royal assent on 1 August 1861. Britannica says the new council "was given an Indian-nominated element"; Wikipedia says Indian members were nominated, not elected, and drawn from zamindars, princes and nobles; in 1862 Canning nominated three: the Raja of Benares, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao. So "gave representation to Indians" should be read as association by nomination, not representation by election. The council was advisory and had no power over finance. (b) "Power of Viceroy decreased": true of control from London (the Secretary of State could reject laws even after the Viceroy approved them), but within India the Act gave the Viceroy an ordinance power (up to six months) and the portfolio system (Canning, 1859) turned the Executive Council into a "miniature cabinet" under him (Wikipedia). Present it as: subordinate to London, supreme in India.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Dalhousie: Governor-General 1848-56; conquest (Punjab 1849), Lapse (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur), Oudh 11 Feb 1856 (misgovernment, not Lapse). Canning: Governor-General during the revolt; first Viceroy.
  • Caste Disabilities Removal Act (Act XXI), 11 Apr 1850 ("Religious Disabilities" / "Lex Loci" Act): no forfeiture of rights on change of religion or loss of caste.
  • Sati abolished by Bentinck (Regulation XVII, 1829); Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856.
  • General Service Enlistment Act, 25 Jul 1856 (new recruits; overseas service); batta lost after Oudh and Punjab annexation; Post Office Act 1854 (free postage withdrawn).
  • Trigger: Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle; greased cartridge rumour (cow tallow and pig lard); replaced Brown Bess.
  • Leaders: Delhi - Bahadur Shah II, Bakht Khan; Lucknow - Hazrat Mahal, Birjis Qadr; Kanpur - Nana Sahib, Tantia Tope; Allahabad - Liaquat Ali; Bareilly - Khan Bahadur Khan; Faizabad - Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah; Jhansi - Lakshmibai; Jagdishpur - Kunwar Singh, Amar Singh.
  • British officers: Delhi - Hodson, Nicholson; Lucknow - Lawrence, Havelock, Outram, Campbell; Kanpur - Havelock, Campbell; Jhansi - Hugh Rose; Arrah - Vincent Eyre.
  • Bombay Army: 3 mutinies in 29 regiments; Madras Army: none (Wikipedia). Bihar rising mainly in the west.
  • Government of India Act: 2 Aug 1858 (in force 1 Nov); Board of Control and Court of Directors abolished; Secretary of State + Council of 15; Governor-General became Viceroy. Queen's Proclamation: 1 Nov 1858.
  • Indian Councils Act 1861 (assent 1 Aug 1861): nominated Indians (1862: Raja of Benares, Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao); portfolio system. Sarda Act: 28 Sep 1929; girls 14, boys 18.

Mains Points

  • Dalhousie's policy: annexation by conquest, Lapse and "misgovernment" together turned rulers, taluqdars and sepoys (many from Oudh) against the Company; but few rulers joined, so avoid saying "rulers legitimised the revolt" (c02).
  • Religious fear and service conditions: distinguish civilian fears (missionaries, social reform) from sepoy grievances (privileges, allowances, overseas service, cartridges); the cartridge was a catalyst, not the only cause (c04, c07).
  • Nature: use the "more than a mutiny, less than a national revolt" line with the evidence for and against (Awadh and Bundelkhand vs south, Punjab, Bengal) and name the historians (Savarkar, Stokes) (c10, c11).
  • Why it failed: limited geography, no unified leadership or programme, loyalty of princes and Punjab, British resources and communications.
  • Watershed: Crown rule; Proclamation; end of annexation; army reorganisation and "martial races"; consultation by nomination; caution in social reform; tighter control from London and a strong Viceroy in India (c13-c16).

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Dalhousie - Governor-General 1848-56; Doctrine of Lapse. Canning - Governor-General in 1857; first Viceroy. Bentinck - Governor-General 1828-35; abolished sati.
  • Doctrine of Lapse - annexation of a dependent state without a natural or approved heir. Misgovernment - the plea for annexing Oudh. Lex Loci / Religious Disabilities Act - the 1850 Caste Disabilities Removal Act.
  • Sepoy - Indian soldier of the Company. Bhatta - extra allowance for foreign service. Brown Bess - the old smoothbore musket. Enfield rifle - the new rifle-musket (Pattern 1853). Purbiyas - eastern (Awadh and Bihar) recruits of the Bengal Army.
  • Bahadur Shah II - last Mughal emperor, nominal leader. Bakht Khan - rebel commander at Delhi. Hazrat Mahal and Birjis Qadr - Lucknow. Nana Sahib (Dhondu Pant), Tantia Tope - Kanpur. Liaquat Ali - Allahabad. Khan Bahadur Khan - Bareilly. Ahmadullah Shah - Faizabad. Rani Lakshmibai - Jhansi. Kunwar Singh, Amar Singh - Jagdishpur (Bihar).
  • Hodson, Nicholson, Wilson - Delhi. Lawrence, Havelock, Outram, Campbell - Lucknow and Kanpur. Hugh Rose - Jhansi and central India. Vincent Eyre - relief of Arrah.
  • Secretary of State for India - Cabinet minister (1858) with a Council of 15. Viceroy - Crown's representative. Martial races - groups preferred for army recruitment. Sarda Act - 1929 marriage-age law. Indian Councils Act 1861 - nominated Indians.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Act vs Proclamation: Act (2 Aug 1858) = Crown rule, Secretary of State, Council; Proclamation (1 Nov 1858) = no annexation, rulers' dignity, equal treatment.
  • Double government: Board of Control + Court of Directors (1784-1858) is not Clive's dual system in Bengal (1765-72).
  • Outram: Lucknow, not Delhi. Delhi: Hodson and Nicholson; Arrah: Vincent Eyre.
  • Oudh: annexed on the plea of misgovernment (11 Feb 1856), not by Lapse.
  • Enlistment Act: applied to new recruits; the loss of bhatta was a separate grievance.
  • Bombay and Madras armies: largely loyal, not "neutral"; only isolated mutinies.
  • Bihar: rising mainly in the west (Arrah, Danapur), not most districts.
  • Sati (1829, Bentinck) and widow remarriage (1856, Canning's time): different rulers.
  • Indian Councils Act 1861: nominated, not elected.

30-Second Revision

Causes 4-6: Dalhousie's annexations and Lapse (1848-56; Oudh 1856); missionaries, the 1850 Act, sati/widow-remarriage/girls' education seen as interference; sepoys' privileges, Post Office Act 1854, General Service Enlistment Act 25 Jul 1856, loss of bhatta. Trigger: Enfield cartridges (cow and pig fat rumour). Centres: Delhi (Bahadur Shah, Bakht Khan; Hodson, Nicholson), Lucknow (Hazrat Mahal; Havelock, Outram, Campbell), Kanpur (Nana Sahib, Tantia Tope), Allahabad (Liaquat Ali), Bareilly (Khan Bahadur), Faizabad (Ahmadullah), Jhansi (Lakshmibai; Hugh Rose), Arrah (Kunwar Singh; Eyre). Failure: limited spread, loyal princes and Punjab, no unified plan, British resources. Nature: more than a mutiny, less than a national revolt. Result: Act 2 Aug 1858 (Secretary of State, Council of 15, Viceroy), Proclamation 1 Nov 1858, martial races, Councils Act 1861, caution in social reform.
