Introduction: Start of the Modern Phase

  • Historians in India are described as having reached a consensus that the modern phase of Indian history began in the early 18th century (c01).
  • The period was marked by two major developments:
    1. Rivalry among the European trading companies.
    2. Decline and disintegration of the Mughal empire and the emergence of regional kingdoms.

[Correction] "Consensus" is too strong. Textbooks (NCERT, Bipin Chandra) take the 18th century as the start - with the decline of the Mughal empire after Aurangzeb's death in 1707 - but there is no single accepted date: many accounts begin with the Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757), and some use the Third Battle of Panipat (1761). For answers, state "conventionally the 18th century" and name the criterion used. The 1707 and Plassey dates are widely cited [Verification Required] against the Class 12 textbook chapter for the exact wording.

A. Rivalry among European Trading Companies

1. The Age of Exploration

  • Europe saw an age of exploration and discovery in the 15th-16th centuries. Europeans made long sea voyages to find an alternative trade route to the East (Map 1).
  • This effort led to the "discovery" of America by Columbus and of the Cape of Good Hope by Bartholomew Diaz.
  • In 1497 the Portuguese sailor Vasco da Gama sailed from Lisbon and reached Calicut in 1498 by way of the Cape of Good Hope.
  • He bought spices and other goods, returned to Europe and made a profit by selling them. This encouraged other Europeans to come, leading to the arrival of the European trading companies in India.
[Additional] The three voyages in the notes

Bartholomew DiazPortugal1488First European to round the southern tip of Africa (Cape of Good Hope); turned back before reaching India
Christopher ColumbusSpain (Crown of Castile)1492Sailed west seeking Asia and reached the Americas (Bahamas), not India
Vasco da GamaPortugal1497-98Lisbon to Calicut via the Cape; first direct sea link between Europe and India (landed 1498)

[Correction] Names and dates. Spellings: "colombus" = Columbus; "Brtholemew" = Bartholomew Diaz (also written Dias); "portugese" = Portuguese; "calicut" = Calicut (Kozhikode). Dates added: Diaz 1488, Columbus 1492. Columbus' "discovery" was a European arrival in the Americas (the continent was already inhabited) and he believed he was near Asia, which is why the Americas' people were called "Indians". Gama's voyage began on 8 July 1497 and he anchored near Calicut on 20 May 1498 [Verification Required] - widely quoted dates, confirm in the textbook. Gama did not "find" the Cape; Diaz did, ten years earlier.

[Additional] Why the sea route? The fall of Constantinople (1453) to the Ottomans made the old land and Mediterranean routes for the Asia trade costlier and riskier for Europeans, and Portugal and Spain, backed by their crowns, sought direct ocean routes (the Portuguese programme under Prince Henry the Navigator). The ruler of Calicut was the Zamorin. The Portuguese later seized Goa from the Bijapur Sultanate in 1510 under Alfonso de Albuquerque and controlled shipping with a permit (cartaz) system (verified through UPSC 2022, PYQ 1).

[Correction] Order of arrival - what the notes get right, and two traps. The sequence Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danes, French matches the standard textbook order of arrival in India, and the French were the last (UPSC 2007, PYQ 7). Two traps: (1) the English company (1600) was founded earlier than the Dutch VOC (1602) although the Dutch reached the Coromandel coast about the same time or earlier; (2) the Danish company (1616) was founded after the English and its Indian bases came later still. "Denmark" is correct: the Danes' home country.

3. "Factories"

  • The trading centres were called factories. In the 17th-18th centuries the word did not mean a processing or manufacturing unit; a factory served as a warehouse.
  • The name came from "factors", the low-paid clerical class who kept the accounts.
  • Since the place was also the office and residence of the factors, it was called a factory.

[Correction] Factors and factories. A factor was a commercial agent who bought and sold goods on the company's behalf; the settlement where factors worked and lived was a factory (a "trading post"). Factors were not simply clerks: the EIC's covenanted servants rose through ranks - writer (clerk), factor, junior merchant, senior merchant - so writers were the clerical grade and factors the next step [Verification Required] on the exact ladder. A factory was a warehouse plus office plus residence and was often fortified.

[Correction] "Always on the coast." Most factories were on the coast or at river ports because trade was by sea, but not always: the English, Dutch and French also had inland factories such as Patna, Agra, Cossimbazar and Dacca (e.g. English Patna 1620; Dutch Agra 1621 and Patna 1638; French Cossimbazar and Dacca) - see PMF IAS explanation and French India (Wikipedia). Say "mostly coastal" in answers.

  • Mahe ("Mahe (Pondichery)" in the notes), Jinji (Tamil Nadu), Chandannagar (Bengal) (Map 4).

4. The British East India Company (EIC)

  • The company, famously the EIC, was established in 1599. On 31 December 1600 it received a charter from Queen Elizabeth I to trade with the East (Asia).
  • The charter was valid for 15 years; in 1615 James I renewed it for an indefinite period (c07).
  • In 1608 Captain William Hawkins reached the court of Jahangir (1605-27) and sought permission for a factory at Surat; permission was granted, but the EIC could not set up a factory there that year (c08).
  • In 1611 the Sultan of Golconda allowed a factory at Masulipatnam.
  • In 1613 the EIC finally set up a factory at Surat. Exactly 20 years later, 1633, it opened a factory at Balasore (Odisha) (c09; Map 3).
  • Over time it set up more factories; the three most important - Bombay, Calcutta and Madras. Each factory had low-paid employees (factors and writers) under a Governor. Later the Governors of the three places came to be called President, and the towns that grew around them were presidency towns (c10).
  • 1639 - Francis Day built the factory at Madras, later fortified as Fort St George (c11).
  • 1662 - the Portuguese princess Catherine married "Prince Charles II" and Portuguese Bombay passed to Charles II as dowry. 1668 - the EIC obtained Bombay from Charles II on lease (c12).
  • 1698 - Job Charnock built a factory at Calcutta, known as Fort William, which has been renamed Vijay Durg recently (c13, c14).

[Correction] Formation and charter. A group of London merchants resolved on 22-24 September 1599 to form the venture; the company was chartered on 31 December 1600 as the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies" (first Governor: Thomas Smythe). So "established 1599" refers to the founding resolution, while 1600 is the date of the royal charter. The notes say the charter's 15-year term was renewed in 1615; sources give 1609 for James I's renewal for an indefinite period (with a clause allowing withdrawal of privileges if trade was unprofitable) [Verification Required]. 1615 is the year James I sent Sir Thomas Roe to Jahangir's court (see c08). Sources: the charter (Wikisource), East India Company (Wikipedia), Imperial Gazetteer of India, vol. II p. 454.

[Correction] Hawkins, Surat and Roe. "Captain william hawkens" = Captain William Hawkins. His ship Hector reached Surat in 1608; he went on to Jahangir's court at Agra in 1609 [Verification Required]. Jahangir (r. 1605-27) first granted permission, but it was withdrawn after opposition from the Portuguese and local interests, so Hawkins' mission failed. The EIC then beat the Portuguese at the Battle of Swally (1612) and obtained the Surat factory in 1613. The later, more successful mission was that of Sir Thomas Roe (sent 1615, at Jahangir's court 1615-18), who secured wide trading rights. Remember: Hawkins and Roe both went to Jahangir, not to Akbar (UPSC 1999, PYQ 9).

[Correction] Masulipatnam, Surat and Balasore. The notes' dates are broadly confirmed: Masulipatnam 1611 (a temporary factory; the permanent factory is dated about 1616), Surat 1613 (the EIC's first permanent factory in India), Balasore 1633 (the first English factory in Odisha / Eastern India; Hugli 1651 followed in Bengal). Spellings: "Machilipatnam" = Masulipatnam. UPSC 2003 and 2021 test these dates (PYQ 8, PYQ 2); sources: PMF IAS (2003), PMF IAS (2021). [Additional] The Golconda ruler's later "Golden Farman" (1632) gave the EIC duty-free trade in its ports for a yearly payment [Verification Required].

[Correction] Governors, Presidents and presidency towns. The three towns Madras, Bombay and Calcutta became the presidencies of the EIC; their heads carried the title "President" (later usually "President and Governor in Council"). The notes say the Governors "came to be known as President", but sources show President as the earlier, and "President and Governor" as the later usage [Verification Required] on the sequence of titles - use "head of the presidency" if unsure. [Additional] Surat was the first presidency; Bombay replaced it in the late 17th century [Verification Required]. The word "presidency town" survives in the Calcutta, Bombay and Madras High Courts and universities.

[Correction] Madras 1639. Francis Day (with Andrew Cogan) obtained the land in 1639 from Damarla Venkatappa (Venkatapathy) Nayak, a Nayak under the Vijayanagara empire, and the factory was fortified as Fort St George (work on the fort from 1639-40 and completed in 1644, on St George's Day) [Verification Required] on the exact date. It was the first fort built by the British in India (UPSC 2007, PYQ 6) - Fort William came later. The 2022 question confirms the land was leased from a representative of the Vijayanagara empire (PYQ 1). "Fort St. George" - spelling with a full stop is also seen.

[Correction] Bombay. "Prince Charles II" = King Charles II of England (restored 1660). The Portuguese ceded Bombay to England under the marriage treaty of 1661; Catherine of Braganza married Charles II in 1662; and in 1668 the Crown leased Bombay to the EIC for a nominal rent (about £10 a year) [Verification Required]. The rest of the notes is correct: the dowry of Bombay passed to the Crown first, then to the company on lease. [Additional] Portugal had held Bombay since the 1530s [Verification Required].

[Correction] Calcutta, Charnock and Fort William. The notes combine three separate events. (1) Job Charnock returned and set up the EIC's headquarters at Sutanuti (Calcutta) on 24 August 1690, after the Mughals permitted the company to restart trade in Bengal (an imperial grant followed on 10 February 1691, and the agency became independent of Madras in January 1692). He died in January 1692 (or 1693), so he could not have built anything in 1698. (2) Fort William was built from 1696 (completed about 1702 for the main fort) under Sir Charles Eyre and Sir John Goldsborough, and named after William III. (3) 1698 is the year the EIC acquired the zamindari of the three villages Sutanuti, Kalikata and Govindpur [Verification Required] - the Calcutta site. [Additional] In 2003 the Calcutta High Court, on an expert committee's report, held that Charnock should not be treated as the sole "founder" of Calcutta, since an older settlement existed (the Sabarna Roy Choudhury family held the land). Sources: Job Charnock (Wikipedia) and Fort William (Wikipedia), to be cross-checked with the Class 12 chapter.

[Correction] "Renamed Vijay Durg." This is correct: the Army's Eastern Command headquarters at Fort William, Kolkata was officially renamed Vijay Durg (after the old coastal fort in Maharashtra's Sindhudurg district) - reported by The Hindu, 5 February 2025. The name "Fort William" is still the term to use for the historical fort in exam answers.

B. Decline and Disintegration of the Mughal Empire; Emergence of Regional Kingdoms

  • In 1717 the Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar issued a farman (royal order) to the EIC granting duty-free trade from Bengal.
  • In return the company had to pay Rs 3,000 a year as a token amount to the Mughal ruler.
  • The company could issue passes / permits for trade known as dastaks.
  • The employees of the company could also trade, but were not covered by the farman; when trading in their personal capacity they had to pay customs duty.

[Correction] The 1717 farman. Farrukhsiyar (r. 1713-19) issued it after the embassy of John Surman, in which surgeon William Hamilton treated the emperor [Verification Required] on the names and dates (1715-17). It is often called the company's "Magna Carta" [Verification Required]. Besides Bengal (duty-free trade for a yearly payment - the Rs 3,000 in the notes), it also confirmed privileges at Surat (against a yearly payment) and other places, and let the company buy villages near Calcutta [Verification Required] on the details. The key point in the notes is correct: dastaks covered the company's own goods only; servants' private trade was not exempt in law, but misuse of dastaks by servants became a long-running dispute with the Nawabs of Bengal. Wikipedia's summary: "in 1717 customs duties were completely waived for the English in Bengal" (East India Company).

Heading mismatch in the notes. The heading "Decline and disintegration of the Mughal empire and emergence of regional kingdoms" has only the 1717 farman under it, so the regional kingdoms are not yet covered in the rough notes. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Diaz 1488 (Cape of Good Hope), Columbus 1492 (Americas), Gama 1497-98 (Lisbon to Calicut); Goa captured 1510 by Albuquerque from Bijapur.
  • Order of arrival: Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danes, French; the French were last.
  • EIC: founding resolution 1599; charter 31 Dec 1600 (Elizabeth I); indefinite renewal 1609 (not 1615).
  • Hawkins (1608-09, Jahangir) failed; Surat factory 1613 (after Swally 1612); Roe (1615-18) at Jahangir's court.
  • Masulipatnam 1611 (temporary); Balasore 1633 (Odisha); Madras 1639 (Francis Day; Fort St George - first British fort); Bombay (dowry 1661-62; EIC lease 1668); Calcutta (Charnock, 24 Aug 1690; Fort William from 1696).
  • French: company 1664; Surat 1668 (first), Masulipatam 1669, Pondicherry 1673, Chandannagar 1692, Yanam 1723, Mahe 1725, Karaikal 1739.
  • Presidency towns: Bombay, Calcutta, Madras. A factory = warehouse + office + residence of factors (a trading post, not a manufacturing unit).
  • Farman of 1717 (Farrukhsiyar): duty-free trade in Bengal for Rs 3,000 a year; dastak = company pass; servants' private trade not covered.
  • Fort William (Kolkata) renamed Vijay Durg (reported 5 Feb 2025); Army Eastern Command HQ.

Mains Points

  • Why the Europeans came: demand for Asian spices and textiles; the old trade routes were disrupted (1453); crowns and merchant capital backed new sea routes; Portugal led, followed by joint-stock companies (EIC 1600, VOC 1602).
  • From trade to power: coastal factories - fortified posts (Fort St George, Fort William) - presidency towns - privileges from Indian rulers (Surat 1613, the Golconda firman, the 1717 farman) - use of the Mughal decline to gain political leverage.
  • Rivalry: Portuguese naval control (cartaz) challenged by the Dutch and English; Anglo-French rivalry later fought out in India (Carnatic Wars) - to be covered in the next classes.
  • Importance of Mughal concessions: the 1717 farman gave the company a trade advantage over Indian and rival merchants; abuse of dastaks by servants led to conflict with the Bengal Nawabs.
  • Cautions: avoid "founder of Calcutta" as a settled fact (2003 High Court ruling); do not treat the EIC as the only company with Mughal permissions - the French Surat factory (1668) also followed a firman from Aurangzeb.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Columbus found the Americas, not India; Diaz found the Cape (1488) but did not reach India; Gama reached India (1498).
  • EIC 1600 is before VOC 1602, but the French were last (company 1664, Surat 1668).
  • Mahe is in Kerala (Malabar coast), not at Pondicherry; Jinji (Gingee) was a fortress, not an early factory.
  • Factories were mostly coastal, not always (Patna, Agra, Cossimbazar); a "factory" is a trading post, not a manufacturing unit; factors were agents, not just clerks.
  • Charter: 1600 for 15 years; indefinite renewal 1609, not 1615 (1615 = Roe's mission).
  • Hawkins failed (1608-09); Roe succeeded (1615-18); both met Jahangir, not Akbar.
  • Surat 1613 = first permanent EIC factory; Masulipatnam 1611 = temporary; Balasore 1633 = first in Eastern India.
  • Fort St George (Madras) is the first British fort in India; Fort William (Calcutta) was built later (1696-1702) and Charnock died before it was finished.
  • Bombay reached the EIC via Charles II (King, not "Prince"), by lease in 1668.
  • "1698" is not Charnock's date: he settled Calcutta in 1690; 1698 is the zamindari purchase [Verification Required].
  • Farman of 1717 covered the company's trade only, not servants' private trade (the dastak abuse).

With reference to the entry of European powers into India, which one of the following statements is not correct?

Corrections Made

Each link returns to the explanation and preserves the original rough-note claim or its intended meaning.

  1. "Consensus" that the modern phase began in the early 18th century is too strong; the 18th century / 1707 or Plassey 1757 are conventions.
  2. Spellings (Columbus, Bartholomew Diaz, Portuguese); dates 1488, 1492, 1497-98; Gama did not find the Cape.
  3. Order of arrival confirmed; EIC (1600) older than VOC (1602); Danes later.
  4. Factors were commercial agents, not merely clerks; writers were the clerks.
  5. Factories were mostly, not always, coastal (Patna, Agra, Cossimbazar).
  6. French places: Surat 1668 correct; Mahe is on the Malabar coast, not Pondicherry; Chandannagar 1692; Jinji is a fortress.
  7. EIC formation 1599 vs charter 1600; indefinite renewal 1609, not 1615.
  8. Hawkins: Surat 1608, Agra 1609; failed; Roe 1615-18; both at Jahangir's court.
  9. Masulipatnam 1611 (temporary), Surat 1613, Balasore 1633 confirmed; Golden Farman (additional).
  10. Governors, Presidents and presidency towns: use "head of the presidency".
  11. Madras 1639: Francis Day; Nayak's grant; Fort St George is the first British fort.
  12. Bombay: "Prince Charles" = King Charles II; treaty 1661, marriage 1662, lease 1668.
  13. Calcutta: Charnock 1690 (died 1692/93); Fort William 1696-1702; 1698 = zamindari purchase; 2003 High Court ruling.
  14. Fort William renamed Vijay Durg confirmed (5 Feb 2025).
  15. 1717 farman: Surman embassy, scope of dastaks, private trade.
