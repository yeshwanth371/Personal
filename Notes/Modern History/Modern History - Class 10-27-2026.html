1. Phase 2 of British Commercial Policy: Industrial Capitalism (1800-1860, class scheme)

1.1 Background

  • The Industrial Revolution in England created a new class of manufacturers (capitalists).
  • Under pressure from this class, Parliament passed the Charter Act of 1813, which ended the monopoly of the EIC on trade with India (tea and China excepted; see 10-25, section 1.7).
  • The result was the arrival of cheap British goods in India.
  • The government of India (the Company's government) followed policies to promote the interests of British capitalists (next table).

[Correction] Duties and "fines". The direction of the policy is well supported: after the Charter Act of 1813 British textiles entered Indian markets and raw cotton was imported into Britain without tariffs, while yarn and cloth were sold back to India also without tariffs; the value of British textile imports into India rose from about £5.2 million in 1850 to £18.4 million in 1896 (Economic history of India, Wikipedia). Britain had earlier protected its own weavers by the Calico Acts (1685-1774), which taxed imported Indian textiles (same source). The notes' precise claims - that Indian goods paid "high" duty in England (textbooks give figures of about 70-80%, against about 2.5-3.5% for British goods in India) and that England imposed "fines" - could not be traced to a source I could open; the "fines" most likely refer to penalties on wearing or selling printed calicoes under the Calico Acts of the early 18th century, which were before this phase. [Verification Required] on both the percentages and the fines. The key point, safe for exams, is unequal tariffs in favour of British industry.

[Correction] Motive: a market for British goods or administrators? The notes link English education to creating a taste for British goods. Macaulay's own stated aim was "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect", to be "interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern" and to refine the vernaculars - an administrative and cultural aim (Wikipedia, quoting the Minute). The notes' economic reading (consumers for British goods) is a later critical interpretation, consistent with the timing but not Macaulay's own words; present it as an interpretation. [Additional] The Downward Filtration Theory (educate the elite and let knowledge percolate down) is linked to this policy (Wikipedia). 2. Phase 3: Finance Capitalism (1860-1947, class scheme)

  • By 1860, British capital began to be invested in India in banking, insurance, railways and irrigation projects.
  • By 1914, the total British capital invested was about £500 million.
  • Despite this large investment, India did not industrialise, because the capital was invested to maximise imperial control over India.
  • Industrialisation needs basic industries such as iron and steel; however, steel began to be manufactured only from 1913.
  • Banks existed but did not encourage Indian industrialists; Indian industries also received no protective tariff until 1924.

[Correction] Figures and dates. (a) The £500 million by 1914 is a textbook figure for the stock of British investment (mainly loans and railway capital); I could not trace it to a source I opened [Verification Required]. (b) Steel: the Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO) opened its plant at Jamshedpur in 1908 (Economic history of India, Wikipedia); the first steel was produced in 1912, with regular output in the following year [Verification Required]; so "1913" is the date of regular production, not of the first steel. TISCO was a private Indian enterprise, set up with Indian funds, and the same source notes that the Raj promised to buy surplus steel. (c) "Protective tariff till 1924": the Steel Industry (Protection) Act, 1924, following a Tariff Board inquiry, gave the first discriminating protection [Verification Required]; the earlier fiscal autonomy convention (1919) is also linked [Verification Required]. (d) [Additional] Railway companies were guaranteed a return of up to 5% in their early years (Wikipedia; see section 3.3); this is the clearest example of capital invested for imperial purposes. The word "finance capitalism" is a historians' label (see the 10-26 c13 on periodisation).

3. Deindustrialisation

Meaning: the decline of traditional industries without the simultaneous rise of modern industries.

Factors

  1. Loss of princely patronage (karkhanas). The Company's conquests and annexations deprived Indian rulers of their wealth. Rulers had maintained separate factories (karkhanas) employing some of the best craftsmen; when they lost their kingdoms they could not maintain them, causing unemployment.
  2. Loss of the army. Without kingdoms the rulers could not maintain armies, so demand for swords, spears and other weapons fell and those workers became unemployed.
  3. Railways. In planning, construction and management British imperial interests were considered and Indian interests were neglected (next subsection).

[Correction] A contested process. The notes state deindustrialisation as a settled fact with three causes. It is, in fact, debated: (a) nationalist and Marxist historians stress British policy (unequal tariffs, loss of patronage); (b) Jeffrey Williamson and David Clingingsmith argue India deindustrialised between 1750 and 1860 in two phases - after Mughal collapse (1750-1810) and then through British factory competition and falling transport costs (1810-60); (c) Prasannan Parthasarathi argues that Bengal, the Marathas and Mysore did not decline in the late 18th century; (d) Rajat Kanta Ray dates the relative decline of the cotton industry to the mid-1820s, estimates it shrank about 28% by 1850 and links it more to British technological innovation than to discrimination; (e) handloom yarn output is said to have fallen from 419 million lb (1850) to 240 million lb (1900); all from Economic history of India, Wikipedia (which also gives Indian industry's share of world output falling from 25% in 1750 to 2% in 1900, an estimate attributed to Williamson [Verification Required]). The notes' causes (1) and (2) are the "loss of princely patronage" thesis of the textbooks and are plausible, but I found no source opened here that quantifies them [Verification Required]. Modern industry did arise later - the first Bombay cotton mills in the 1850s and TISCO in 1908 (Wikipedia) - which is why "without the simultaneous rise of modern industry" is the key part of the definition.

3.1 Railways

  • Dalhousie's government offered a 5% guaranteed return on the investment of British capitalists.
  • Tracks were laid linking ports to nearby interior regions, which were also raw-material producing centres.
  • Such a network would also help in suppressing revolts, because the army could be deployed quickly.

[Correction] Dates and nuance. The first passenger service ran between Bori Bunder (Bombay) and Thane, about 34 km, in 1853; Dalhousie formulated a plan for trunk lines in 1854; the companies were promised land and a guaranteed annual return of up to five percent in the early years, under 99-year leases, with the government keeping the right to buy; route mileage grew from 1,349 km (1860) to 25,495 km (1880), mostly radiating inland from Bombay, Madras and Calcutta (Economic history of India, Wikipedia; the same article lists a short Madras freight line, the Red Hill Railway of 1837, as an earlier first). Wikipedia also says that the railways' stores policy required contracts to be submitted in London, shutting out most Indian firms, and most hardware was bought in Britain (citing Headrick). The notes' judgement that railways caused deindustrialisation is one reading: critics (Marxist and nationalist) call them "the catalyst of complete colonization" (the phrase is from a History optional question of 2012); others note that they integrated markets and helped trade; weigh both. [Additional] Dalhousie's railway minute is dated 1853 [Verification Required] on the exact date; the notes' "government of Dalhousie" is correct for the guarantee scheme.

3.2 Consequences

  • The cumulative effect: formerly flourishing urban centres such as Murshidabad, Dhaka and Patna became depopulated - deurbanisation (see Map 1).
  • This pressed on land, leading to fragmentation of holdings and a rise in poverty.
  • Indian agriculture was already stagnating, and the newly migrating workers could not be absorbed; they formed a large mass of disguised unemployed people.

[Correction] The urban decline claim. That Murshidabad, Dhaka and Patna declined is a textbook claim from the nationalist school (Dhaka was Bengal's capital and had a population said to exceed a million, and was famed for its muslin: Wikipedia). The decline of Murshidabad also followed the shift of the revenue capital to Calcutta (1772) (10-25, revenue-farming table), not only manufacturing. Urbanisation figures are uncertain: Wikipedia gives 4.6-15% urban in 1600 and 6.4-11% around 1840, so the net change is disputed [Verification Required]. Disguised unemployment means more workers than the work needs, so that some add nothing to output. Poverty and land fragmentation are generally accepted, though their weight relative to population growth is debated.

4. The Revolt of 1857

4.1 Names, Dates and Overview

  • Exactly 100 years after the Battle of Plassey, a mighty and popular revolt took place in northern India and soon spread to eastern regions (notes; see c08).
  • When and where: from 29 March 1857 (Barrackpore) and, as a general rising, 10 May 1857 (Meerut); mainly in the Bengal Army's area - the Ganga-Yamuna Doab, Rohilkhand, Oudh, Bundelkhand, central India and Bihar; it lasted until 1858-59.
  • Who: the sepoys of the Bengal Army, with the deposed or aggrieved rulers, taluqdars, peasants, artisans and others in different areas.

[Correction] "Exactly 100 years after Plassey". Plassey was fought on 23 June 1757; the rising began on 10 May 1857 (Barrackpore, 29 March 1857), so it was about, not exactly, a century later. A popular prophecy that Company rule would end a hundred years after Plassey circulated in 1857, and the centenary on 23 June 1857 is often noted [Verification Required] as a rumour, not as proof of planning. Plassey: see the 10-24 file. Where: "northern India spread to the east" is broadly right, but the rising was patchy: Punjab, Bengal (except Barrackpore), Madras and Bombay remained mostly quiet, and Rajputana and central India had local risings (Britannica: it began in the Bengal Army "because it was only in the military sphere that Indians were organised").

4.2 Causes

Causes of the Revolt (rough notes in plain text; additions marked)
TypePoints
1. Economic
  • British economic policies were exploitative and destroyed the traditional structure of the village economy (the "self-sufficient village economy"; see c10).
  • [Additional] Heavy revenue demands under the three systems, the auction of land for arrears and the resumption of tax-free (inam) grants (the Inam Commission in Bombay from 1852) hurt peasants and landholders; deindustrialisation (section 3) threw artisans out of work. [Verification Required] on the Inam Commission dates (the 10-26 file's source gives 1852).
2. Administrative
  • Corruption - more at the lower levels; the police, judiciary etc. were corrupt "to the extreme"; the complexity of the legal system let the rich exploit the poor, increased dependence on lawyers, and caused delays (see c11).
  • Policy of exclusion - Indians were excluded from well-paid superior jobs: all posts above sub-inspector (civil/police) and above subedar (army) were reserved for whites.
  • The British did not extend patronage to musicians, writers etc., which hurt their livelihood.
3. Social
  • The British remained perpetual foreigners; they did not mix with Indians and felt racial superiority.
  • They looked at Indians with contempt and arrogance and kept separate residential areas.
  • This racial arrogance hurt Indians (see c12).

5. Religious and cultural [Additional]
  • Fear that the British aimed to break caste and convert Indians: missionary activity, western education and reforms such as the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856), the abolition of sati (1829) and the law letting converts share family property (Britannica; the Religious Disabilities Act 1850 is the usual textbook name [Verification Required]).
6. Military [Additional]
  • Sepoys were paid less than Europeans and could not rise above subedar; the General Service Enlistment Act (1856) required overseas service, which caste Hindus feared [Verification Required]; the postal concession withdrawn in 1854 [Verification Required].
  • Immediate cause: the Enfield rifle. To load it sepoys had to bite off the ends of greased cartridges; a rumour said the grease was pig and cow fat, an insult to Muslims and Hindus. Britannica: there was "no conclusive evidence" that such fat was used, but the perception fed larger suspicion that the British meant to undermine Indian society.

[Correction] "Self-sufficient village economy". The image of a self-sufficient pre-British village is itself contested: M. N. Srinivas published "The Myth of Self-Sufficiency of the Indian Village" (Economic Weekly, 1960, listed in Wikipedia). The villages had trade and credit links even before the British. What the British changed was the basis of revenue (cash, fixed or revisable demand), land as a saleable commodity, and the decline of handicrafts (see the 10-26 note on impact). Use "self-sufficient" as the nationalist description that the notes follow.

[Correction] Corruption, lawyers and the "exclusion" claim. The sentence "It has inc dependency of layers who delayed further more which lead to..." is incomplete in the notes; read as: complex law and procedure increased dependence on lawyers and caused long delays. On exclusion: the Charter Act of 1833 (Section 87) declared that no Indian was to be barred from office by religion, birth, descent or colour, but in practice the higher civil posts stayed European (see the 10-25 file, c11); in the army the subedar was the highest Indian rank. "All posts above sub-inspector" is the notes' summary and I could not confirm the exact rule in a source I opened [Verification Required]; Indians were employed as munsiffs, sadar amins and deputy collectors in the middle ranks [Verification Required].

[Correction] Racial separation: details. The "separate areas for habitation" are the civil lines and cantonments [Additional]. The notes' point that the British remained "perpetual foreigners" is a nationalist and colonial-critic description; contemporary British views varied, and Macaulay and the utilitarians believed in "improving" Indians (see c03). Present the racial arrogance as a widely reported grievance, not as a precise measure.

Outline (Britannica and the Government of India Act 1858 page, with dates as given there):

  1. Spark: 29 March 1857, Mangal Pandey attacked British officers at Barrackpore; executed 8 April 1857. In April, troopers at Meerut refused the Enfield cartridges and were jailed; on 10 May their comrades rose, shot their officers and marched to Delhi.
  2. Delhi: the garrison joined; by night the pensioned Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II was "nominally restored to power". The seizure of Delhi "provided a focus and set the pattern for the whole rebellion". With the exception of the emperor, his sons and Nana Sahib, none of the important Indian princes joined the rebels (Britannica).
  3. Spread: Oudh (from 30 May), Kanpur, Jhansi, central India, Bihar (the Danapur troops rose on 25 July).
  4. British counter-moves in three parts: (i) the desperate struggles at Delhi, Kanpur and Lucknow in summer 1857; (ii) operations round Lucknow in winter 1857-58 under Colin Campbell; (iii) the "mopping up" campaigns of Hugh Rose in 1858.
  5. End: Delhi retaken in September 1857; Kanpur on 6 December 1857; Jhansi on 3 April 1858; Gwalior on 19 June 1858; peace officially declared on 8 July 1859 (see c13).

[Correction] Dates differ by source. (a) Delhi: Britannica's table gives the recapture as 21 September 1857 after a week of fighting aided by reinforcements from Punjab; many textbooks give 20 September 1857 for the city and the next day for the palace and the capture of Bahadur Shah [Verification Required]. (b) Kanpur: Britannica says it was recaptured on 6 December 1857 (Campbell's victory); textbooks also mention an earlier British recovery of the town in July 1857 under Havelock [Verification Required]. (c) Peace: Britannica gives 8 July 1859 for the official end; some textbooks give 1858 [Verification Required]; the Queen's Proclamation of 1 November 1858 is a separate event. (d) Jhansi/Gwalior: Jhansi fell on 3 April 1858 and Gwalior was retaken on 19 June 1858 (Britannica); the Rani died in the Gwalior fighting in June 1858 (the day, 17 or 18 June, is [Verification Required]). The class should use the stated year-and-month unless the teacher gives a different convention.

[Correction] "Mighty and popular". The notes call the revolt "mighty" and "popular". Britannica's description is more cautious: "widespread but unsuccessful", with few princes joining and "traditional society" making "its protest against the incoming alien influences", which "had failed". Participation was strong in Oudh (taluqdars and peasants), in the Doab and in Bundelkhand, and weak or absent in Punjab, most of Bengal and the south. The study page below also records Bayly's remark that "it was not one movement ... it was many" and the view of Kathleen Gough that many earlier rebellions were "restorative". Treat "popular" as regionally true, not as an all-India fact (Britannica; EduRev study page, secondary).

4.6 Why the Revolt Failed

  • Limited spread (mainly the Bengal Army area) and no all-India aim; Punjab, Bengal, Madras and Bombay stayed largely quiet.
  • Few princes joined; many were loyal (Britannica: "the princes and other natural leaders had either held aloof ... or had proved ... incompetent").
  • No unified leadership or modern plan; leaders such as Bahadur Shah were symbolic. [Additional] Superior British arms, telegraph and reinforcements (Britannica notes reinforcements from Punjab at Delhi).
  • Lack of modern ideas: the revolt looked back to the past; Britannica: "all serious hope of a revival of the past or an exclusion of the West diminished".

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Phase 2: Industrial Revolution → capitalists → Charter Act 1813 ended the monopoly; British goods nominal duty, Indian goods high duty in England; revenue maximised; cash crops; roads and railways; English-educated class.
  • Education: 1813 Rs 1 lakh; 1823 Committee of Public Instruction (5 : 5); Macaulay's Minute 2 Feb 1835; Bentinck's resolution March 1835 (7 March); Anglicists vs Orientalists; English Education Act 1835.
  • Phase 3: British capital from 1860; £500 million by 1914 (textbook); steel from 1912-13 (TISCO, 1908); tariff protection 1924.
  • Deindustrialisation: decline of old industry without new; karkhanas, arms, railways; 5% guarantee (Dalhousie); first train 1853 (Bombay-Thane, 34 km); Murshidabad, Dhaka, Patna.
  • Revolt of 1857: Mangal Pandey 29 March (hanged 8 April); Meerut 10 May; Delhi 11 May (Bahadur Shah II; Bakht Khan); Kanpur (Nana Sahib, Tantia Tope); Lucknow (Begum Hazrat Mahal); Jhansi (Rani Lakshmibai); Arrah (Kunwar Singh); Faizabad (Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah); Bareilly (Khan Bahadur Khan).
  • Immediate cause: greased Enfield cartridges. Political causes: Doctrine of Lapse, Oudh annexed 11 Feb 1856, Nana Sahib's pension.
  • Suppression: Delhi Sep 1857; Kanpur 6 Dec 1857; Jhansi 3 Apr 1858; Gwalior 19 Jun 1858; peace 8 Jul 1859 (Britannica).
  • Result: Government of India Act 2 Aug 1858 (in force 1 Nov 1858); Secretary of State + Council of 15; Queen's Proclamation 1 Nov 1858; Indian Councils Act 1861.

Mains Points

  • Phase 2 as an economic policy: the Company state became an instrument of British industry - tariffs, revenue, cash crops, railways, education. Distinguish what the sources say from the critical reading that links these policies together.
  • Education: Macaulay's stated aim was administrative "interpreters"; critics see a colonial and market motive; a lasting result was an English-educated class that later led nationalism (note: that link is a later development, not in today's notes).
  • Deindustrialisation: a debate - policy versus technology and market forces; modern industry began only slowly (1850s mills, TISCO in 1908).
  • Railways: built for imperial ends (guaranteed returns, London contracts, port-to-raw-material lines, military use) yet integrated markets.
  • 1857: a combination of long-term (annexations, revenue, social and religious fears, racial attitude, exclusion) and immediate causes (cartridges); limited in area and participation; the watershed: from Company to Crown, from annexation to conservative co-option of princes, and from reform to non-interference.

Personalities, Organisations and Terms

  • Macaulay - Legal Member; Minute on Indian Education (1835). William Bentinck - Governor-General 1828-35; accepted the Minute. Anglicists / Orientalists - groups for English / Oriental learning. Downward filtration - educate the elite and let it percolate.
  • Deindustrialisation - decline of traditional industry without new industry. Karkhana - a court or royal workshop. Disguised unemployment - surplus workers who add nothing. Dalhousie - Governor-General 1848-56.
  • Mangal Pandey - Barrackpore sepoy. Bahadur Shah II - last Mughal emperor. Bakht Khan - Delhi commander. Nana Sahib; Tantia Tope; Azimullah Khan - Kanpur. Begum Hazrat Mahal - Lucknow.   • Colin Campbell; Hugh Rose - British commanders. Canning - Governor-General at the Revolt, first Viceroy. Enfield rifle - cause of the greased cartridge rumour. Doctrine of Lapse - annexation without natural or approved heir.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Anglicist vs Orientalist: Anglicists (Macaulay) wanted English; Orientalists wanted Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian learning. The 1813 grant did not say English.
  • Minute vs resolution: Macaulay's Minute (2 Feb 1835) vs Bentinck's resolution (March 1835).
  • Doctrine of Lapse vs misrule: Oudh was annexed for "misgovernment" (11 Feb 1856), not under the Doctrine of Lapse.
  • Meerut vs Barrackpore: Mangal Pandey (Barrackpore, 29 Mar) is a separate incident from the Meerut rising (10 May); Delhi followed on 11 May.
  • Leader-place pairs: Kanpur - Nana Sahib; Lucknow - Begum Hazrat Mahal (not Nana Sahib, as the Testbook question's wrong statement 3 says); Jhansi - Lakshmibai; Arrah - Kunwar Singh.
  • 1858 vs 1859: the Act (2 Aug 1858) and Proclamation (1 Nov 1858) vs the official end of hostilities (8 July 1859, Britannica).
  • Company vs Crown: after 1858 the Governor-General became the Viceroy; the Board of Control and Court of Directors were replaced by the Secretary of State and the Council.
  • Railway guarantee was 5% (not 15%); the first line (1853) was Bombay-Thane.
