[Correction] "Positives done by the British" and the "Renaissance". The institutions had administrative and political motives: Fort William trained Company servants; the Benares college was meant to show British support for Indian learning; the Madrasa served the Muslim elite. The reform movements themselves arose from Indian initiative (Ram Mohan Roy, Vidyasagar, Phule) meeting Western ideas, not as a British gift. "Indian (or Bengal) Renaissance" is a historians' label and is contested: it was largely Bengal-centred, urban and upper-caste, and the comparison with the European Renaissance is debated [Verification Required] (no scholarly source on this was opened). Wikipedia notes that many historians date the start of the Bengali Renaissance to Roy's settling in Calcutta in 1815. Present the "positives" as one view, alongside the nationalist critique of British educational policy (10-27).

The notes' "shifted to London" is read as a reference to the Haileybury arrangement.

2.1 Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772-1833): the "Major Face"

  • Born at Radhanagar (Hooghly district), Bengal, on 22 May 1772; died 27 September 1833 at Bristol, England (Britannica; Map 1, no. 2). He worked on social reform, modern education, religious reform and political awareness.
  • Sati: he crusaded against sati; it was declared illegal in 1829 (Bengal Sati Regulation, Regulation XVII, under Bentinck; 4 December 1829 [Verification Required] on the day). Britannica: his writings "emboldened" the Company's Council to prohibit it.
  • Women's rights: he held that equality between men and women was impossible without economic equality, so he advocated that women inherit parental property. [Additional] He wrote on the "ancient rights of females" in Mirat-ul-Akhbar in 1822 (Wikipedia).
  • Education: he believed modern education would create the right environment for reform. He supported David Hare in setting up the Hindu College (c05) and founded the Vedanta College (1826; Anglo-Hindu School 1822), which taught Indian philosophy together with Western social and physical sciences (Britannica). [Additional] In 1823 he wrote to Lord Amherst opposing a Sanskrit college and urging English education (Britannica, Wikipedia) - a stand on the Orientalist side of the Anglicist-Orientalist controversy (he was for English).
  • Monotheism: he wrote Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin ("A Gift to Monotheists") and criticised the worship of many gods: God is one, formless and all-pervading. He said the idea of one God is found not only in Christianity but in the oriental religions, and that the Upanishads teach one God (c06).
  • Organisations: to preach to a few followers he founded the Atmiya Sabha (1814-15) (Wikipedia 1814; Britannica 1815). In 1828 he founded the Brahmo Sabha, which became the Brahmo Samaj (c07). It criticised caste, polygamy and idolatry.
  • Attitude to British rule: see the table below; he published the journals Sambad Kaumudi (Bengali, 1821) and Mirat-ul-Akhbar (Persian, 1822) to inform Indians on current issues.

Britannica: when the British imposed censorship on the Calcutta press in 1823, Roy, as editor of two early weeklies, "organized a protest" arguing for freedom of speech as a natural right; this was a turning point in his life towards political action. The notes call him the first Indian to start an agitation on a political issue (a traditional textbook claim; see c08).

Conservative reaction: Dharma Sabha (1830)

The conservative group led by Raja Radhakanta Deb founded the Dharma Sabha in 1830 in Calcutta to counter Roy's ideas; its impetus was the ban on sati, and it appealed to the Privy Council against Bentinck's regulation (the appeal was rejected; the ban was upheld in 1832). It later opposed the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856 with a petition of about four times as many signatures as Vidyasagar's (Wikipedia, Dharma Sabha). [Additional] It published Samachar Chandrika.

[Correction] David Hare and the Hindu College. David Hare (1775-1842) was a Scottish watchmaker, not Dutch. The Hindu College (later Presidency College) was founded on 20 January 1817; Hare discussed an English school with Ram Mohan Roy (whom he met through the Atmiya Sabha) and the proposal went through Buddinath Mukherjee and Chief Justice Sir Hyde East (Wikipedia, David Hare). So Roy supported the idea, but the notes' wording that he supported Hare "in establishing the college" is best read as a supporting role; the exact extent of his role is debated [Verification Required]. Radhakanta Deb was a co-secretary with Hare of the Calcutta School Society (1818), so the orthodox leader also backed English education.

[Correction] Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin: date and the "Christian inspiration". (a) The tract was written in 1803-04 (Britannica: composed in 1803; Wikipedia: 1804 at Murshidabad, in Persian with an Arabic preface), not 1819. (b) Because it dates from before Roy studied Christianity (Britannica: he later learned Hebrew and Greek to read the Bible, and published The Precepts of Jesus in 1820), it was not inspired by Christianity. It reflects his earlier Persian-Arabic education and rationalist monotheism; Wikipedia says his beliefs combined Hinduism, Islam, 18th-century Deism, Unitarianism and Freemasonry, and that he did not yet know the Upanishads when he wrote it. The notes' own point that monotheism is found in the oriental religions is therefore closer to the evidence than "inspired by Christianity".

[Correction] Brahmo Sabha became Brahmo Samaj (not the other way round). The notes say Roy "founded Brahmo Samaj which later changed its name". The body was founded in August 1828 (Britannica) as the Brahmo Sabha; the later name Brahmo Samaj dates from the opening of its own premises at Chitpur Road in January 1830 [Verification Required] (not confirmed in a source opened). Britannica and most exam books use "Brahmo Samaj, founded 1828" for both. The exact day (20 August 1828) is also [Verification Required]. [Additional] In 1829-30 Roy went to England as the unofficial envoy of the Mughal emperor Akbar II, who gave him the title Raja (Britannica, Wikipedia); he died at Stapleton, Bristol, on 27 September 1833.

[Correction] Press law, who agitated, and the repeal. (a) "John Adams" is John Adam, acting Governor-General in 1823; the "licensing regulations 1823 act" is the Press Regulation (Licensing) of 1823 that required a licence to print [Verification Required] on the exact date and wording; the notes' "Retrospective one" is unclear and is not expanded [Verification Required]. (b) The agitation was by Ram Mohan Roy together with other Calcutta residents, among whom Dwarkanath Tagore is usually named [Verification Required]; it was not with Debendranath Tagore, who was born in 1817 (Britannica) and was a child in 1823. (c) Charles Metcalfe (acting Governor-General 1835-36) repealed the restriction in 1835 [Verification Required] on the Act's details; Roy had died in 1833, so the repeal came after his death and is credited to Metcalfe, with Roy's protest seen as the start of the campaign. (d) "First Indian to start an agitation on a political issue" is the notes' (and exam books') claim; treat it as a traditional assessment, not a proved fact.

Debendranath Tagore

  • After Roy left for England (1830), the Samaj was run by Dwarkanath Tagore, and was later joined by Debendranath Tagore (his son), who also founded the Tattvabodhini (the notes: 1843) (c09). It worked in rural and urban Bengal.
  • Aims (as in the notes): to criticise the Christian missionaries for their zeal for conversion; and to eliminate evils from Hindu society.
  • [Additional] Debendranath (born 15 May 1817; died 19 January 1905) repudiated the authority of the Vedas in 1850, making reason and intuition the basis; his Brahmo-Dharma (1854) is a commentary on the Sanskrit scriptures; he founded Shantiniketan in 1863 and was called Maharishi (Britannica).

[Correction] Dwarkanath, Debendranath and the Tattvabodhini. (a) The Brahmo Samaj in the 1830s was kept going by Dwarkanath Tagore (patron) and others; Debendranath (born 1817) took it up in the early 1840s, joining formally in 1842-43 [Verification Required] on the exact year (not confirmed in a source opened). Wikipedia's statement that Roy "launched Brahmo Sabha with Debendranath Tagore" in 1828 is not accepted here (Debendranath was then about 11). (b) The Tattvabodhini Sabha is usually dated 6 October 1839 and its journal Tattvabodhini Patrika to 1843 [Verification Required]; the notes' "1843" fits the Patrika. (c) The notes' aim "to criticise Christian missionaries" matches the Brahmo concern about missionary attacks on Hinduism; Britannica stresses Debendranath's insistence on rejecting Vedic authority, which the notes do not mention.

Keshab Chandra Sen and the split of 1866

  • 1858: Keshab Chandra Sen joined (Britannica: he joined "at age 19", born 19 November 1838, so about 1857-58, c10). The Samaj spread to Bombay, Madras, the United Provinces (UP) and the Punjab. [Additional] Keshab visited Bombay in 1864 (Britannica, Prarthana Samaj).
  • Under Sen the Samaj became cosmopolitan: he brought in the teachings of other religions. Debendranath disliked this and expelled Sen in 1865; in 1866 two groups emerged: (a) the Adi Brahmo Samaj under Tagore; (b) the Brahmo Samaj of India (Bharatvarshiya Brahmo Samaj) under Sen (c10).
  • Sen's group: Britannica says it became "eclectic and cosmopolitan", sponsored the Band of Hope temperance society, encouraged women's education, and campaigned for widow remarriage and a law against child marriage; Sen was "instrumental" in getting Brahmo marriage rites legally recognised in 1872.
  • In time the Brahmo Samaj of India divided into progressive and conservative sections; the conservatives came to regard Keshab as an incarnation.
  • 1878: although Keshab had always favoured a minimum marriageable age for boys and girls, he arranged the marriage of his minor daughter to the minor ruler of Cooch Behar. The progressive group objected, left, and founded the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj, led by Anand Mohan Bose (c11).

Cooch Behar was a princely state, now in West Bengal (the notes' "(WB)"). The groom's identity (Maharaja Nripendra Narayan) and the date (6 March 1878) are [Verification Required]. The breakaway group's name is the Sadharan ("common") Brahmo Samaj, 1878 (the notes' "sadaran brahma samaj of india" mixes this with the earlier "of India" name); its founding date (15 May 1878) and other leaders (Sivanath Sastri, Vijay Krishna Goswami) are [Verification Required]. The notes' statement that the conservative group worshipped Keshab as an incarnation is a historians' description of the split, not a doctrine; Britannica says the revolt was against his violating his own reformist principles.

2.3 Paramahamsa Mandali (1849)

  • Founded in 1849 at Bombay (Map 1, no. 5); the notes name Atmaram Pandurang, helped by Balkrishna Jayakar and Dadoba Pandurang (c12).
  • It believed in monotheism and was against the caste system; at its meetings members took food cooked by low-caste people.
  • [Additional] It was a secret society (Britannica: formed in 1849 "for discussion, the singing of hymns, and the sharing of a communal meal prepared by a low-caste cook"); it also advocated women's education and widow remarriage, and its exposure in 1860 is said to have hastened its end (Wikipedia). It is the immediate predecessor of the Prarthana Samaj in Bombay (Britannica).

[Correction] Founders of the Paramahamsa Mandali. Wikipedia (citing Jones and Johnson, and Bandyopadhyay's From Plassey to Partition) says it was started by Durgaram Mehtaji, Dadoba Pandurang and a group of friends, with Dadoba Pandurang leading it after he left the Manav Dharma Sabha (Surat, 1844). The notes' version (Atmaram Pandurang as founder, helped by Jayakar and Dadoba) differs: Atmaram's role in 1849 is not confirmed in the sources opened [Verification Required]; Balkrishna Jayakar (Ram Balkrishna Jayakar) is named as a member by Wikipedia's Prarthana Samaj article. Both lists are given so that neither is lost. Dadoba is the notes' "Daduba".

2.4 Prarthana Samaj (1867)

  • The ideas of the Brahmo Samaj and of Keshab Chandra Sen led to its founding in Bombay; it was also founded by Atmaram Pandurang (c13).
  • Prominent members: M. G. Ranade (Mahadev Govind Ranade), R. G. Bhandarkar (Ramkrishna Gopal Bhandarkar), N. G. Chandavarkar (Narayan Ganesh Chandavarkar).
  • Method: it did not confront the orthodox sections but relied on education among the people. Britannica: it never required members to give up caste, idol worship or the traditional sacraments.
  • Social reform objectives: (a) a minimum marriageable age for boys and girls; (b) women's education; (c) widow remarriage; (d) fighting the caste system.

2.5 M. G. Ranade, the Deccan Education Society and the National Social Conference

  • M. G. Ranade (born 18 January 1842; died 16 January 1901): a judge of the Bombay High Court and historian, called a pioneer in the field of education in the notes; "father of Indian economics" (Britannica) [Additional].
  • He was associated with the Deccan Education Society at Pune (Map 1, no. 6), founded by G. G. Agarkar and B. G. Tilak (and others) in 1884 (c14).
  • In 1887 Ranade, with Raghunath Rao, founded the Indian National Social Conference, usually called the National Social Conference. It met at the same venue as the Congress's third annual session at Madras (Map 1, no. 7), and thereafter met wherever the Congress held its sessions (c15).
  • Because of this it was called the social reform cell of the Congress, though the two organisations were different. It organised a pledge movement, asking people to take pledges against child marriage (c15).

[Correction] Deccan Education Society: 1884, not 1824, and its founders. Wikipedia: Vishnushastri Chiplunkar, Tilak and Agarkar set up the New English School (1880); in 1884 they created the Deccan Education Society with Ranade, Agarkar, M. B. Namjoshi, V. S. Apte and others; Fergusson College followed in 1885. So Ranade was among the early associates and promoters (the notes' "associated with" is right), and the notes' "G. G. Agarka" is G. G. Agarkar.

[Correction] National Social Conference: founder, venue and character. Britannica: Ranade "founded" the Indian National Social Conference in 1887; its link with Raghunath Rao and the description of the first session at Madras alongside the Congress's third session (presided over by Badruddin Tyabji, from memory) are the notes' (and exam books') details and are [Verification Required] against a source (the Wikipedia page for the Conference could not be opened). "Social reform cell of the Congress" is a descriptive label; the Conference was a separate body and arose because the Congress kept social questions out of its agenda [Verification Required] on that reason (see the 2012 PYQ, whose answer was not confirmed). The "pledge movement" details are from the notes only [Verification Required].

2.6 Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820-1891)

  • Born Ishwar Chandra Bandyopadhyay; Vidyasagar ("Ocean of Knowledge") was the title given by the Sanskrit College, Calcutta, where he studied (c16). He was a great humanist and was known for his generosity.
  • He fought for women's causes throughout his life. His efforts led to the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856.
  • He was associated with the Bethune School: John Elliot Drinkwater Bethune (the notes' "Drink water Bethune"), a British philanthropist, opened a girls' school at Calcutta in 1849 (c16).

[Correction] Vidyasagar: title, Act and the Bethune School. (a) Wikipedia: born 26 September 1820 at Birsingha (Paschim Medinipur); died 29 July 1891; he studied at the Sanskrit College (1828-1839 in the infobox; graduated 1841 in the text), which gave him the honorific "Vidyasagar"; he also served at Fort William College (head of the Sanskrit department 1841-46). The notes' "Bandopadyay" is Bandyopadhyay. (b) Widow Remarriage: Wikipedia says Vidyasagar petitioned the Legislative Council despite a counter-petition by Radhakanta Deb and the Dharma Sabha (about four times as many signatures); Dalhousie personally finalised the bill, and the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856 was passed under Canning (Act XV of 1856; 26 July 1856 [Verification Required] on the day) - see 10-28 c04. (c) Bethune: Wikipedia says Vidyasagar was secretary of the Hindu Female School, later called the Bethune Female School; Bethune was a Law Member of the Governor-General's Council [Verification Required] and the school's foundation date (7 May 1849) is [Verification Required]. The notes' "second girls school at Calcutta" is unclear: it may count Phule's Pune school (1848) as first; it is not accepted without a source [Verification Required]. Sanskrit College, Calcutta is not the Benares college of 1791 (c03).

2.7 The Young Bengal Movement (1820s-1830s)

  • A radical movement in Bengal in the 1820s and 30s, led by Henry Louis Vivian Derozio (the notes' "Henry Vivim Derozio"), an Anglo-Indian; his followers were the Derozians. He was a teacher at the Hindu College but, because of his radical ideas, was removed; he later died of cholera (c17).
  • They were inspired by the French Revolution and its ideas of liberty and equality; they criticised inequalities, rigidity in society, superstitions and dogmas.
  • Weaknesses (as in the notes): they were obsessed with western culture; their ideas were "bookish" and not feasible; they failed to take up the issues of the peasants in any significant way. These are the usual textbook critical assessments, not agreed facts [Verification Required].
  • [Additional] Prominent Derozians include Ramgopal Ghosh, Krishna Mohan Banerjee, Rasik Krishna Mallick, Peary Chand Mitra and Ramtanu Lahiri (Wikipedia's Young Bengal navigation box); in 1838 they formed the Society for the Acquisition of General Knowledge.

[Correction] Derozio: dismissed, not "expelled". Wikipedia: Derozio (born 18 April 1809 in Calcutta, of Indo-Portuguese descent on his father's side) was appointed teacher of English literature and history at the new Hindu College in May 1826; he formed the Academic Association in 1828; because of a backlash from conservative parents he was dismissed in April 1831, shortly before he died of cholera on 26 December 1831 at age 22. "Expelled from college" (as if he were a student) is therefore corrected to dismissed as a teacher. His followers were drawn from the same college (1820s: Hindu College founded 1817).

2.8 Jyotiba (Jotirao) Phule (1827-1890)

  • Born into a Mali family (a Shudra caste group, Britannica); born 11 April 1827, died 28 November 1890 at Pune. He organised a powerful movement against the caste system in western India.
  • 1871 (notes; 1873 in Britannica): he wrote Gulamgiri ("Slavery") in Marathi; 1873: he founded the Satyashodhak Samaj ("Society of Truth Seekers") (c18). Aims: (1) fight the caste system; (2) educate women and lower-caste people.
  • He dedicated the book to the anti-slavery movement in America, and attacked the caste system through his writings and articles. Britannica: it compares the position of the lower castes to that of enslaved people in the United States.
  • He married Savitribai Phule while both were minors; she was illiterate, he taught her, and the couple opened the first girls' school at Pune in 1848, which the notes say admitted 9 girls on the first day (c18).
  • [Additional] The Satyashodhak Samaj encouraged weddings without Brahmin priests; he opened a home for pregnant widows and an orphanage; he was given the title Mahatma in 1888 (Britannica).

[Correction] Phule: Gulamgiri, the 1848 school and the first-day roll. (a) Britannica says Gulamgiri was published in 1873 (not 1871); the Satyashodhak Samaj was also founded in 1873 (some books give 24 September 1873 [Verification Required] on the day). (b) Britannica (Phule): he opened "a pioneering school for lower-caste girls in Pune in 1848", with his wife as teacher; Britannica (Savitribai Phule): the school at Bhidewada, Poona, began with six girls. The notes' 9 is a figure found in other sources; the numbers differ and neither is confirmed here [Verification Required]. "First girls' school at Pune" is accurate as "a pioneering school"; whether it was the first in India is a claim and not accepted without a source. (c) Savitribai (born 3 January 1831) was married at nine (Britannica); she is "one of the first female teachers in India"; the Phules opened about 18 schools for girls in the Poona region, and she was named the best teacher in the Bombay Presidency in 1852 (Britannica). (d) Phule was from the Mali caste, which Britannica places in the Shudra varna.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Institutions: Calcutta Madrasa - Warren Hastings (1780/81); Sanskrit College, Benares - Jonathan Duncan (1791); College of Fort William - Wellesley (18 Aug 1800, dissolved 1854); Hindu College - 20 Jan 1817 (David Hare, a Scot).
  • Ram Mohan Roy: b. 22 May 1772 Radhanagar; d. 27 Sep 1833 Bristol; Atmiya Sabha (1814-15); Brahmo Sabha Aug 1828; Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin (1803-04, Persian); Precepts of Jesus (1820); Sambad Kaumudi (1821, Bengali); Mirat-ul-Akhbar (1822, Persian); Vedanta College (1826); Sati banned 1829; title Raja from Akbar II; press protest 1823 (John Adam's regulation; repealed by Metcalfe 1835).
  • Dharma Sabha: 1830, Radhakanta Deb (anti-reform).
  • Brahmo Samaj: Debendranath Tagore (Tattvabodhini Sabha 1839, Patrika 1843; rejected Vedic authority 1850); Keshab Chandra Sen (joined c. 1857-58); 1866 split: Adi Brahmo Samaj (Tagore) and Brahmo Samaj of India (Sen); 1878: Sadharan Brahmo Samaj (Anand Mohan Bose) after Keshab's daughter's marriage with Cooch Behar; 1872: Brahmo marriage law.
  • Paramahamsa Mandali: 1849, Bombay, secret; Dadoba Pandurang (and Mehtaji / Atmaram Pandurang). Prarthana Samaj: 31 Mar 1867, Bombay; Atmaram Pandurang; Ranade, R. G. Bhandarkar, N. G. Chandavarkar.
  • Deccan Education Society: 1884 (Agarkar, Tilak, Chiplunkar, Namjoshi); Fergusson College 1885. National Social Conference: 1887, Ranade; with Congress's 3rd session at Madras.
  • Vidyasagar: b. 26 Sep 1820, d. 29 Jul 1891; title from Sanskrit College; Widow Remarriage Act 1856; secretary of the Hindu Female School (Bethune School, 1849).
  • Derozio: b. 18 Apr 1809; Hindu College teacher 1826-31; Academic Association 1828; dismissed Apr 1831; d. 26 Dec 1831 (cholera). Phule: b. 11 Apr 1827; school 1848; Gulamgiri and Satyashodhak Samaj 1873; Mahatma 1888; d. 28 Nov 1890.

Mains Points

  • Renaissance: link modern education (1813 onwards) and the Western ideas of liberty and reason to reform, but stress Indian agency and the Bengal-centred, upper-caste limits of the "renaissance" (c01).
  • Roy's two-sided view of British rule: he welcomed the abolition of sati, English education and the press, and attacked white-only senior posts and press curbs; he combined social reform with the first organised political agitation (the press protest of 1823) (c08).
  • The Brahmo Samaj's trajectory: Roy's monotheism, Debendranath's rejection of Vedic authority, Keshab's cosmopolitanism, and the 1866 and 1878 splits show the tension between reform and tradition and between principle and personal leadership (c10, c11).
  • Bengal vs Bombay: the Prarthana Samaj avoided confrontation and kept caste and ritual; the Paramahamsa Mandali was secret; Phule attacked the caste order itself and educated lower-caste girls (c13, c18).
  • Limits: Young Bengal's radicalism was bookish and distant from the peasantry (a textbook judgement); the Social Conference worked through petitions and pledges, and the orthodox counter-movement (Dharma Sabha) mobilised more signatures against widow remarriage than Vidyasagar did.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Two Sanskrit Colleges: Benares (1791, Duncan) vs Calcutta (Vidyasagar's). Fort William College was in Calcutta and trained British officials; Haileybury is in England (c03, c04).
  • Brahmo Sabha (1828) vs Brahmo Samaj; Adi Brahmo Samaj vs Brahmo Samaj of India vs Sadharan Brahmo Samaj; Dwarkanath vs Debendranath Tagore (c07, c08, c09).
  • Paramahamsa Mandali (1849, secret) vs Prarthana Samaj (1867); Atmaram Pandurang vs Dadoba Pandurang (c12, c13).
  • Deccan Education Society 1884 (not 1824); National Social Conference (1887) is not the Congress.
  • Vidyasagar (Widow Remarriage, Bengal) vs Phule (caste, Pune); Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856 vs abolition of sati 1829; Derozio was a teacher, not a student.
  • Gulamgiri (1873) vs Satyashodhak Samaj (1873): both 1873, not 1871.
