1. The Deoband School

1.1 Foundation, founders and aim

  • Who and where (as in the notes): orthodox Muslim scholars - Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi - founded the school at Deoband in Saharanpur district, Uttar Pradesh (Map 1, no. 1; c01).
  • When: 31 May 1866 (15 Muharram 1283 AH) according to Wikipedia, which notes that some books give 30 May; a Muslim seminary named Darul Uloom ("House of Learning"), opened nine years after the Revolt of 1857 (c01). [Additional] It began in a mosque (the Chhatta Masjid) under a pomegranate tree; the first independent building came in 1879 and the foundation of the present campus was laid in 1875 (Wikipedia).
  • Aim (as in the notes): unlike the Aligarh school, Deoband did not prepare its students for jobs; the theme was religious and moral instruction. It was nationalist in orientation (c02).
  • Curriculum [Additional]: a modified version of the 18th-century Dars-i-Nizami: Quran and tafsir, hadis and Hanafi jurisprudence, taught in Urdu (with Arabic and Persian), but with classrooms, a fixed syllabus, examinations and public subscriptions (Wikipedia). It did not accept government funds [Verification Required].
  • Political stance (as in the notes): it welcomed the formation of the Indian National Congress (28 December 1885, Bombay) and issued a fatwa against Sir Saiyid Ahmad Khan's organisations - the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental Association and the United Patriotic Association (c03).

[Correction] "Did not teach for jobs"; "nationalist in orientation". (a) Deoband's stated aim was to preserve Islamic learning and practice after the end of Muslim political power and the Revolt of 1857, not to prepare for colonial service; this is a fair contrast with Aligarh but it does not mean that its graduates avoided all employment [Verification Required]. (b) "Nationalist" is a later description, strongest in the 20th century: graduates led the Silk Letters Movement (Mahmud Hasan, c. 1913-16), the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind (founded at Delhi in November 1919 [Verification Required]) and, through Husain Ahmad Madani, the "composite nationalism" position that opposed the Partition (Wikipedia). In the 1880s the seminary's leaders were not a single political voice (see c03). Present "nationalist" as the textbook view with this caveat.

[Correction] "Welcomed the Congress; fatwa against Sir Saiyid's organisations". (a) Textbook view: the standard exam books (for example Spectrum) say Deoband welcomed the Congress and issued a fatwa against Sir Saiyid's bodies; that is the line the teacher gave. (b) Scholarly caution: historians of Deoband record that in the late 1880s Gangohi and some other ulema advised Muslims against joining the Congress, broadly in line with Sir Saiyid, while the Deoband-linked Mahmud Hasan and Husain Ahmad Madani backed the Congress and the independence movement from the 1910s-20s. I could not open a source stating the date, text or issuer of an anti-Aligarh fatwa, so treat it as [Verification Required] and a contested point; attribute it as "the textbook view" in Mains answers. (c) Names of the targets: Sir Saiyid founded the United Indian Patriotic Association in 1888 (with Raja Shiv Prasad of Benares) to counter the Congress, and the Mohammedan Anglo-Oriental Defence Association of Upper India in 1893 [Verification Required] - the notes' "Mohammadan Anglo Oriental Association" and "United Patriotic Association" are shortened forms; both are not to be confused with the MAO College (1877) of 10-30, c21.

Exam point: Deoband = Darul Uloom, May 1866, Nanautavi and Gangohi (with Sayyid Abid), Saharanpur district. Religious training, no government funding, Urdu medium; textbook line: nationalist, welcomed the Congress, opposed Aligarh's political stand. Later: Mahmud Hasan (Silk Letters), Husain Ahmad Madani (composite nationalism).

2. Characteristic Features of All the Reform Movements

The notes list five features shared by the movements of the 19th century (the Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Arya Samaj, Ramakrishna Mission, Theosophical Society, Aligarh and Deoband). The five points below keep the teacher's order; the caveats are in c04 and c05.

  1. Rationalism and humanism. They criticised the irrational, superstitious and conservative elements of Indian society (idolatry, sati, caste rigidity, child marriage, and so on) (c04).
  2. Importance of education and knowledge. Illiteracy, especially of women, was criticised and seen as a main cause of India's backwardness.
  3. Change with continuity. They did not want to break down the existing structure of society completely; they wanted to remove distortion and deviation, keep the good elements and discard the bad, so they combined elements of change and continuity.
  4. Narrow social base. Their reforming spirit was confined to the educated middle class; for example, in Bengal their message was appreciated mainly by the bhadralok (respectable, educated, high-caste classes). The vast majority of illiterate masses in the villages were untouched by their ideas (c05).
  5. Appeal to the past. Some movements appealed to the past greatness of India and over-emphasised its achievements, which gave a sense of false pride (c05).

[Correction] "All these movements were characterised by rationalism and humanism." This is the textbook generalisation and is true of the Hindu reform movements and of the Aligarh school. It does not hold for the revivalist movements: the Arya Samaj treated the Vedas as infallible, Deoband and the Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya were orthodox, and the Theosophists included occult beliefs. In answers, write "most reform movements" or limit the claim to the reformist group (10-30 classification table). Humanism here means concern for the dignity of the individual (for example, women and low castes).

[Correction] "Narrow social base"; "false pride". (a) Narrow base: the classic exam-book view; it must be balanced. The Satyashodhak Samaj (Phule), the Wahabi and Faraizi movements and the later Self-Respect and Ezhava movements reached peasants and low castes, and the Arya Samaj spread widely in Punjab through the DAV and Gurukul. The "bhadralok" refers to Bengal, as the notes say. (b) "False pride": this is a judgement, not a fact; scholars argue that the appeal to the past was also a defence of self-respect against colonial and missionary criticism and helped nationalism (Britannica on Besant; 10-30, c07). Its flip side was that it fed religious exclusivism. Present both views in Mains (Mains Points).

3. National Movement / National Awakening

Rise of nationalism (as in the notes): the feeling of nationalism began to grow from the 1870s, mainly among the intelligentsia (c06). Four factors contributed:

1 Political and economic unification+2 Transport and communication+3 Western education+4 Press and literature→National awakening
1870s-1885

3.1 Political and economic unification

[Additional] (the notes gave only the heading). Under British rule almost the whole subcontinent came under one administration (the Company's territories, then the Crown after 1858), with a common legal and judicial system, a common civil service, a single currency and a single market. Economic exploitation also created a common grievance (the "drain of wealth", on which the Moderates wrote; see Naoroji below). Princely states remained under British paramountcy. Therefore it is colonial rule itself, together with the common opposition to it, that gave Indians a shared political identity - a point developed in the Dalhousie answer below. [Verification Required] against NCERT / Bipan Chandra for the wording of the "common grievance" thesis.

3.2 Improvement in means of transport and communication

The notes gave only the heading; the content comes from the class question below, plus [Additional] facts: the first passenger railway in India ran between Bombay and Thane on 16 April 1853 (about 34 km), the first line in the east (Howrah-Hooghly) followed in 1854 and Madras in 1856; the electric telegraph was introduced in 1851-54 (O'Shaughnessy) and the Post Office Act, 1854 introduced a uniform low rate of postage; the Public Works Department was set up in 1854 to build roads, canals and bridges. All dates [Verification Required] except where stated in the answer (c07).

Class question: "In any way Lord Dalhousie was the maker of modern India. Comment."

Answer as given in class (cleaned up):

  • Dalhousie, though an imperialist, could be held responsible for paving the way for a modern India.
  • Political unity: his policy of annexation brought several kingdoms under a common political unit. It was hated by contemporary rulers but in the long run contributed to modern India. People of these kingdoms were governed by the same set of laws, which forced a kind of unity among Indians.
  • Roads and communication: he started the Public Works Department (PWD), which constructed metalled roads and made communication easier and faster.
  • Railways: he introduced the railways, "one of the greatest unifying forces", which carried people in bulk and gave Indians of different regions the opportunity to share their views.
  • Balance: there is no denying that his policies strengthened British rule - he framed them to make the administration stronger. In the long run, however, the same policies contributed to unity among Indians; from this perspective he can be called a "maker of modern India" (c07).

[Additional] Structure for a Mains answer: (1) introduction (Governor-General 1848-56, imperialist); (2) political unification - annexation of the Punjab (1849), Lower Burma (1852), Doctrine of Lapse (Satara 1848, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854, among others) and Awadh (1856, on grounds of misgovernment); (3) communication - railways (1853), telegraph, uniform postage (1854), PWD (1854), Grand Trunk Road and the Ganges Canal (opened 1854); (4) education - Wood's Despatch (19 July 1854) leading to the universities of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1857) [Verification Required]; (5) the opposing view: the aim was imperial control, and the annexations helped provoke the Revolt of 1857; (6) conclusion. Remember the title Governor-General (Dalhousie was not a "Viceroy": that title began in 1858).

3.3 Western education

[Additional] (the notes gave only the heading). English education, introduced by Macaulay's Minute of 1835 and the 1857 universities, produced a class of Indians who knew English, read about liberty, equality and nationalism in Europe and used a common language across regions (it also later produced the leaders of the Congress). The same education taught that British rule claimed to rest on law and rights, which gave a ground to criticise it. [Verification Required] for dates against NCERT.

[Correction] "Nationalism started from the 1870s." A useful date for the organised phase: the Indian Association (26 July 1876) and the Ilbert and Vernacular Press controversies of the late 1870s-80s, culminating in the Indian National Congress (28 December 1885, Bombay, 72 members, president W. C. Bonnerjee: Britannica). Political associations (the Landholders' Society 1837, the British Indian Association 1851, the Madras Native Association 1852) existed earlier, and the Revolt of 1857 was a form of resistance, so "from the 1870s" means modern national consciousness among the educated, not the first anti-British feeling. Britannica calls the Indian Association a forerunner of the Congress, and the East India Association a "forerunner" as well.

[Correction] Dalhousie answer: facts and terms. (a) Official title: Dalhousie was Governor-General (1848-56), not a Viceroy. (b) "PWD ... metal roads": the Public Works Department was set up in 1854 ([Verification Required]); "metalled" means surfaced with broken stone; the notes' "metal roads" is the same. (c) "Same set of laws": the annexed areas received Company regulations (the Punjab, for example, under a Board of Administration, 1849-53); a uniform law across British India is better dated to the Indian Penal Code (drafted 1837, enacted 1860 - [Verification Required]) and the Crown rule of 1858. (d) Interpretation: "maker of modern India" is a contested phrase; Dalhousie's annexations (especially the Doctrine of Lapse and Awadh) were also a cause of the Revolt of 1857 (27 October note). (e) Railways were intended mainly to move troops, raw cotton and goods; their unifying effect on Indians was an unintended consequence.

3.5 Associations: Indian Association (1876) and East India Association (1866)

  • Indian Association: founded by Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose in July 1876 at Calcutta (Map 1, no. 4) - 26 July 1876 (Albert Hall) per the Testbook snippet, [Verification Required] (c18). Britannica: it favoured local self-government, was supported mainly by younger professionals of the Bengali intelligentsia, and wished to create a strong public opinion, unite Indians on common political ideals, promote Hindu-Muslim harmony and include the masses.
  • Campaigns [Additional]: against the lowering of the ICS entry age from 21 to 19 (1877; a mass meeting at Calcutta in March 1877 and Banerjea's tour of Amritsar, Lahore, Allahabad, Lucknow, Kanpur, Varanasi, Bombay and Madras), the Vernacular Press Act and the Arms Act (1878), and for the Ilbert Bill; it also held national conferences in 1883 and 1885 (Britannica) and merged in effect into the Congress. It supported tenants' rights (Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885).
  • Coolies / indentured labourers (as in the notes): the Indian Association raised the concerns of the indentured labourers (coolies) of the Assam tea plantations [Verification Required] (a source opened for the Association did not mention it; the usual textbook link is to Dwarkanath Ganguly's campaign of the 1880s).
  • East India Association: "ten years earlier" than 1876: founded in London by Dadabhai Naoroji to raise Indian concerns before the British public (Britannica: 1867, about 600 members, including retired British officials; many books give 1866) (c18).

Mains Points

  • Features of reform movements: they were limited by their narrow base and by their appeal to the past, but they changed attitudes to women, caste and education; an even-handed answer gives both (c04, c05).
  • Muslim responses compared: Deoband (religious preservation, later nationalism), Aligarh (modern education, loyalty) and the Wahabi movement (jihad): three strategies after 1857 (table; 10-30). Treat the Deoband-Congress relationship as contested (c03).
  • Dalhousie: argue from intent vs consequence: the imperial motive of annexation, railways and the telegraph (control, trade, troops) vs the unintended unity, communication and administrative integration that nationalism later used (c07).
  • Role of the press: newspapers formed public opinion, linked regions and languages and trained leaders (Tilak, Banerjea, Gandhi, Azad, Ambedkar); the Vernacular Press Act (1878) was a reaction to them (c14).
  • Associations: Indian Association (1876) as an all-India precursor of the Congress; Naoroji's East India Association shows that the early agitation had a base in London as well as in India (c18).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Deoband (religious, Urdu, 1866) vs Aligarh (modern education, 1875/77); Deoband is not "Devganj" (c01); do not assert a single Deoband view of the Congress (c03).
  • Dalhousie = Governor-General (not Viceroy); the railways began in 1853 (his time), the telegraph and PWD 1854 (c07).
  • East India Association (London, Naoroji) vs British Indian Association (Calcutta, 1851) vs Indian Association (Calcutta, 1876).
  • Madras Mahajana Sabha (1884, Subramania Iyer) vs Madras Native Association (1852, Lakshminarasu Chetty) (2017 PYQ; c08).
  • Rast Goftar (Gujarati) vs Voice of India (English) (c10); Kesari (Marathi) vs Maratha (English).
  • Harijan not "Harichand"; Independent not "Independence" (c15, c16).
  • Stuttgart = flag (1907), not a journal; Free Hindustan in Vancouver (c17).
  • Reform movements were not all rationalist (c04).

30-Second Revision

Deoband: Darul Uloom, May 1866, Nanautavi and Gangohi, Saharanpur district; religious, Urdu, no jobs; textbook: nationalist, welcomed Congress. Reform movements: rationalism, education, change-and-continuity, narrow base, glorified past. Awakening (1870s): unification, transport/communication, Western education, press. Dalhousie: annexation, PWD, railways, but imperial motive. Press: Hindu / Swadesamitran (Iyer), Kesari / Maratha (Tilak), Bengalee (Banerjea), Amrita Bazar Patrika (Ghoshes), Gandhi's three, Comrade, Al-Hilal, Bahishkrit Bharat, overseas press (Das, Cama, Varma). Associations: East India (1866-67, Naoroji), Indian Association (26 July 1876).
