5. Significance of the Swadeshi Movement (continued)

5.2 Significance (continued) - point 6: the cultural field

  1. The movement showed itself in culture as well. Nationalist literature, songs and music were composed.
    • It was during this time (1905) that Rabindranath Tagore wrote Amar Sonar Bangla ("My golden Bengal") (c01). [Additional] It was sung at the anti-partition protests of 16 October 1905 and is now the national anthem of Bangladesh (adopted 1971-72).
    • His nephew Abanindranath Tagore painted the famous Bharat Mata (1905) (c02). The painting reflected the collective aspirations of contemporary Indians: a saffron-clad woman with four arms holding a book, sheaves of paddy, a white cloth and a rosary - learning, food, clothing and spiritual life.
    • [Additional] Other cultural landmarks: "Vande Mataram" (Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, Anandamath, 1882) became the movement's slogan; songs of Rajanikanta Sen, D. L. Roy and Mukunda Das; the Bengal School of Art led by Abanindranath (Nandalal Bose and others) turned from Victorian academic painting to Mughal, Rajput and Ajanta traditions; the Indian Society of Oriental Art (1907).

[Correction] "Amar sonar Bangle" = Amar Sonar Bangla. It was composed in 1905 in protest against the partition of Bengal (the exact month is variously given as August or September 1905 [Verification Required]).

[Correction] "Abrindranath tagore" = Abanindranath Tagore (1871-1951), nephew of Rabindranath. The painting was first called Banga Mata (Mother Bengal) and was renamed Bharat Mata as it came to stand for India; its date is usually given as 1905 (some catalogues say c. 1904-05) [Verification Required].

5.4 Limitations of the Swadeshi movement

  1. The movement was largely confined to the urban middle classes (and the educated Hindu gentry, the bhadralok) of Bengal.
  2. The peasants - mostly Muslims living in the rural areas of East Bengal - remained aloof. [Additional] Many landlords and moneylenders were Hindu, the leaders did not take up peasant grievances such as rent, and the use of Hindu religious symbols narrowed its appeal (Sumit Sarkar). Swadeshi goods were also dearer than foreign ones.
  3. The British government suppressed the movement; most leaders were detained or deported (see 9.1).
  4. During the movement the government also encouraged the idea of a separate Muslim political party, which led to the birth of the All-India Muslim League on 30 December 1906 at Dacca (Dhaka) (c03; Map 2, places 1-2).
  5. At the peak of the movement there was infighting among the leaders, which ended in the Surat split (December 1907) (see 8).
  6. [Additional] It lacked an effective organisation and discipline - the Extremists could not create one, and the movement had no single all-India leadership; by 1908 the open movement had faded, and some young people turned to revolutionary terrorism.

[Correction] "The British govt popped up the idea of a separate Muslim party". Better: the government encouraged Muslim separatist politics. Who/when/where: a deputation of about 35 Muslim leaders led by the Aga Khan III met Viceroy Lord Minto at Simla on 1 October 1906, asking for separate electorates and representation by "political importance"; Minto responded sympathetically. The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dacca on 30 December 1906, at a meeting hosted by Nawab Salimullah, with Viqar-ul-Mulk presiding. Interpretations: nationalist historians call the Simla Deputation a "command performance" arranged with officials (Minto's private secretary Dunlop Smith); others stress the genuine anxieties of the Muslim elite about elections. Present both views; the government's sympathy is well documented, its exact role is debated.

7. Calcutta Session of the Congress (December 1906; President: Dadabhai Naoroji)

  • The Calcutta session was historic because the sitting president, Dadabhai Naoroji, declared Swaraj to be the goal of the Congress - the first time it was demanded from the Congress platform. [Additional] He defined it as self-government "like that of the United Kingdom or the colonies" - which the Moderates read as colonial self-government and the Extremists as a step to full freedom.
  • It passed four resolutions:
    1. Swadeshi
    2. Swaraj (self-government)
    3. National Education
    4. Boycott
  • [Additional] The resolutions were a compromise; the Moderates wanted to read boycott narrowly and soon tried to dilute them - one cause of the 1907 clash. Venue: Map 2, place 3.

8. The Surat Split (December 1907)

  • The differences above led to the Surat split in December 1907 (session of 26-27 December).
  • [Additional] Why Surat? The 1907 session was due at Nagpur, an Extremist stronghold; the Moderates moved it to Surat in Tilak's home province, where by convention he could not be president. The Extremists wanted Lajpat Rai as president and the four Calcutta resolutions reaffirmed; the Moderates proposed Rash Behari Ghosh. The session broke up in chaos (Map 2, places 4-5).
  • The Moderates captured the organisation and the Extremists were shut out; Rash Behari Ghosh was the Moderates' president (c04).
  • The British were very pleased with the outcome and followed a dual policy - see 9.

[Correction] (a) "Raj Bihari Ghosh" = Rash Behari Ghosh (1845-1921), lawyer and Moderate leader - president-elect of the aborted Surat session (1907) and president of the Madras session (December 1908); do not confuse him with the revolutionary Rash Behari Bose. (b) "Expelled the extremists": there was no formal expulsion. A Moderate convention at Surat and the Allahabad Convention (April 1908) framed a Congress constitution requiring members to accept self-government "by constitutional means" - which kept the Extremists out until they were readmitted at Lucknow (1916).

9. The British Dual Policy: Repression and Conciliation

The British followed a dual policy - often summed up as "rally the Moderates" (Minto) or the "carrot and stick": (a) suppress the Extremists and (b) pacify (conciliate) the Moderates. [Additional] Nationalist historians add a third element - divide and rule through separate electorates for Muslims (9.2).

9.2 (b) Pacification of the Moderates: the Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms)

To pacify the Moderates the government passed the Indian Councils Act, 1909, known as the Morley-Minto reforms after John Morley (Secretary of State for India) and Lord Minto (Viceroy, 1905-10) (c09).

Provisions

  1. It introduced elections to the legislative councils, though these were indirect in nature (c10; Figure 2).
    • Members of local bodies (municipal committees, district boards) elected an electoral college, which elected members of the provincial legislative councils; the non-official members of the provincial councils in turn elected members of the Imperial (central) Legislative Council. Landholders, chambers of commerce, universities and Muslims had separate constituencies.
  2. [Additional] The councils were enlarged: the Imperial Legislative Council from 16 to 60 additional members (with an official majority kept at the centre); the larger provincial councils to up to 50, with a non-official majority in the provinces.
  3. The legislatures were only advisory bodies with no control over the executive. [Additional] Members could discuss the budget, ask supplementary questions and move resolutions, but these were not binding; there was no responsible government.
  4. An Indian was to be a member of the Viceroy's (central) Executive Council: Satyendra Prasanna (S. P.) Sinha became the first Indian member, as Law Member (1909). [Additional] Indians were also admitted to provincial executive councils, and two Indians (K. G. Gupta and Syed Husain Bilgrami) had been appointed to the Secretary of State's Council in London in 1907.
  5. It introduced separate electorates for Muslims - seats reserved for Muslims, for which only Muslim voters voted. The aim was presented as protecting the interests of Muslims; nationalists saw it as promoting Muslim loyalty to the British and dividing Indians (c11). [Additional] Muslims also got weightage (more seats than their population share) and lower voting qualifications; Minto is called the "Father of Communal Electorate".

10. The Delhi Durbar (12 December 1911)

  • In 1911, during the viceroyalty of Lord Hardinge II (1910-16), a Durbar was held at Delhi, attended by King George V and Queen Mary - the first visit to India by a reigning British monarch, held to proclaim their coronation.
  • Two famous announcements were made (by the King himself):
    1. The partition of Bengal was annulled (revoked) - Bengal was reunited.
    2. The capital was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi (c12).
  • [Additional] Also announced: Bihar and Orissa became a separate province (capital Patna) and Assam again a Chief Commissioner's province (effective 1 April 1912). Delhi was made a separate province in 1912; New Delhi was formally inaugurated in 1931.
  • [Additional] Why? Official reasons: to end the agitation in Bengal and to give the capital a central, historic seat. Nationalist reading: moving away from Calcutta also moved the government away from the centre of Bengali political agitation. Muslim leaders in East Bengal resented the annulment.
  • [Additional] On 23 December 1912 a bomb was thrown at Hardinge during his state entry into Delhi (the Delhi Conspiracy Case; Rash Behari Bose was the organiser).

[Correction] "Capital was shifted to Delhi from Bengal" - from Calcutta (Kolkata), which was the capital of British India and of the Bengal presidency. "Harding" = Lord Hardinge (Charles Hardinge, Hardinge of Penshurst), not to be confused with Governor-General Henry Hardinge (1844-48). The Durbar was planned by the Government of India; the announcements were made by George V.

11. The Home Rule Movement (1916-1918)

[Additional] What "Home Rule" meant: self-government for India within the British Empire, modelled on the Irish Home Rule leagues - not complete independence.

11.1 Tilak's Home Rule League (April 1916)

  • Tilak returned to public life in June 1914, on release from Mandalay, and tried to get re-entry into the Congress for himself and his followers, but did not succeed in that year (c13).
  • He also noticed that the Congress had become a weak organisation that had lost touch with the masses. Therefore, in April 1916, he started his Home Rule League - first meeting at Belgaum, headquarters at Poona (Map 5, places 1-2).
  • It was a movement to spread awareness among the people about self-rule. [Additional] Its demands included Swaraj, linguistic provinces and education in the vernacular; it worked through six branches. Tilak's slogan "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it" was popularised in 1916.

11.2 Annie Besant's All-India Home Rule League (September 1916)

  • Annie Besant (1847-1933), the Irish-born Theosophist who had joined the Congress in 1914, was a great supporter of unity between the Moderates and Extremists. Since early 1915 she had been popularising the demand for self-rule through her two newspapers, New India (daily) and Commonweal (weekly).
  • She started her own league in September 1916 at Madras (Adyar) [Additional] with George Arundale as organising secretary, and B. P. Wadia and C. P. Ramaswami Aiyar.
  • To avoid confusion, the two leaders demarcated their areas:

11.3 Repression and decline

  • As the movement became popular, Besant was arrested (interned) in June 1917 (c15). This was one reason the movement gradually started fading. [Additional] Her internment caused a nationwide protest - Sir S. Subramania Iyer renounced his knighthood - and she was released in September 1917 and elected President of the Calcutta Congress (December 1917), the first woman to hold the post.
  • [Additional] Other reasons for the decline: the Moderates were satisfied by the August Declaration (1917) and the prospect of reforms (13); Besant herself wavered over the reforms and over passive resistance; there was no effective organisation after 1918; and leadership passed to Gandhi.
  • Tilak went to London (from September 1918) to fight a libel case against Sir Valentine Chirol, who in his book Indian Unrest (1910) had called Tilak the "Father of Indian Unrest" (c16; Map 3, place 3). [Additional] Tilak lost the case in 1919.
  • Members of G. K. Gokhale's Servants of India Society (founded 1905, for social service) were not allowed to join the Home Rule Leagues (c17).

[Correction] "Tilak returned to India in 1914" - he was released from Mandalay jail (Burma, then part of British India) in June 1914. His re-entry: the Moderates (Pherozeshah Mehta's group) blocked it in 1914-15; after Mehta's death (1915) the Bombay session (December 1915, S. P. Sinha) amended the Congress constitution to admit delegates from other bodies, and the Extremists formally returned at Lucknow (December 1916).

[Correction] "Annie Besant's HRM was in rest of world" - it covered the rest of India, including Bombay city.

[Correction] "Arrested in June 1917" - more precisely, the Madras government interned her (with her associates George Arundale and B. P. Her release came in the same weeks as the August Declaration, and her popularity rose rather than fell, so "the movement faded because of her arrest" is only part of the story.

[Correction] "Wellington Chirole" = Sir Valentine Chirol (1852-1929), journalist of The Times; the book is Indian Unrest (1910), in which he called Tilak the "Father of Indian Unrest". Tilak sued for libel in London; the case was decided against him in 1919 (exact date [Verification Required]).

[Correction] "GK Gokhle (for social service) members of Indian society" = members of the Servants of India Society, founded by G. K. Gokhale at Poona in 1905 to train national workers for social service. Gokhale himself had died in February 1915, before the leagues began; the Society's rules barred its members from joining.

11.4 Significance of the Home Rule Movement

  1. It gave fresh energy to the national movement when it was passing through a phase of despondency (after the Surat split and repression, 1908-14).
  2. Several of its leaders played an important role later, when the national movement entered its truly mass phase under Gandhi.
  3. It provided an organisational network linking the towns and the countryside - the network later used by Gandhi (c18).
  4. Some of its leaders helped mobilise peasants to set up their own associations, the Kisan Sabhas; this happened mostly in Awadh. Example: the UP Kisan Sabha (February 1918) [Additional] - founded by Home Rule leaders Gauri Shankar Misra and Indra Narain Dwivedi with Madan Mohan Malaviya's support; the Awadh Kisan Sabha (October 1920) followed, with Baba Ramchandra.
  5. The Home Rule movement and other developments of the period compelled the British government to announce new constitutional reforms - the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms, or Montford reforms (report, 1918; Act, 1919) (c19).
  6. [Additional] It shifted the emphasis from the educated elite to the masses, created a generation of new leaders, and helped the Moderate-Extremist reunion and the Lucknow Pact (1916).

[Correction] "Organisational network between rural areas and HRM" - the standard point (Bipan Chandra) is that it built organisational links between town and country, which proved valuable when Gandhi launched mass movements.

[Correction] "Montago-Flemsford reforms 1918" = the Montagu-Chelmsford (Montford) reforms, after Edwin Montagu (Secretary of State, 1917-22) and Lord Chelmsford (Viceroy, 1916-21). The Report on Indian Constitutional Reforms was published in July 1918; the reforms were enacted by the Government of India Act, 1919 (royal assent 23 December 1919) and came into force in 1921.

12. Factors behind the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms

  1. World War I (1914-18) - Britain needed Indian men, money and goodwill; the war (fought "for democracy and self-determination") raised Indian expectations. [Additional] About 1.3 million Indians served overseas [Verification Required].
  2. 1916 - unity at Lucknow (Congress session of December 1916, President Ambika Charan Mazumdar):
    • the Moderates and Extremists were reunited;
    • the Congress and the Muslim League came together in the Lucknow Pact; Muhammad Ali Jinnah (then a member of both) was its key architect, with Tilak;
    • Sarojini Naidu called Jinnah the "Ambassador of Hindu-Muslim Unity".
  3. [Additional] The Home Rule agitation (11) and the government's wish to win back the Moderates.

[Additional] Terms of the Lucknow Pact. Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims (and weightage in provinces where they were a minority; one-third of the elected Indian seats at the centre). Joint demands: an early declaration of self-government; enlarged legislatures with elected majorities and more powers; a five-year term; half the members of executive councils to be Indians; the Secretary of State's salary to be paid by the British Treasury. Assessment: it was a landmark of Hindu-Muslim political cooperation, but by accepting separate electorates the Congress also recognised communal politics - historians stress both sides.

13. The August Declaration (20 August 1917)

The rough notes asked: "Add details of this". This whole section is [Additional].

  • Who and where: Edwin Montagu, the new Secretary of State for India, in the House of Commons, London, on 20 August 1917 (also called the Montagu Declaration). Viceroy: Lord Chelmsford.
  • What it said: the policy of His Majesty's Government was "the increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration and the gradual development of self-governing institutions with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire."
  • Conditions: progress would come in successive stages; the British Government and the Government of India - not Indians - would judge the timing and extent of each step.
  • Why it was made: the war, the Home Rule agitation, the Lucknow Pact and the Mesopotamian Commission's criticism of the Indian administration (Montagu's predecessor Austen Chamberlain resigned in July 1917).
  • Significance: the first official statement that responsible government was the goal of British policy; after it, the demand for self-government could no longer be treated as sedition.
  • Limitations: no timeframe; "responsible government" in stages, not Swaraj or dominion status; the British kept the right to decide the pace.
  • Indian response: the Moderates welcomed it; the Home Rulers and Extremists called it inadequate. Montagu toured India with Chelmsford (November 1917 - April 1918); the Montagu-Chelmsford Report came out in July 1918; a special Congress session at Bombay (August-September 1918, President Syed Hasan Imam) called the proposals "disappointing and unsatisfactory"; Moderates led by Surendranath Banerjea left to form the Indian National Liberal Federation (1918-19).

14. The Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)

[Additional] Royal assent 23 December 1919; in force from 1921 (first elections November 1920, boycotted by the Congress during Non-Cooperation).

Provisions

  1. The salary of the Secretary of State for India would be paid from the British Treasury and not from Indian revenues.
  2. Some of his powers and functions were transferred to a new official, the High Commissioner for India in London, who was appointed and paid by the Government of India. [Additional] He took over agency work such as purchasing stores and looking after Indian students; the first High Commissioner, Sir William Meyer, took office in 1920.
  3. It demarcated and separated the central and provincial subjects, with two lists - a central list and a provincial list (c20). A subject not found in the provincial list automatically fell in the central list (residuary power with the centre). [Additional] Provincial budgets were separated from the central budget, but government remained centralised and unitary.
  4. Separate electorates were extended to Sikhs, Anglo-Indians, Indian Christians and Europeans (c21).
  5. The ICS examination would also be held in India - from 1922, first at Allahabad (c22; Map 2, place 6). [Additional] The Act also provided for a Public Service Commission; India's first, the forerunner of the UPSC, was constituted on 1 October 1926 (Lee Commission, 1924).
  6. It introduced bicameralism at the centre: the lower house, the Central (Indian) Legislative Assembly, and the upper house, the Council of State. [Additional] The Assembly had 145 members (104 elected) and the Council of State 60 (33 elected); most elected members were chosen by direct election, on a franchise limited by property, tax or education (only a few per cent of adults). The Viceroy could certify bills, restore cut grants and issue ordinances. Members' figures as usually given [Verification Required] for the Council of State.
  7. It introduced dyarchy ("double government") in the provinces. Provincial subjects were divided into two groups (c23; Figure 4):
    • Reserved subjects - the more important ones: finance, law and order, police, justice, land revenue, irrigation, etc. They were administered by the Governor with his Executive Council, who were not responsible to the legislature.
    • Transferred subjects - the less important ones: education, health, agriculture, local self-government (also public works, excise, industries). They were administered by the Governor with Ministers chosen from the elected members of the provincial legislative council and responsible to it.
    • [Additional] Dyarchy applied in eight Governor's provinces (Madras, Bombay, Bengal, the United Provinces, the Punjab, Bihar and Orissa, the Central Provinces and Assam). The Act itself defined only the transferred subjects; "reserved subjects" were the provincial subjects other than those transferred.
  8. [Additional] Three of the six members of the Viceroy's Executive Council (other than the Commander-in-Chief) were to be Indians.
  9. [Additional] A statutory commission was to review the working of the Act after ten years - this became the Simon Commission (1927).
  10. [Additional] Provincial legislatures could give women the vote; Madras did so first (1921).

[Additional] Why dyarchy failed. Finance was a reserved subject, so ministers had responsibility without money; the Governor could override them; ministers were not collectively responsible; the franchise was narrow. Tilak called the reforms "unworthy and disappointing - a sunless dawn"; Congress rejected them and, with the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh (1919), moved to Non-Cooperation. The Act of 1935 ended provincial dyarchy (and proposed dyarchy at the centre).

[Correction] "It completely separated the central and provincial subjects" - it demarcated them and relaxed central control, but did not make India federal: the Governor-General in Council kept overriding powers. "Ceded in central list" = fell within the central list. (UPSC 2015 tested exactly this point - PYQ 3.)

[Correction] "Sikh, Anglo-Indians and Indian born Christians" - the usual list is Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans.

[Correction] The ICS examination in India was conceded after the war and the Montford reforms; it was not a clause of the Act itself. UPSC's own history confirms that the examination was held in India from 1922, first at Allahabad (moved to Delhi later); the month "February 1922" could not be confirmed [Verification Required].

[Correction] Who administered what. Reserved subjects: the Governor acting with his Executive Council (not the Governor alone). Transferred subjects: the Governor acting with Ministers, who were chosen from the elected members and responsible to the legislative council - not "elected Indian members" in general. "Polic" = police.

  • Indian Councils Act 1909: indirect elections; councils enlarged; separate electorates for Muslims; S. P. Sinha first Indian (Law Member) on the Viceroy's Executive Council.

  • Home Rule Leagues: Tilak - April 1916, Belgaum/Poona, Maharashtra (minus Bombay city), Karnataka, C.P. and Berar; Besant - September 1916, Madras, rest of India; renamed Swarajya Sabha (1920).

  • Lucknow 1916: President A. C. Mazumdar; reunion; Lucknow Pact; Congress accepted separate electorates.
  • August Declaration 20 Aug 1917: "progressive realisation of responsible government".
  • GoI Act 1919: dyarchy in provinces; bicameral centre (Legislative Assembly + Council of State); separate electorates extended; High Commissioner in London; Secretary of State paid from British revenues; statutory commission after 10 years; ICS exam in India from 1922 (Allahabad).

Mains Points

  • Swadeshi - achievements vs limits: new mass base (students, women), new methods (boycott, national education, samitis) and cultural nationalism; but an urban, upper-caste, Hindu middle-class base, weak peasant and Muslim participation, no organisation, repression and the Surat split.
  • Moderate-Extremist split as a strategic debate: scope of boycott, area of agitation, pace of struggle; the split weakened both and allowed "rally the Moderates".
  • Dual policy: repression (Regulation III of 1818, press and meeting laws, sedition trials) plus conciliation (1909 reforms) plus separate electorates - "divide and rule" in the nationalist reading; officials saw it as balancing interests.
  • 1909 vs 1919: from indirect, advisory councils to partial responsibility (dyarchy) - but finance and law and order stayed reserved; separate electorates widened.
  • Home Rule movement as a bridge: revived politics in 1914-18, linked town and country, trained Gandhian-era leaders, helped the Lucknow reunion and Pact, and pushed the British to the August Declaration.
  • Role of foreigners in the freedom struggle (Mains 2013): Annie Besant (Home Rule, Congress presidency), Sister Nivedita (Swadeshi culture, Bharat Mata), A. O. Hume, C. F. Andrews, Madeleine Slade.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Rash Behari Ghosh (Moderate, Surat/Madras) ≠ Rash Behari Bose (revolutionary; Delhi bomb 1912; INA).
  • Reform pairs are named Secretary of State first, Viceroy second: Morley-Minto; Montagu-Chelmsford.
  • Indian Councils Act (1861, 1892, 1909) ≠ India Council (the Secretary of State's council in London).
  • Dyarchy: provinces in 1919; proposed at the centre in 1935 (never implemented). Bicameralism at the centre: 1919.
  • Separate electorates: Muslims 1909; extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans 1919; accepted by Congress in the Lucknow Pact 1916.
  • Durbars: 1877 (Lytton), 1903 (Curzon), 1911 (Hardinge; George V present).
  • Capital moved from Calcutta (announced 1911); New Delhi inaugurated 1931.
  • Tilak's league: Maharashtra excluding Bombay city. Besant founded neither the Theosophical Society (Blavatsky and Olcott, 1875) nor the Congress.
  • Gandhi did not found either league; he became president of Besant's league in 1920 (Swarajya Sabha).
  • New India: Bipin Chandra Pal's weekly (1901) and Besant's daily (1914) - two different papers.
  • Mandalay was a prison (Tilak 1908-14) and a place of deportation (Lajpat Rai, Ajit Singh 1907) - Ajit Singh's later exile was in Persia and Europe.
  • Montagu-Chelmsford Report 1918 vs Act 1919 vs in force 1921.

30-Second Revision

Swadeshi reached culture (Amar Sonar Bangla, Bharat Mata, 1905) and spread to Delhi, Punjab, Andhra and Madras, but stayed urban, middle-class and weak among Muslim peasants; the government encouraged the Muslim League (Dacca, 1906). Moderates and Extremists split over boycott, area and the 1906 presidency; Calcutta 1906 (Naoroji) adopted Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education; Surat 1907 split the Congress. The British repressed Extremists (Tilak - Mandalay; Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh - deported; Aurobindo - Alipore case) and conciliated Moderates with the 1909 Act (indirect elections, S. P. Sinha, separate electorates). 1911 Durbar: partition annulled, capital to Delhi. Home Rule 1916 (Tilak - April, Belgaum; Besant - September, Madras) revived politics, linked town and country and fed into Lucknow 1916, the August Declaration (20 Aug 1917) and the 1919 Act - dyarchy, bicameral centre, wider separate electorates.
