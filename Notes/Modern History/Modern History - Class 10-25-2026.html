1. Constitutional History: From Trading Company to Parliamentary Control

1.1 Rise of the East India Company

  • The EIC was established in 1599 (a group of London merchants).
  • On 31 December 1600 it received a Royal Charter from Queen Elizabeth I: the exclusive right to trade with the East Indies for an initial period of 15 years.
  • The Company "received India" in 1608: its first ship reached Surat that year (see c01).
  • Proprietors (shareholders) issued the capital and elected the directors who ran the company; the 24 directors collectively formed the Court of Directors (COD).

[Correction] "East Asia", "1608" and the charter. The charter gave a monopoly of trade with the East Indies (the lands east of the Cape of Good Hope, not "East Asia" alone). The Company was formed in December 1599 and the charter of 31 December 1600 is the founding document; the first term was 15 years, and a later charter (1609, James I) made it open-ended with a three-year notice clause [Verification Required]. "Received India in 1608" means that the first Company ship, the Hector under Captain William Hawkins, anchored at Surat in August 1608; Hawkins reached Jahangir's court in 1609 and the Mughal farman for a Surat factory came in 1613, with Sir Thomas Roe at the court in 1615-19 [Verification Required] on exact dates. The Company did not "receive" India: it received permission to trade at Surat. The settlements that became the Presidencies (Madras, Bombay, Calcutta) are on Map 2 and are described in the 10-23 file.

[Additional] How the Company was organised in Britain. Court of Proprietors (shareholders; the electorate) -> elected the Court of Directors (24 members; the executive). Before 1773 the directors were elected annually; the Regulating Act changed this (see c02). The Company ran its commercial and, after 1765, its territorial affairs.

1.2 Why Parliament Became Concerned

  • Edmund Burke (philosopher and MP): the fate of the people of Bengal should not be handed over to a private company like the EIC. He later led the impeachment of Warren Hastings (the trial began on 13 February 1788; Hastings was acquitted on 24 April 1795).
  • Adam Smith (The Wealth of Nations, 1776) attacked monopolies of chartered companies and argued for laissez-faire (free trade, minimum state control). This pressure later ended the Company's monopoly (1813: India trade; 1833: China trade and tea).
  • Nabobs - the English form of Nawabs; the nickname for Company officials who returned to Britain with fortunes made in India (after Plassey especially), who bought land and seats in Parliament and were resented.
  • After the Glorious Revolution (1688) established the supremacy of Parliament, MPs questioned the activities of the Company (private trade, plunder, wars, corruption).
  • Parliament formed two committees (a Select Committee and a Secret Committee) to find out what was happening in Asia; on the basis of their reports, the Regulating Acts were passed - the Regulating Act 1773 for the supervision of the Company and its governors in India.

[Correction] Attribution of the Burke and Smith points. The notes record Burke's view as a paraphrase; Burke's argument (strongest in his 1783 speech on Fox's India Bill and in the Hastings trial) was that the Company's charter was a trust for the governed, not a private property [Verification Required] on the exact wording. Laissez-faire is a phrase from the French physiocrats; Smith's own phrase was the "invisible hand" and "system of natural liberty"; he is the thinker linked with the end of company monopolies. The notes' "which was later came to an end of monopoly" is kept as the 1813 / 1833 sequence (sections 1813 and 1833). Sources: Warren Hastings, Wikipedia (impeachment dates).

[Correction] The "two committees" and their dates. Two sets of parliamentary inquiries exist: the Select and Secret Committees of 1772-73 (before the Regulating Act) [Verification Required] on the chairmen, and the Select and Secret Committees of 1781, which investigated the relations between the Supreme Court and the Council and the Maratha war, and whose reports fed Pitt's India Act of 1784 (Pitt's India Act, Wikipedia). The notes' statement that "regulation acts" followed the reports is correct for both. [Additional] The 1773 background also included the Company's financial crisis: unable to meet its payments since about 1768, it owed money to the Bank of England and the government, and the Regulating Act (with the Tea Act 1773) relieved it by a loan (Regulating Act 1773, Wikipedia).

1.3 Regulating Act 1773

The first attempt of the British Parliament to regulate the affairs of the Company - hence the name. Passed under Lord North (royal assent 21 June 1773); it came into force in 1773-74.

Regulating Act 1773 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome (requested in the notes)
Background
  • After Plassey (1757) and the Diwani (1765), the Company became a territorial power but was run by merchants with no experience of governing; officials made large private fortunes (Nabobs).
  • The Bengal famine of 1770 and the dual government exposed the failure of its administration.
  • By 1773 the Company was in financial crisis and needed a government loan.
  • Parliament's Select and Secret Committees (see c04) reported on abuses.
Reasons
  • Control the Company's conduct and corruption and bring Indian affairs under the supervision of the Crown.
  • Give unity to the three separate Presidencies (Bengal, Madras, Bombay), which acted independently and sometimes in conflict.
  • Rescue the Company financially without taking over its territories.
Provisions (as in the notes)
  1. In England: the proprietors would elect the directors for 4 years; 24 directors collectively form the Court of Directors (COD).
  2. The governors were required to submit all reports received from India to the British government.
  3. In India the Governor of Bengal became Governor-General of Bengal, assisted by a council of 4 members (the 4 + 1 structure = Governor-General in Council / Executive Council). Decisions by majority; in a tie the Governor-General had a casting vote; 3 members formed the quorum.
  4. The Governors of Bombay and Madras were subordinated to the Governor-General in Council.
  5. A Supreme Court was to be set up at Calcutta with a Chief Justice and three judges (established 1774; first Chief Justice Sir Elijah Impey).
Outcome / defects
  • Beginning of Parliamentary oversight and the first step to a centralised administration (Governor-General of Bengal -> of India in 1833).
  • It ended dual government in Bengal and (by banning private trade and presents) tried to curb corruption.
  • Defects: the Governor-General could be outvoted by his council (Hastings against Philip Francis and others); the Supreme Court's jurisdiction was unclear and clashed with the Council; the Bombay and Madras governors were not fully controlled; the Company's directors kept much autonomy. These led to the Amending Act (1781) and Pitt's India Act.

Warren Hastings. Last Governor of Bengal (1772-73) and first Governor-General of Bengal (1773-85). The notes' "rules and regulations were made by the 4 + 1 members (the Governor-General's council)" refers to the Council's power to make regulations for the good order of the Company's settlements.

[Correction] Directors' terms and "proprietors". Before 1773 the 24 directors were elected for one year; the Act changed this to four-year terms with one-quarter (six) retiring each year (the first set served 1, 2, 3 and 4 years to stagger the change), and the notes' 4 years is correct. The Act also raised the shareholding qualification to vote (to £1,000 stock, according to common accounts [Verification Required]) and capped the dividend at 6% until the government loan was repaid (Regulating Act 1773, Wikipedia). The loan is given as about £1.4 million in most textbooks and £1.5 million in this source [Verification Required].

[Correction] Title, council members and procedure. The post is formally Governor-General of the Presidency of Fort William in Bengal; Hastings was Governor from 28 April 1772 and Governor-General from 20 October 1773 to 8 February 1785 (Warren Hastings, Wikipedia). The four councillors named in the Act, for five years, were John Clavering, George Monson ("Manson" in the source), Richard Barwell and Philip Francis; Barwell sided with Hastings, the other three opposed him. The Governor-General had no veto and voted only to break a tie. The notes' "last Governor of Bengal 1772-73, first Governor-General 1773-85" is correct. Other provisions: servants were forbidden private trade and accepting presents from Indians (source); the salaries (Governor-General about £25,000, councillors about £10,000) are [Verification Required]. The Bombay and Madras governors were subordinate in matters of war, peace and revenue, except in emergencies [Verification Required]. Source for text: the Act on legislation.gov.uk.

1.4 Amending Act of 1781 (Act of Settlement)

  • This Act established that the judges of the Supreme Court could not question the official decisions of the Governor-General in Council (acts done in their official capacity).
[Additional] Amending Act of 1781 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome
BackgroundThe Supreme Court (1774) and the Council quarrelled over jurisdiction: the Nand Kumar case (1775) (Hastings's Indian opponent, hanged for forgery under English law), the Patna case and the Cossijurah case (the Court tried to hear cases against zamindars and officials outside Calcutta) [Verification Required] on details. The 1781 Select Committee reviewed this conflict (c04).
ReasonsTo define the limits of the Supreme Court and protect the Company's administration and revenue collection from judicial interference.
Provisions
  • The Governor-General and Council were not subject to the Supreme Court for acts done in their official capacity; the Court had no jurisdiction in revenue matters [Verification Required].
  • Courts were to respect Hindu and Muslim personal laws in matters such as inheritance and contract [Verification Required].
  • The Governor-General in Council could frame regulations for the provincial courts (Adalats) and hear appeals from them.
OutcomeThe executive became supreme over the judiciary in the revenue and administrative field; the clash ended, but the independence of the judiciary was limited.

[Correction] Name and nature. The 1781 Act is often called the Act of Settlement 1781 or Amending Act; it amended the Regulating Act. The Chief Justice and three judges were the Court's original strength (Impey, Chambers, Lemaistre, Hyde) [Verification Required]; the Court was set up by a royal charter of 1774 and sat from October 1774 [Verification Required] on the exact date.

1.5 Pitt's India Act 1784

Passed when William Pitt the Younger was Prime Minister (royal assent 13 August 1784), hence Pitt's India Act. It was enacted to remove the defects of the Regulating Act.

Pitt's India Act 1784 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome
Background
  • The Regulating Act had failed to control the Company: deadlock in the Council, the Supreme Court dispute, wars with Mysore and the Marathas and the Company's debts.
  • The Select and Secret Committees of 1781 (c04) produced reports used against the Company in Parliament.
  • Fox's India Bill (1783) - which proposed taking power from the Company - was defeated, and Pitt formed a government in December 1783 [Verification Required]; the 1784 Act was a compromise that left the Company's patronage and commerce with the directors.
ReasonsTo bring the Company's political functions under direct government control, while avoiding the nationalisation of its trade; to remove the council deadlock and make Bombay and Madras obey Calcutta.
Provisions (as in the notes)
  1. In England a new authority, the Board of Control (BoC), was created to supervise the civil, military and revenue affairs of the Company. The COD continued to be responsible for commercial functions - the arrangement is known as Double Government / dual control.
  2. The strength of the executive council in India was reduced from 4 to 3 (the new structure 3 + 1).
  3. The Governors of Bombay and Madras were made subordinate to the Governor-General in Council in matters of war, revenue and diplomacy.

[Additional] The Company's possessions were officially called the "British possessions in India" for the first time; schemes of conquest and extension of dominion were declared repugnant to British policy (soon ignored); officers had to declare their property in India and Britain; and offences in India could be tried by a special court in Britain (Pitt's India Act, Wikipedia).

Outcome / defects
  • Established the system of dual control that lasted until 1858, with the President of the BoC becoming the minister for Indian affairs.
  • The Board vs COD boundaries were hazy, and the Governor-General served two masters; the Act addressed the council deadlock but not all conflicts.
  • The next step was the Charter Act 1793.

[Correction] Composition of the Board of Control. The notes say the BoC had 6 members, 2 MPs and 4 Privy Council members. The Act provided for not more than six Privy Councillors as "Commissioners for the Affairs of India", including a Secretary of State and the Chancellor of the Exchequer; at least three formed the board, headed by the President of the Board (source). Textbooks give the composition as a Secretary of State, the Chancellor of the Exchequer and four Privy Councillors appointed by the Crown; use this form. The notes' "2 MPs + 4 Privy Council" is a common mnemonic variant and is retained only as the intended meaning. [Additional] Also: the three-member council included the Commander-in-Chief and the Governor-General had a casting vote (source). Do not confuse this "dual government" (England, 1784) with Clive's dual government of Bengal (1765-72).

Supplementary Act of 1786 (veto power to Cornwallis)

The margin note in the rough notes says that the Act of 1786 gave the Governor-General Cornwallis the power to override his council.

[Correction] The veto / override power. The supplementary Act of 1786 was passed because Cornwallis refused the office unless he was given power to override the council's majority and to hold the post of Commander-in-Chief together with it; he became the second Governor-General (1786-93), after the acting Governor-General John Macpherson. The Charter Act 1793 affirmed the override power for all Governors-General (and extended it to the Governors of Madras and Bombay [Verification Required]), so "veto" in the notes for the 1793 Act means the continuation of the 1786 power (Charter Act 1793, Wikipedia; Pitt's India Act, Wikipedia).

1.6 Charter Act 1793

Charter Act 1793 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome
BackgroundThe Company's 20-year charter was due to expire; the Act of 1786 had given Cornwallis the override power. The 1793 Act "passed with minimal trouble" and made few changes to the system set up in 1784 (royal assent 11 June 1793).
ReasonsTo renew the charter and to put the Board of Control on a firm financial footing; to confirm the Governor-General's strong executive powers.
Provisions
  1. It renewed the charter for another 20 years (with the trade monopoly).
  2. The salaries of the members of the Board of Control and its staff were to be paid from the Company's revenue of India, not from the British State Treasury.
  3. The Governor-General of Bengal's override ("veto") power over his council was continued (and the power over Bombay and Madras strengthened).

[Additional] The Board's President and two junior members need not be Privy Councillors; the dividend could rise to 10%; revenue administration was separated from the judicial (the Cornwallis Code, 1793); and the Company had to pay the Crown about £5 lakh a year from Indian revenue (Charter Act 1793, Wikipedia).

OutcomeConsolidated the Cornwallis system; made India pay for the machinery of its own supervision; the Governor-General became the real ruler. The next renewal (1813) came in a very different climate - the Industrial Revolution and demands for free trade.

[Correction] Notes' reading of 1786 vs 1793. The margin note "Act of 1786 gave veto power to Cornwallis" is kept as the origin of the power; see c08. "Company's revenue of India" means the revenues of the Company's Indian territories, charged to the Company's account and so ultimately paid by Indians. Linked: the Permanent Settlement (Bengal, 1793) was enacted in the same year under Cornwallis (section 2).

1.7 Charter Act 1813

Charter Act 1813 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome
Background
  • The Industrial Revolution: British manufacturers (Manchester, Liverpool, Glasgow) wanted open access to Indian and Asian markets; the Napoleonic Wars and the Continental System closed European markets.
  • Free-trade ideas (Adam Smith; section 1.2) attacked the Company's monopoly.
  • Evangelicals (Charles Grant, William Wilberforce, the Clapham group) demanded permission for missionaries and a moral duty to educate Indians; the Company, afraid after the Vellore Mutiny (1806), resisted.
ReasonsRenew the charter (due in 1813) but open Indian trade to British merchants, and clarify the legal status of the Company's territories (royal sovereignty).
Provisions
  1. Ended the monopoly of the EIC in India trade, with two exceptions: trade with China and trade in tea.
  2. It defined the constitutional position of the British territories in India: the Act asserted the sovereignty of the Crown over them, the Company holding them in trust for 20 more years.
  3. It allowed Christian missionaries to enter India and preach Christianity (under licence from the Board / Company).
  4. It provided that the Company's government of India should spend Rs 1 lakh annually on the promotion of education in India (the revival of literature and encouragement of the learned natives, and the promotion of science).
Outcome
  • British goods flooded India and traditional handicrafts (especially textiles) declined; India became a market for British factory goods and a source of raw materials (Charter Act 1813, Wikipedia; the pace of this decline is debated among economic historians).
  • The education grant sparked the Orientalist-Anglicist controversy (resolved in the Anglicists' favour in the 1830s) [Verification Required] on dates, and missionary activity expanded.
  • Next: 1833 ended the China trade monopoly too.

[Correction] Missionaries and education. Textbooks say the Act "allowed" missionaries; scholars note that the Act itself did not openly authorise preaching and the Company kept a policy of religious neutrality, while the licensing arrangement and the Bishop of Calcutta's establishment opened the door in practice [Verification Required] (source). The Rs 1 lakh was "for the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of the learned natives of India", and it remained largely unspent for years because of disputes over the medium of instruction. The Act text is on legislation.gov.uk. Royal assent: 22 July 1813. [Additional] The monopoly of the opium trade and China also stayed with the Company (see also the "country trade" of 1793).

1.8 Charter Act 1833 (Saint Helena Act)

Charter Act 1833 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome
Background
  • The 1813 compromise left tea and China trade with the Company; British industrialists, the Reform Act of 1832 and the Whig government of Earl Grey (liberal, utilitarian, free-trade ideas of Bentham, James Mill and the Utilitarians) pushed further.
  • The Company's commercial profits had shrunk; its rule was now chiefly territorial; the legislative confusion of three Presidencies each making their own regulations was widely criticised.
  • Lord William Bentinck (Governor-General of Bengal 1828-33; of India 1833-35) wanted a uniform, rationalised system.
ReasonsEnd the Company's commercial role, centralise power and law-making, open the services to Indians in principle, and renew the charter for 20 years as a political agent of the Crown.
Provisions (as in the notes)
  1. It ended the two earlier exceptions as well (trade with China and the tea trade); the Company became a purely administrative body.
  2. It allowed Europeans (whites) to purchase property and settle in India.
  3. Administration was centralised: the Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India - William Bentinck (1828-35: 1828-33 Governor-General of Bengal, 1833-35 Governor-General of India).
  4. Legislation was centralised: the Governors of Bombay and Madras lost their power of making laws; they could only suggest matters to the Governor-General regarding law-making.
  5. For law-making, the Governor-General in Council had to consult an additional (fourth) member who was an expert in Indian laws (the Law Member).
  6. A Law Commission was to be constituted for the consolidation and codification of Indian laws.

Additional lines recorded in the notes: services / jobs under the Company's government were open to all Indians irrespective of caste, class, colour, etc. (in principle); the Company's government should take steps to improve the condition of slaves; Act 5 of 1843 made slavery illegal in India.

Outcome / limits
  • The First Law Commission (1834) under Macaulay led to the drafting of the Indian Penal Code (enacted 1860).
  • The claim of equal opportunity remained mostly on paper: the Court of Directors kept its patronage and the open-competition provision was nullified.
  • Over-centralisation made Madras and Bombay governance slow; the proposal to split Bengal into Fort William and Agra Presidencies was never put into effect (source).

[Correction] Name, dates and terms. The Act is the Saint Helena Act 1833 (it also vested the island of St Helena in the Crown; Map 1); royal assent 28 August 1833, in force 22 April 1834; the charter was extended until 30 April 1854 (Government of India Act 1833, Wikipedia; legislation.gov.uk). Bentinck was the first Governor-General of India "at the end of 1833". The equal-opportunity clause (Section 87) says that no native of India shall be disabled from any office by reason of religion, place of birth, descent or colour - the notes' "caste, class" is broader than the text [Verification Required]. Slavery: the Act only directed the Governor-General to take steps; Act V of 1843 (under Lord Ellenborough) abolished the legal status of slavery [Verification Required] on the Governor-General. The Law Member was at first not a full member of the Executive Council (full membership came in 1853; see 1853); the first was Macaulay (1834-38) [Verification Required].

1.9 Charter Act 1853

Charter Act 1853 - background, reasons, provisions and outcome
Background
  • The 1833 charter was to expire in 1854; Parliament again held inquiries (1852-53) into the Company's rule, and public opinion in Britain demanded reform of the Company's patronage and Indian representation in lawmaking.
  • India had grown vast (Punjab, Sindh, Lower Burma under Dalhousie; see the 10-24 file); the Governor-General could not run Bengal and India both.
ReasonsContinue Company rule but without a fixed term, make the civil service merit-based, and separate legislation from administration.
Provisions (as in the notes)
  1. The Act did not extend the charter for any fixed period; the Company could administer India as long as Parliament wanted.
  2. The strength of the Court of Directors was reduced from 24 to 18.
  3. It introduced open competition for the ICS: the first exam was held in London in 1855; Satyendranath Tagore was the first Indian to qualify (1863-64).
  4. The Law Member was given full membership of the Executive Council, and for law-making 6 additional members were provided: 2 judges of the Supreme Court and 4 ICS (Company) officers nominated by the provincial governments. The body was known as the Governor-General's Legislative Council / Indian Legislative Council / Central Legislative Council.
Outcome
  • The Legislative Council (1854) began to act like a mini-Parliament, asking questions and debating bills (it was resented by the Government and curbed).
  • Open competition ended the directors' patronage in civil appointments, but entry stayed in London and in practice almost closed to Indians for decades.
  • Company rule ended only five years later, after the Revolt of 1857 (Government of India Act 1858; next topic).

[Correction] Directors, legislative council and the ICS. Of the 18 directors, 12 were elected by the proprietors and 6 nominated by the Crown [Verification Required]. The four additional members were provincial representatives (from Bengal, Madras, Bombay and the North-Western Provinces), senior Company servants nominated by the respective governments; with the Governor-General, the Commander-in-Chief and four ordinary members, the Legislative Council had 12 members [Verification Required]. The ICS competitive exam followed the report of the Macaulay Committee (1854) [Verification Required]; Satyendranath Tagore (elder brother of Rabindranath) passed in 1863 and joined in 1864. The Act also let the Government appoint a separate Lieutenant-Governor for Bengal (1854) [Verification Required]. No stable Wikipedia article for the 1853 Act was available during preparation (the page does not exist at the cited title), so the Act's provisions above rely on standard textbooks; verify against the text on legislation.gov.uk (search "East India Company Act 1853", 16 & 17 Vict. c. 95).

2. British Land Revenue Policies - Warren Hastings and Revenue Farming

2.1 Background and History

  • The Company started collecting land revenue in 1765 with the Diwani (see Dual Government), but it had few officers who knew the land system of Bengal; collection was left to the Naib Diwan and the zamindars, with the Company adding supervisors.
  • The famine of 1770 (deaths estimated between two and ten million) showed that revenue was collected "without regard for future income or local welfare" and that officials were corrupt or lax (Permanent Settlement, Wikipedia; Warren Hastings, Wikipedia).
  • Warren Hastings (1772-85) did not evolve any scientific / rational system of land revenue. He preferred a temporary arrangement called Revenue Farming / Ijarah.
  • Under this, land was auctioned to the highest bidder (the ijaradar / farmer). Initially auctions were held every 5 years, but later it became annual.
  • It did not bring the desired result for the Company and proved exploitative for the peasants. Therefore Cornwallis (1786-93) replaced it with a different system - the Permanent Settlement (next class).

[Additional] Revenue farming (Ijarah) - reasons, working, defaulters, effects and outcome (requested in the notes)
Reasons
  • The Company was short of trained administrators and had no records or knowledge of how much each estate could really pay; auction was a quick way of "discovering" the value of land.
  • It urgently needed cash for the Company's trade purchases ("investment"), for the wars of the 1770s and for its debts and dividends.
  • The Company did not want to take over village administration directly or to upset the traditional power of Bengal's landholders (source), and it distrusted both zamindars and the Mughal-era officials (Hastings removed the Naib Diwan, Muhammad Reza Khan, in 1772 [Verification Required]).
  • Revenue farming was not new: it was a Mughal practice, used under Murshid Quli Khan in Bengal [Verification Required].
How it worked
  • A Committee of Circuit toured the districts and auctioned the right to collect revenue to the highest bidder for five years (1772) [Verification Required]; Collectors were appointed to supervise. Early arrangements also moved the treasury from Murshidabad to Calcutta (Map 2).
  • The farmer paid the Company the amount of his bid and collected as much as he could from the peasants; the Company had no say in how much he took.
  • Later the lease was shortened to one year (the notes: "annual"; c. 1777 [Verification Required]) as the Company tried to correct the failure.
Defaulters
  • The revenue farmers (ijaradars) themselves: many bid more than the land could yield to win the lease, then fell into arrears or absconded after taking as much as they could between inspections (source); the Company's balance of unpaid revenue (arrears) kept growing.
  • Many hereditary zamindars, outbid or unable to pay inflated demands, lost their estates to the speculators (often Calcutta banians, Company servants' agents and moneylenders); big zamindar houses were reported ruined [Verification Required] on named cases.
  • Notorious farmers such as Raja Devi Singh (Rangpur-Dinajpur, whose cruelty is linked to the Rangpur Dhing, 1783) and Ganga Govinda Singh are named in accounts [Verification Required].
  • The peasants (ryots) became "defaulters" in a different sense: unable to pay the high rents, they were flogged, evicted or fled.
Effects
  • Exploitation of peasants: high and arbitrary demands, extra "abwabs" (cesses), no security of tenure, flight of cultivators and fall in cultivation after a famine that had already killed a large part of Bengal's population.
  • No incentive to improve land: farmers had short leases and no interest in irrigation or production.
  • Instability of the Company's revenue: collections varied, arrears accumulated and the Company still could not predict its income.
  • Social change: an old landed class was displaced by speculators; the zamindar's role as a local protector weakened.
Outcome
  • It did not bring the desired result and was exploitative (the notes). Parliament noticed the failure; Pitt (1784) directed the Calcutta administration to change it, and in 1786 the Court of Directors first proposed a permanent settlement.
  • Cornwallis (1786-93) replaced it: first annual, then a ten-year (decennial) settlement in 1790 with the zamindars, made permanent in 1793 (source) - covered in the next class.

[Correction] Dates and terms. "Ijarah" (Arabic-Persian ijara, a lease) is the same as revenue farming / tax farming. The notes' dates are kept: Hastings 1772-85, the five-yearly auction starting in 1772, and the later annual lease [Verification Required] on the year (a common textbook date is 1777). The margin note "Company started collecting revenues from 1765 - Diwani" is correct: it belongs to the Treaty of Allahabad (16 August 1765) (see 10-24 file). Hastings's own comment that the system was a temporary arrangement is the reason textbooks say he "did not evolve a scientific system". The estimate of how much of Bengal's population died in 1770 (often "one-third") is disputed [Verification Required].

Reading note. The 1793 Permanent Settlement is introduced above only as the successor policy; its provisions, the Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems and the comparison between them belong to the next class (see NCERT, Themes in Indian History III, Chapter 10 "Colonialism and the Countryside").

Key Facts for Prelims

  • EIC formed Dec 1599; Charter 31 Dec 1600 (Elizabeth I); first term 15 years; first ship at Surat 1608.
  • Regulating Act 1773 (Lord North): first Parliamentary control; GG of Bengal (Warren Hastings) + 4 councillors, majority vote, casting vote, quorum 3; Madras and Bombay subordinated; Supreme Court at Calcutta, 1774 (CJ Elijah Impey); directors' term 4 years; servants banned from private trade and presents.
  • Amending Act 1781: the Supreme Court cannot question the GG-in-Council's official acts.
  • Pitt's India Act 1784: Board of Control (6 commissioners) + COD = dual control; council 4 to 3; "British possessions in India".
  • 1786: Cornwallis gets the override power; 1793 Act renews charter 20 years; BoC paid from Indian revenue.
  • 1813: monopoly ended except tea and China; Crown's sovereignty; Rs 1 lakh for education; missionaries.
  • 1833: St Helena Act; GG of India (Bentinck); commercial role ended; Law Member; Law Commission (Macaulay); legislative centralisation; Section 87 (no disqualification by religion, birth, descent or colour).
  • Act V of 1843: slavery made illegal. 1853: no fixed term; directors 24 to 18; ICS exam 1855; Law Member full member; S. N. Tagore (1863-64); legislative council with 6 additional members.
  • Revenue farming (Ijarah): Hastings 1772-85; auction to the highest bidder; five-year then annual; replaced by Cornwallis (1790 decennial; 1793 Permanent Settlement).

Mains Points

  • From company to state: each Charter Act removed a layer of the Company's independence - 1773 (supervision), 1784 (political control), 1813 (monopoly), 1833 (commercial role), 1853 (patronage and fixed term) - until the Crown took over in 1858.
  • Centralisation: the Governor-General of Bengal (1773) became the Governor-General of India (1833); Madras and Bombay lost first their independence (1773, 1784) and then their lawmaking (1833).
  • Pressures from Britain: mercantile and industrial interests, free-trade ideas, evangelicals and reformers shaped the Acts more than the interests of Indians; the education grant, missionary entry and the opening of the services were mostly ideology and limited practice.
  • Revenue farming: a stop-gap that treated land as a source of quick cash - it enriched speculators, hurt peasants and failed the Company; it prepared the ground for the Permanent Settlement.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Two "dual governments": Clive's in Bengal (1765-72: Company = Diwani, Nawab = Nizamat) vs Pitt's in England (1784: BoC + COD).
  • Governor-General of Bengal (1773) vs Governor-General of India (1833); Hastings was the first of Bengal, Bentinck the first of India.
  • 1813 vs 1833: 1813 ended the monopoly except tea and China; 1833 ended those and the commercial role.
  • Charter Act 1793 / veto: the power began in the 1786 Act (Cornwallis); do not credit it to 1784 or 1813.
  • Regulating Act vs Pitt's Act: council 4 -> 3; BoC created in 1784, not 1773; the Supreme Court was 1774.
  • Charter Act 1853 vs 1833: open competition for the ICS was attempted in 1833 but introduced in 1853 (exam 1855).
  • Revenue farming (Hastings) vs Permanent Settlement (Cornwallis); do not confuse with the Diwani of 1765.
  • Charter of 1600 was for the East Indies (not just India) and for 15 years initially.
