1. The Beginning of the Modern Phase and the European Companies

1.1 What "modern" means here

Textbooks treat the early eighteenth century as the start of the modern phase of Indian history. Two developments mark it: the rivalry among the European trading companies, and the decline and break-up of the Mughal empire, which allowed regional kingdoms to emerge. The Company's rise from trader to ruler happened where these two developments met.

[Correction] "Consensus" on the starting date. The rough notes say historians agree that the modern phase began in the early eighteenth century. That is too strong. The usual convention is the eighteenth century, with the decline of the Mughal empire after Aurangzeb's death (3 March 1707); many accounts begin instead with Plassey (23 June 1757), and a few use the Third Battle of Panipat (1761). In an answer, say "conventionally the eighteenth century" and name your criterion.

1.2 The age of exploration

Europe's long sea voyages of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries were a search for a direct route to the wealth of the East - spices, textiles and other goods. The fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453 [Additional] made the old land and Mediterranean routes costlier and riskier, and the crowns of Portugal and Spain backed ocean exploration. Bartholomew Diaz (Portugal) rounded the southern tip of Africa, the Cape of Good Hope, in 1488 but turned back before reaching India. Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, went west in 1492 and reached the Americas, which he took to be near Asia - the reason the inhabitants of the Americas came to be called "Indians". Vasco da Gama sailed from Lisbon in July 1497, went round the Cape and anchored near Calicut (Kozhikode), ruled by the Zamorin, in May 1498 [Verification Required on the exact days]. He bought spices, sold them in Europe at a large profit, and thereby tempted other Europeans to follow.

The Portuguese were the first to build a power in India. Under Alfonso de Albuquerque they took Goa from the Bijapur Sultanate in 1510, and they controlled Indian Ocean shipping through a permit system called the cartaz. They were the first European power to arrive and the last to leave: Goa, Daman and Diu stayed Portuguese until 1961. (Diaz's note: Gama did not "find" the Cape - Diaz did, ten years earlier.)

1.3 The five companies and the order of arrival

After the Portuguese came the Dutch, then the English, then the Danes, and last the French. The Dutch United East India Company (the VOC, 1602) was mainly interested in the Spice Islands of South-East Asia and used Indian textiles as exchange goods; its early Indian bases were on the Coromandel coast (Masulipatnam, Pulicat) from about 1605-10. The English Company was founded in 1599-1600 (so it is older than the VOC even though the Dutch reached the Coromandel coast about the same time). The Danes founded a company in 1616 and settled at Tranquebar (1620) and Serampore (1755); their settlements were sold to the British in 1845 [Verification Required]. The French Compagnie des Indes orientales came in 1664 and the first French factory was at Surat in 1668; the French were therefore the last of the major companies to arrive. 1.4 What a "factory" was

The trading centres of the companies were called factories. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word did not mean a manufacturing unit. A factor was a commercial agent who bought and sold on the company's behalf, and the place where factors worked, lived and stored goods - a warehouse, office and residence in one, usually fortified - was a factory, or "trading post". The Company's servants rose through a ladder of ranks (writer, factor, junior merchant, senior merchant), so factors were more than clerks [Verification Required on the exact ladder]. Most factories were on the coast or on river ports because trade was seaborne, but not all: Patna, Agra, Cossimbazar and Dacca were inland factories, so the safe phrase is "mostly coastal".

1.5 The English East India Company from 1599 to the three presidencies

A group of London merchants resolved in September 1599 to form the venture, and on 31 December 1600 Queen Elizabeth I granted a royal charter giving the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies" a monopoly of trade with the East Indies for fifteen years. Shareholders, called proprietors, elected the 24 directors who formed the Court of Directors. The first Governor was Thomas Smythe.

The indefinite renewal by James I is dated 1609 in the sources [Verification Required]; 1615 is the year Sir Thomas Roe was sent to Jahangir's court. (2) The charter covered the East Indies, not "East Asia" alone. (3) "Hawkins in 1608" means the first Company ship, the Hector under Captain William Hawkins, reached Surat in 1608 and he went to Jahangir's court in 1609; his mission failed because of Portuguese and local opposition. The Company then beat the Portuguese at the Battle of Swally (1612) and the Surat factory was set up in 1613. Sir Thomas Roe (at court 1615-18) won wider trading rights. Hawkins and Roe both went to Jahangir, never to Akbar.

From Surat the Company spread eastwards and southwards. A factory at Masulipatnam was permitted by the Sultan of Golconda in 1611 (temporary; the permanent one dates from about 1616). In 1633 a factory at Balasore in Odisha was the first in eastern India. In 1639 Francis Day obtained land at Madras from a Nayak of the Vijayanagara empire and the settlement was fortified as Fort St George, the first fort built by the British in India. Bombay passed to the English Crown as part of the marriage settlement of Catherine of Braganza and Charles II (treaty 1661, marriage 1662), and the Crown leased it to the Company in 1668. In Bengal, after the Mughals allowed the Company to resume trade, Job Charnock settled at Sutanuti (Calcutta) on 24 August 1690; Fort William was built from 1696 (the main fort was completed about 1702) and named after William III. The heads of these settlements became the heads of the three presidencies - Madras, Bombay and Calcutta - and the towns that grew around them were the presidency towns.

[Correction] Calcutta, Charnock and Fort William. The rough notes join three separate events. Charnock settled Calcutta in 1690 and died in 1692 or 1693, so he did not build anything in 1698; the fort was built from 1696; and 1698 is the year the Company acquired the zamindari of the villages Sutanuti, Kalikata and Govindpur [Verification Required]. In 2003 the Calcutta High Court, following an expert committee, said Charnock should not be called the sole "founder" of Calcutta. Fort William in Kolkata, the Army's Eastern Command headquarters, was renamed Vijay Durg (reported 5 February 2025); use "Fort William" for the historical fort.

1.6 The French settlements

The French set up factories at Surat (1668) and Masulipatam (1669), acquired Pondicherry in 1673 from the Bijapur governor of Valikondapuram (it became their capital, with François Martin as first governor), and then Chandannagar in Bengal in 1692 with the permission of the Mughal governor Shaista Khan. Later additions were Yanam (1723), Mahe (1725, on the Malabar coast) and Karaikal (1739). Pondicherry and Chandannagar became the bases from which the French challenged the English.

[Correction] French places. Mahe is in Kerala (the Malabar coast), not at Pondicherry; Pondicherry is a separate settlement on the Coromandel coast. "Chandanagar" is Chandannagar (1692). Jinji (Gingee) is a hill fortress in Tamil Nadu used by the French during the Carnatic Wars, not an early trading factory [Verification Required]. 2. Mughal Decline and the Eighteenth-Century Setting

2.1 The farman of 1717

The Company's first big political-commercial gain from a weakening Mughal court came in 1717, when Emperor Farrukhsiyar (r. 1713-19) issued a farman after the embassy of John Surman (the surgeon William Hamilton is said to have treated the emperor) [Verification Required on the names and dates]. It allowed the Company to trade in Bengal duty-free in return for a token payment of Rs 3,000 a year, and let it issue passes called dastaks for its goods. The farman covered the Company's own trade, not the private trade of its servants, who were legally bound to pay duty. In practice the servants misused dastaks for their personal trade, the Nawabs lost customs revenue, and this became one of the standing quarrels between the Company and the Bengal Nawabs - one of the causes of Plassey.

2.2 The Mughal empire breaks up

After Aurangzeb died in 1707, central control weakened under his successors. Provincial governors made themselves practically independent while still acknowledging the emperor: Murshid Quli Khan in Bengal, Bihar and Odisha (Nazim from about 1717), Saadat Khan in Awadh (from 1722) and Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah I in Hyderabad (from 1724). The Marathas, under their Peshwas at Poona, grew into the chief power of the subcontinent, while the Sikhs, Jats and Rohillas built local states and, later, Hyder Ali took power in Mysore (from about 1761).

[Additional] Two shocks completed the weakening of Delhi. Nadir Shah, ruler of Persia, invaded in 1739, defeated the Mughals at Karnal (24 February 1739) and sacked Delhi, carrying away its treasure, including the Peacock Throne. Ahmad Shah Abdali of Afghanistan then invaded repeatedly, and on 14 January 1761 his army crushed the Marathas under Sadashivrao Bhau at the Third Battle of Panipat. That battle broke the Maratha bid to rule Delhi and left a political vacuum in north India just when the Company was establishing itself in Bengal. This is why some historians date the modern phase from 1761 as well as from 1757.

3. The Anglo-French Struggle: the Carnatic Wars (1746-63)

[Additional] The rough notes mention the Carnatic Wars only in passing (Dupleix, Wandiwash, the Treaty of Paris). They are the missing link between the arrival of the two companies and the conquest of Bengal, so they are set out here in full.

Background. The Carnatic (the Coromandel coastal region, with its Nawab at Arcot) was nominally under the Nizam of Hyderabad, and both territories were riddled with succession disputes after the Mughal decline. Joseph Francois Dupleix, who became governor of the French Company at Pondicherry in 1742, saw that European soldiers and European-trained Indian troops (sepoys) could decide such disputes, and that the side that helped the winner could obtain territory, money and influence. The Company's officers soon copied him. The wars were therefore struggles by the two European companies, backing rival Indian claimants, on a battlefield defined by the wars their home countries fought in Europe.

First Carnatic War (1746-48). This was the Indian side of the War of the Austrian Succession. In 1746 a French naval force under La Bourdonnais captured Madras (21 September 1746). The Nawab of the Carnatic, Anwaruddin, sent a large army to take Madras for himself, but a small French force beat it at St Thome (the Adyar river), showing that disciplined European-trained troops with artillery could defeat much larger Indian armies. The war ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), by which Madras was returned to the English in exchange for the French fortress of Louisbourg in North America. It was also the first military experience of Robert Clive, who was captured at Madras and escaped.

Second Carnatic War (1749-54). There was no war in Europe, but the struggle continued in India as a proxy war over two successions: in Hyderabad (Nasir Jang, backed by the English, against Muzaffar Jang, backed by the French) and in the Carnatic (Chanda Sahib, French-backed, against Muhammad Ali, English-backed). The French were at first successful and Dupleix's candidates took Arcot and Hyderabad. In 1751 Clive led a small force that seized and then held Arcot through a long siege, which broke the French momentum. The French Company, unhappy at the cost, recalled Dupleix in 1754 and sent Godeheu, who agreed to a treaty (the Treaty of Pondicherry, signed in 1754 or early 1755) that left the English with the better position [Verification Required on the exact date].

Third Carnatic War (1758-63). The outbreak of the Seven Years' War in 1756 renewed the conflict. Clive took the French fort at Chandannagar in March 1757, just before Plassey. The war was decided in the south: Sir Eyre Coote defeated the French commander Count de Lally at Wandiwash (22 January 1760), and Pondicherry surrendered in January 1761. The Treaty of Paris (1763) gave Pondicherry, Chandannagar and the other French settlements back, but on condition that they were not fortified and kept no troops; the French also agreed not to support rivals of British client rulers. From then on France was no longer a political challenger in India.

Why the British won. [Additional] Several reasons are usually given. The British had command of the sea, which let them reinforce Madras and Bengal and cut off Pondicherry. The English Company was a private body with its own resources and could use the wealth of Bengal (after Plassey) to finance the war in the south, while the French company depended on a state that gave it little help and was itself fighting costly wars in Europe. The British had able commanders (Clive, Coote) and a stable political base in the three presidencies, whereas the French leadership was divided (Dupleix, Lally) and Lally's harshness cost him allies.

[Correction] Dupleix and the French government. The rough notes call Dupleix a French "government" figure. He was the governor of the French Company at Pondicherry. The Carnatic Wars should be dated 1746-48, 1749-54 and 1758-63 (some textbooks give 1757-63 for the third); the Treaty of Pondicherry date is uncertain [Verification Required].

4. The Conquest of Bengal, 1740-72

4.1 From Alivardi Khan to Siraj-ud-Daulah

Bengal became semi-independent during the reign of the Mughal emperor Muhammad Shah (1719-48). After Murshid Quli Khan's successors Shuja-ud-din (1727-39) and Sarfaraz Khan (1739-40), Alivardi Khan took power in 1740 after defeating Sarfaraz at the Battle of Giria [Verification Required]. He had three daughters and no son, so he named his daughter's son Siraj-ud-Daulah as his heir (formally from 1752), and Siraj became Nawab when Alivardi died on 9 April 1756. Siraj was young and aggressive but lacked political vision, and his accession angered his aunt Ghasiti Begum and his cousin Shaukat Jang, the governor of Purnea. In 1756 the Seven Years' War broke out in Europe; expecting it to spread to India, both English and French began to fortify their factories, and the English steps strained relations with the Nawab until the clash at Plassey.

[Correction] Ghasiti Begum and Siraj. Ghasiti Begum (Mehr-un-Nisa) was Siraj's aunt, not a male "candidate"; the male rival claimant was her nephew Shaukat Jang. Siraj's mother Amina Begum was Alivardi's youngest daughter. The Seven Years' War is 1756-63, and its Indian theatre was the Third Carnatic War (section 3).

4.2 Why the Nawab and the Company quarrelled

The Nawab charged the Company with five things. It had fortified Fort William and mounted cannons on its walls without his approval and refused to stop; it had given shelter to Krishna Ballabh (Krishnadas, son of Rajballav of Dhaka), an official accused of misappropriating funds, who fled to Calcutta with his treasure; it was said to have supported his rivals, Ghasiti Begum and Shaukat Jang [Verification Required on the Company's role]; and its servants misused the farman of 1717 (dastaks), depriving him of customs revenue. In June 1756 Siraj captured Calcutta (renamed Alinagar), and the "Black Hole" imprisonment occurred on the night of 20-21 June 1756; the number of deaths in Holwell's account (123 of 146) is disputed. Clive and Admiral Watson came from Madras and retook Calcutta (Treaty of Alinagar, 9 February 1757), and in March 1757 the French at Chandannagar were beaten.

4.3 The conspiracy and the Battle of Plassey

The Company then plotted with the Nawab's own officers and bankers. Omichand, a merchant who acted as a go-between, learnt of the plot and demanded Rs 30 lakh; Clive therefore made two treaties, a real "white" one and a false "red" one that promised Omichand his share, and Admiral Watson's signature on the red one was forged. Manik Chand, in charge of Calcutta, is said to have yielded the town; Jagat Seth, the great banker of Bengal, feared for his wealth under Siraj and backed the plot; and Mir Jafar, commander of the Nawab's army, was promised the Nawabship and signed a treaty with the Company on 4 June 1757. On 23 June 1757 at Plassey Clive's force of about 800 Europeans and 2,200 sepoys faced the Nawab, whose large army held back under Mir Jafar's and others' orders; the Nawab fled, was captured and was executed on 2 July 1757 on the orders of Mir Jafar's son Miran. The victory owed more to treachery than to fighting.

4.4 From Mir Jafar to Buxar and the Treaty of Allahabad

Mir Jafar (1757-60) was installed as Nawab and paid the Company and its officers large sums and compensation; the zamindari of the 24 Parganas went to the Company. He turned to the Dutch for help against the English, but the Company beat the Dutch at Chinsurah (Bedara) in 1759, and he was replaced in 1760 by his son-in-law Mir Qasim (1760-63). Mir Qasim granted the revenues of Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong to the Company, but then tried to act as a real ruler: he moved his capital from Murshidabad to Munger, built a trained army, and abolished duties on internal trade for everyone, ending the Company's special advantage and the dastak abuse. This brought a clash; his men killed some forty-five Europeans at Patna in 1763, he was defeated at Katwa, Giria and Udhaunala and fled to Awadh. Mir Jafar was put back (1763-65) and died in early 1765; his son Najm-ud-Daula followed (1765-66).

Mir Qasim then joined Shuja-ud-Daula, Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal wazir, and the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. At Buxar (Bihar) on 22-23 October 1764 the Company's army of about 7,000 under Major Hector Munro defeated an allied army of more than 40,000. The Company's discipline, firepower and regular pay, and the lack of unity among the allies (Shuja-ud-Daula abandoned the emperor and blew up his own bridge of boats), decided the battle. Buxar made the Company the real military power of north-eastern India, which is why it is judged more decisive than Plassey.

The settlement came in two steps in 1765. Under the Treaty of Allahabad (16 August 1765) Shah Alam II granted the Company the Diwani (the right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha; the Company agreed to pay the Emperor Rs 26 lakh a year and secured Kora and Allahabad for him. Awadh was returned to Shuja-ud-Daula, who paid Rs 50 lakh as indemnity, ceded Allahabad and Kora, and accepted a defensive alliance that left Awadh dependent on the Company.

[Correction] Buxar's commander and who paid whom in 1765. The "reasons for defeat" in the rough notes name Munro, who commanded at Buxar, not at Plassey (where Clive commanded and where treachery decided the issue). In the Treaty of Allahabad the Company paid Rs 26 lakh to the Emperor (a tribute for his upkeep); the notes' wording "pension" given by the Emperor is wrong in direction. A separate sum (about Rs 53 lakh) was fixed for the Nawab of Bengal's expenses [Verification Required].

4.5 The Dual Government of Bengal (1765-72)

In February 1765 the Company signed a treaty with Najm-ud-Daula (the notes write "Nizam-ud-daula"), asking him to cut his army and agreeing that Bengal would be administered by a deputy subedar (deputy Nawab) whom the Company would appoint and could not be dismissed without its approval: the Nizamat (administration, police and justice) thus passed in practice to the Company. With the Diwani from Allahabad, the Company controlled both revenue and the army but took no responsibility for governing. The Nawab's deputy (Muhammad Reza Khan for Bengal, Raja Shitab Roy for Bihar) held the responsibility but neither money nor force. The system, credited to Robert Clive on his return as Governor in 1765, was one of power without responsibility for the Company and responsibility without power for the Nawab. It coincided with the terrible famine of 1770 (a figure of one-third of Bengal's population is often quoted but disputed), and it ended in 1772 when Warren Hastings took revenue administration into the Company's hands. Do not confuse this dual government with Pitt's 1784 "dual control" (section 5.4).

5. Parliament and the Company: the Constitutional Story, 1600-1833

5.1 From a trading company to a concern of Parliament

For 150 years the Company was a private trading corporation governed by its charter. Its early charters already gave it important powers. The Charter of 1661 (Charles II) let it make war and peace with non-Christian rulers and administer justice in its settlements, and the Charter of 1726 (George I) set up Mayor's Courts in Madras, Bombay and Calcutta and gave each presidency's Governor and Council the power to make by-laws [Additional; Verification Required]. After Plassey (1757) and the Diwani (1765), however, the Company ruled millions of people with an organisation designed for trading. Company servants made huge private fortunes through plunder, gifts and private trade; those who returned to Britain - called Nabobs (a form of Nawabs) - bought land and seats in Parliament and were widely resented.

Critics in Britain attacked the arrangement from two sides. Edmund Burke argued that the people of Bengal should not be left to a private company, and he later led the impeachment of Warren Hastings (the trial began on 13 February 1788; Hastings was acquitted on 24 April 1795). Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations (1776), attacked the monopolies of chartered companies and argued for free trade and minimum state control; this pressure later helped to end the Company's monopoly (1813 for India trade; 1833 for China and tea). After the Glorious Revolution of 1688 established the supremacy of Parliament, MPs also felt entitled to ask what the Company was doing. Parliament formed a Select Committee and a Secret Committee (in 1772-73, and again in 1781) to inquire, and on the basis of their reports it passed a series of Acts. By 1773 the Company was also in financial trouble: it could not meet its payments to the Bank of England and the government and needed a loan.

[Correction] Burke, Smith and the committees. The notes paraphrase Burke; his strongest statements come from his speech on Fox's India Bill (1783) and the Hastings trial, where he argued that the Company's charter was a trust for the governed [Verification Required on exact wording]. "Laissez-faire" is a French phrase used by the physiocrats; Smith's own phrases were the "invisible hand" and the "system of natural liberty". There were two sets of Select and Secret Committees, those of 1772-73 and those of 1781.

5.2 The Regulating Act, 1773

The Regulating Act of 1773 (Lord North's government; royal assent 21 June 1773) was the first attempt by Parliament to regulate the Company, hence its name. It was passed because the Company's servants were corrupt, because the three presidencies acted independently and sometimes against each other, and because the Company needed a government loan. Its provisions were as follows. In England the directors were to be elected for four years (one-quarter retiring each year) and the Company's dividend was limited until the loan was repaid. The Company had to send to the British government all reports received from India. In India the Governor of Bengal became Governor-General of Bengal, assisted by an Executive Council of four members (the "4 + 1" structure); decisions were by majority, the Governor-General having a casting vote only in a tie and no veto, and three members formed the quorum. The governors of Bombay and Madras were subordinated to the Governor-General in Council in matters of war, peace and revenue (with exceptions for emergencies [Verification Required]). A Supreme Court was to be set up at Calcutta with a Chief Justice and three judges; it began work in 1774 under Sir Elijah Impey. Company servants were forbidden to engage in private trade or accept presents from Indians.

The Act had serious defects. The Governor-General could be outvoted by his council: the four councillors named in the Act were Clavering, Monson, Barwell and Philip Francis, and the first three opposed Hastings for years. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction was unclear and it clashed with the Council. Bombay and Madras were not fully brought under Calcutta. Warren Hastings was the last Governor of Bengal (from 28 April 1772) and the first Governor-General of Bengal (20 October 1773 to 8 February 1785).

5.3 The Amending Act, 1781 (Act of Settlement)

The clash between the Supreme Court and the Council had become serious in the cases of Nand Kumar (the Indian rival of Hastings, hanged for forgery in 1775 under English law), Patna and Cossijurah (where the Court tried to reach zamindars and officials outside Calcutta). The Amending Act of 1781 settled that the judges of the Supreme Court could not question the acts of the Governor-General in Council done in their official capacity and that the Court had no jurisdiction in revenue matters [Verification Required]; it also required courts to respect Hindu and Muslim personal laws and gave the Governor-General in Council power to frame regulations for provincial courts. The executive thus gained the upper hand over the judiciary.

5.4 Pitt's India Act, 1784, and the Act of 1786

Pitt's India Act (royal assent 13 August 1784, under William Pitt the Younger) was passed to remove the defects of the Regulating Act. Fox's India Bill of 1783, which would have taken power from the Company, had been defeated, so the 1784 Act was a compromise. A new body in England, the Board of Control, was created to supervise the civil, military and revenue affairs of the Company, while the Court of Directors continued to run its commercial affairs and patronage. This arrangement of dual control lasted until 1858. In India the Governor-General's executive council was reduced from four to three members (the "3 + 1" structure), and the governors of Bombay and Madras were made subordinate to the Governor-General in Council in war, revenue and diplomacy. The Company's lands were for the first time officially called the "British possessions in India", and the Act declared schemes of conquest repugnant to British policy (a pious wish soon ignored).

[Correction] Composition of the Board of Control. The rough notes say six members, "2 MPs and 4 Privy Council". The Act provided for not more than six Privy Councillors as Commissioners for the Affairs of India, including a Secretary of State and the Chancellor of the Exchequer, with a President of the Board at its head; at least three formed a quorum. Textbooks give the composition as a Secretary of State, the Chancellor of the Exchequer and four Privy Councillors named by the Crown. The Board was a Crown body of 1784, not of 1773.

The Governor-General still could not overrule his council. When Cornwallis was offered the post in 1786, he refused unless he was given the power to override the council's majority and to hold the office of Commander-in-Chief together with it. A supplementary Act of 1786 granted this, and Cornwallis became Governor-General (1786-93), after the acting Governor-General John Macpherson (1785-86).

[Correction] The "veto". The rough notes attach the override power to the Charter Act of 1793. It began in the 1786 Act for Cornwallis and was confirmed for all Governors-General in 1793 (and extended to the governors of Bombay and Madras [Verification Required]); do not credit it to 1784 or 1813.

5.5 The Charter Act of 1793

The Company's charter was due to expire, and the Act of 11 June 1793 renewed it for twenty years (with the trade monopoly) and made few changes to the system of 1784. It provided that the salaries of the Board of Control and its staff would be paid from the Company's Indian revenue, not from the British Treasury, and it continued the Governor-General's power to override his council. It also allowed the Company to raise its dividend to 10% and required it to pay the Crown about five lakh pounds a year from Indian revenue [Additional]. The Cornwallis Code of 1793, which separated revenue administration from the administration of justice, was enacted in the same year.

5.6 The Charter Act of 1813

By 1813 the climate had changed. The Industrial Revolution had made British manufacturers (in Manchester, Liverpool and Glasgow) eager to sell to Asia, the Napoleonic Wars and the Continental System had closed European markets, and free-trade ideas had gained force. Evangelical Christians (Charles Grant, William Wilberforce and the Clapham group) demanded permission for missionaries and a duty to educate Indians, which the Company, alarmed by the Vellore Mutiny (1806), had resisted. The Act of 22 July 1813 renewed the charter for twenty years but ended the Company's monopoly of trade with India, with two exceptions: trade with China and trade in tea. It asserted the sovereignty of the Crown over the Company's territories in India, which the Company held in trust. It opened the way for Christian missionaries to enter India under licence, and it directed that the government should spend Rs 1 lakh a year on education (the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of the learned natives of India, and the promotion of science). The result was that British manufactured goods poured into India and traditional handicrafts, especially textiles, suffered (the pace of decline is debated by economic historians), and the education grant, largely unspent for years because of disputes over the medium of instruction, started the Orientalist-Anglicist controversy.

[Correction] Missionaries. Textbooks say the Act "allowed" missionaries; scholars note that the Company kept a policy of religious neutrality and that the licensing arrangement, together with the establishment of the Bishop of Calcutta, opened the door in practice [Verification Required]. The monopoly of the China and opium trades stayed with the Company until 1833.

5.7 The Charter Act of 1833 (Saint Helena Act)

The compromise of 1813 left the Company's trade in tea and with China intact, but the pressure of British industrialists, the Reform Act of 1832 and the Whig government of Earl Grey (with its liberal and utilitarian ideas, Bentham and James Mill among them) went further. The Company's commercial profits had shrunk, and its rule was now mainly territorial. The Act received royal assent on 28 August 1833 (in force 22 April 1834) and extended the charter to 30 April 1854. Its provisions were: the Company's commercial functions ended - the last two monopolies, the China trade and the tea trade, were abolished - so the Company became a purely administrative body; Europeans were allowed to purchase property and settle in India; the Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India (the first being Lord William Bentinck); law-making was centralised so that the governors of Bombay and Madras lost their power to make laws and could only suggest measures to the Governor-General; the Governor-General's Council was to include an additional (fourth) member, the Law Member, an expert in Indian law (first Macaulay); and a Law Commission was appointed to consolidate and codify Indian law, whose work led to the Indian Penal Code (enacted 1860). The island of St Helena passed to the Crown. The Act also stated in Section 87 that no native of India should be disqualified from any office by reason of religion, place of birth, descent or colour, and it directed the government to improve the condition of slaves; slavery was made illegal by Act V of 1843. In practice, equal opportunity stayed largely on paper and the proposal to split Bengal into the Fort William and Agra Presidencies was never put into effect.

[Correction] Details of 1833. The rough notes extend the equal-opportunity clause to "caste, class"; the text says religion, place of birth, descent or colour [Verification Required]. The Law Member was at first not a full member of the Executive Council (full membership came in 1853). Bentinck was Governor-General of Bengal 1828-33 and of India 1833-35. The 1833 Act is the one that renamed the post (a 2023 UPSC Prelims question tests this; see the PYQ section).

6. The Governors-General, 1772-1835: Expansion, Wars and Reform

The constitutional Acts set the framework; the Governors-General made the empire. This part follows them in order and fills the wars and reforms missing from the rough notes. The table at the end gathers the names and terms.

6.1 Warren Hastings (1772-85)

Hastings first reorganised revenue and justice in Bengal (section 7) and then faced wars on three fronts. [Additional] In 1774 the Company lent troops to Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh in the Rohilla War, which ended with the Rohilla chief Hafiz Rahmat Khan killed and Rohilkhand handed to Awadh; the Company was paid for the loan of troops (Rs 40 lakh is the usual figure [Verification Required]). In 1775 Nand Kumar, an Indian accuser of Hastings, was hanged for forgery after a trial before the Supreme Court headed by Hastings's friend Impey, a case widely seen as a judicial murder. In 1781 Hastings demanded money and troops from Chait Singh, Raja of Banaras, and later extracted money from the Begums of Awadh; these affairs formed part of the charges in the impeachment. Hastings also encouraged learning: the Calcutta Madrasa (1781) and the Asiatic Society of Bengal (founded 1784 by William Jones with his support). He left India in February 1785; his impeachment lasted from 1788 to 1795 and ended in acquittal.

The First Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82) and the Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780-84) belong to his term and are told in sections 6.6 and 6.5.

6.2 Lord Cornwallis (1786-93)

Cornwallis, given an override power over his council, was an administrator by temperament. [Additional] He introduced the Cornwallis Code (1793), whose central idea was the separation of the revenue and judicial functions: the district Collector now collected revenue only, and civil justice went to district judges, a hierarchy of courts under the Sadar Diwani Adalat; the police were reorganised under district superintendents and daroghas at thanas. He improved the pay of the covenanted civil service and forbade its members private trade and gifts, which cut down corruption, but he reserved the higher posts for Europeans and kept Indians out - a limitation the nationalists later attacked. His revenue settlement of 1793 is in section 7. His military achievement was the Third Anglo-Mysore War (section 6.5). He returned to India in 1805 as Governor-General for a second term and died at Ghazipur in October 1805.

6.3 Sir John Shore (1793-98)

Shore followed a policy of non-intervention in the quarrels of Indian states. When the Marathas defeated the Nizam of Hyderabad at Kharda (1795), the Company did not help its ally [Additional], a failure that Wellesley later used to justify his forward policy. The renewal of the charter in 1793 took place in his early days.

6.4 Lord Wellesley (1798-1805) and the Subsidiary Alliance

Wellesley's aim was to make the Company the paramount power in India, and the instrument was the Subsidiary Alliance. The system was first used by Dupleix in the Carnatic in the 1740s, when he lent French troops to Indian rulers for payment or territory; the Company applied it after Plassey and Buxar; and it reached its most systematic form under Wellesley, who was driven by fear of Napoleon (his Egyptian expedition of 1798) and of Tipu Sultan's links with France. In a dispatch of 1801 Wellesley described the aim as putting the allied states in such dependence on British power that they could not form a confederacy against it.

The ruler who accepted the alliance had to maintain a British force in his state and pay for it (in cash or by ceding territory); he could make no treaty with another state without the Company's consent; he had to accept a British Resident at his court; he could employ no European without the Company's permission and could keep no army of his own that was independent of the Company; and the Company, in return, promised to protect him from outside attack and not to interfere in his internal affairs. In practice, interference in succession and administration grew with time, so non-interference was largely a promise in name. The system cost the Company nothing, kept the French out and turned states into protectorates; its aim was paramountcy, not a fixed income.

The states signed in this order: Hyderabad (1798), the first, with a further treaty in 1800 giving the Company the "Ceded Districts"; Mysore (1799), after Tipu's death at Seringapatam (4 May 1799), with the Wadiyar prince Krishnaraja III restored on subsidiary terms; Tanjore (1799); Awadh (1801), which ceded about half of its territory, including Rohilkhand and the lower Doab; and the Peshwa, Baji Rao II, by the Treaty of Bassein (31 December 1802), signed after Holkar defeated the Peshwa and Scindia at Poona and the Peshwa fled to Bassein (Vasai). The Peshwa accepted 6,000 Company troops, ceded districts yielding Rs 26 lakh, gave up claims over Surat and Baroda, excluded other Europeans and agreed to consult the Company on foreign relations; he was restored on 13 May 1803. The Bhonsle of Nagpur (1803) and Scindia (1804) followed, and later Jaipur, Jodhpur and the Holkar of Indore (1818). Wellesley also founded the College of Fort William at Calcutta in 1800 to train civil servants [Additional].

[Correction] Bassein date and Awadh. The Treaty of Bassein was signed on 31 December 1802, not 30 December. Hyderabad, not Mysore, was the first state to accept the alliance. For Dalhousie's later annexation of Awadh, see section 8.2.

6.5 The Four Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767-99)

[Additional] The rough notes list the wars in a table; the narrative is as follows. Hyder Ali had risen from a soldier to ruler of Mysore (about 1761), made a strong army with French help and challenged the Company in the south.

In the First Anglo-Mysore War (1767-69) Hyder, at first ranged against the Company with the Nizam and Marathas, was joined by the Nizam only briefly (the Nizam turned to the British side in 1768); Hyder won enough victories that he reached the walls of Madras, and the war ended with the Treaty of Madras (April 1769), by which both sides returned the territory they had taken and promised to help each other against attack. The English failed to honour the promise when the Marathas attacked Mysore in 1771, and this bitterness fed the next war.

In the Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780-84), under Warren Hastings, Hyder made an alliance with the Nizam and the Marathas against the English. Tipu, his son, defeated Colonel Baillie at Pollilur (September 1780), and the Company's army under Sir Eyre Coote beat Hyder at Porto Novo (1781). Hyder died in December 1782 and Tipu carried on. The war ended with the Treaty of Mangalore (11 March 1784), which restored the position before the war; Hastings disliked the terms, which were made by the Madras government.

In the Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790-92) Tipu, allied with France, attacked Travancore (a British ally) in 1789, and Cornwallis led the Company's army, supported by the Nizam and the Marathas, to besiege Seringapatam. By the Treaty of Seringapatam (1792) Tipu surrendered nearly half his territory to the Company and its allies, paid a heavy indemnity and gave up two sons as hostages.

In the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1798-99) Wellesley, alarmed by Tipu's French contacts, attacked from all sides; Seringapatam fell and Tipu was killed (4 May 1799). Mysore was dismantled, and the remaining core was given to the Wadiyars on subsidiary terms. The Congreve rockets used at Pollilur later influenced British rocket development.

[Correction] Dating and sides. Textbooks usually give the wars as 1767-69, 1780-84, 1790-92 and 1799 (Wikipedia gives 1798-99 for the fourth); use the treaty year when it is asked. Tipu's ally against the English was France; the Nizam and the Marathas fought with the British in the third and fourth wars, and the Nizam changed sides in the first.

6.6 The Three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775-1819)

[Additional] The Maratha confederacy was led by the Peshwa at Poona with powerful chiefs: Scindia (Gwalior), Holkar (Indore), Bhonsle (Nagpur) and Gaekwad (Baroda). After the murder of the Peshwa Narayan Rao in 1773, a dispute arose between his uncle Raghunath Rao (Raghoba) and the ministers who ruled in the name of the infant Madhavrao II, led by Nana Phadnavis. Raghoba sought British help, and this gave the Company its opening.

The First Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82) began when the Bombay Council signed the Treaty of Surat (1775) with Raghoba. The Calcutta Council disapproved and itself signed the Treaty of Purandar (1776) with the Poona ministers; Bombay did not accept it and again backed Raghoba. In January 1779 a British force was surrounded at Wadgaon and forced to sign a humiliating convention. The war ended with the Treaty of Salbai (17 May 1782) (ratified in 1783), negotiated through Mahadji Scindia: the Company recognised Madhavrao II as Peshwa, Raghoba was pensioned, and the position before the war was largely restored, with Salsette remaining British. The treaty gave twenty years of peace with the Marathas, during which the Company dealt with Mysore.

The Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803-05) followed the Treaty of Bassein (1802), which the other Maratha chiefs saw as a surrender of independence. In 1803 Wellesley's brother Arthur Wellesley won at Assaye and Argaon and General Lake at Laswari, and Delhi was occupied (the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II came under Company protection). The Bhonsle signed the Treaty of Deogaon (December 1803) and Scindia the Treaty of Surji-Arjungaon (December 1803), both ceding territory (Cuttack and Balasore, the Ganga-Yamuna doab and other areas [Verification Required on details]). Holkar fought on in 1804-05 and made the Treaty of Rajpurghat (December 1805). Wellesley was recalled in 1805 because the Directors disliked the costs of his wars.

The Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817-19) was fought under Lord Hastings (section 6.8). It broke the Maratha power for good: the office of Peshwa was abolished and Maratha chiefs became subsidiary rulers.

6.7 Barlow and Minto (1805-13)

[Additional] Sir George Barlow (acting Governor-General 1805-07) reversed Wellesley's forward policy to save money and returned territories to some Maratha chiefs. During his term the Vellore Mutiny (10 July 1806) broke out among sepoys at Vellore, provoked by new dress regulations that required a new turban and banned caste marks and earrings; the Governor of Madras, Lord William Bentinck, was recalled after it. Lord Minto I (1807-13) kept the policy of avoiding wars with Indian rulers and sought to secure the north-west against Napoleon's allies. His main achievement there was the Treaty of Amritsar (April 1809) with Ranjit Singh of the Punjab, negotiated by Charles Metcalfe, which fixed the Sutlej as the limit of Ranjit Singh's expansion to the south and east, leaving the Sikh chiefs of the cis-Sutlej territory under British protection.

[Correction] Who was in office at Vellore? The 1813 charter debates are sometimes linked with Vellore; the mutiny itself occurred in 1806, during Barlow's acting term, when Bentinck was Governor of Madras [Verification Required on the dates of Barlow's acting term].

6.8 The Marquess of Hastings (1813-23)

The Governor-General of 1813 to 1823, Lord Moira, who was made Marquess of Hastings (not to be confused with Warren Hastings), returned to a forward policy and completed the conquest of the sub-continent's central and western parts.

The Anglo-Nepal (Gurkha) War (1814-16) arose from border disputes with the Gurkhas, who had expanded into Sikkim, Kumaon and Garhwal. General David Ochterlony drove them from Garhwal and Kumaon in 1815 and, after a second campaign that took him near Kathmandu, Nepal accepted the terms. The Treaty of Sugauli (drafted December 1815, signed 4 March 1816) made Nepal give up Kumaon, Garhwal and the Terai lowlands between the Kali and the Rapti, accept a permanent British Resident at Kathmandu, and renounce its claims to Sikkim. It settled the western and eastern limits of Nepal (the Kali river is the western boundary, a source of a modern border dispute at Kalapani).

The Pindaris were bands of mounted plunderers who raided Company territory from Central India and were sheltered by Maratha chiefs. Hastings used the campaign against them to settle the Maratha question. In June 1817 the Peshwa Baji Rao II was forced by the Treaty of Poona to give up the headship of the Maratha confederacy; he rose in revolt in November 1817 and was defeated at Kirkee. The Bhonsle of Nagpur was beaten at Sitabuldi (November 1817) and the Holkar at Mahidpur (December 1817), and the Holkar then accepted a subsidiary treaty at Mandsaur (1818). The Peshwa surrendered in 1818 and was pensioned off at Bithur; the office of Peshwa was abolished and his territories were merged into the Bombay Presidency, with a small state created for the descendant of Shivaji at Satara. The Rajput states, such as Jaipur and Jodhpur, accepted subsidiary alliances in 1817-18. The Company thus became the paramount power in India; only the Punjab and Sindh remained independent.

Two other matters belong here. In 1818 Hastings lifted the prior censorship of the press, but in 1823 the acting Governor-General John Adam required licences for printing, under which Rammohan Roy's Mirat-ul-Akhbar was closed [Additional]. Under Hastings and his successors the Ryotwari system (Thomas Munro in Madras, introduced widely from 1820) and the Mahalwari system (Holt Mackenzie, Regulation VII of 1822, in the North-Western Provinces) were established, which are covered in the next class (section 7.4). Singapore was also founded by Raffles in 1819 [Additional].

6.9 Lord Amherst (1823-28) and the First Anglo-Burmese War

[Additional] The Burmese kingdom of Ava expanded westwards into Assam, Manipur and Arakan, and clashed with the Company along the eastern frontier. The First Anglo-Burmese War began on 5 March 1824. In November 1824 the sepoys of the 47th Native Infantry at Barrackpore mutinied rather than go to Burma by sea, which broke their caste, and were put down with force. The war was long, costly and, in terms of men and money, the most expensive Company war until then (roughly fifteen thousand soldiers died and the cost is given as five to thirteen million pounds; it contributed to a financial crisis in India by 1833 [Wikipedia, Treaty of Yandabo]). It ended with the Treaty of Yandabo (24 February 1826): Burma gave up Assam, Arakan and the Tenasserim coast (and Manipur), ceased to interfere in Cachar and Jaintia, paid an indemnity of one million pounds in instalments and agreed to exchange diplomatic representatives. In January 1826 the Company also stormed the fortress of Bharatpur after a usurper had seized the throne there [Additional].

6.10 Lord William Bentinck (1828-35)

Bentinck is remembered for economy and reform. He was Governor-General of Bengal from 1828 and, under the 1833 Act, the first Governor-General of India (1833-35). The Company's finances were poor after the Burmese War, so he cut military allowances (the "batta"), reduced expenses and balanced the budget. He widened the use of Indians in the judicial and revenue services in subordinate posts (munsifs, sadar amins and deputy collectors) [Additional].

His most famous act was the abolition of sati. Regulation XVII of 1829, passed on 4 December 1829, declared the practice of burning or burying alive Hindu widows illegal and punishable, and those who helped were held guilty of culpable homicide; it was first extended to Bengal and then to Madras and Bombay in 1830 [Verification Required]. Bentinck acted after consulting army officers and civil servants; the campaign was led by Christian missionaries such as William Carey and Hindu reformers such as Raja Rammohan Roy (who founded the Brahmo Sabha in August 1828 [Verification Required on the exact date]), while conservative Hindus under Radhakanta Deb and the Dharma Sabha opposed the ban and appealed to the Privy Council, which upheld it. It was the first major social-reform legislation of the Company. In the early 1830s Colonel Sleeman began the campaign against the thugs (organised bands of robbers and stranglers), whose suppression was formalised in a Thuggee and Dacoity Department in 1835 [Additional].

On the political side Bentinck was no annexationist, but he took over Mysore into direct administration in 1831 for misrule, annexed Cachar (1832) and Coorg (1834) and took Jaintia in 1835 [Additional; Verification Required on these dates]. The Charter Act of 1833 (section 5.7) fell in the middle of his term and made him the first Governor-General of India; the First Law Commission under Macaulay (1834) and Macaulay's Minute on English education (2 February 1835) belong to the same period.

7. Land Revenue Policy down to 1833

7.1 Diwani and its early use

The Company began collecting land revenue in 1765 after receiving the Diwani at Allahabad (section 4.4). It had few officials who knew the land system of Bengal, so collection was left to the Naib Diwan and the zamindars, with supervisors added by the Company. The famine of 1770 (estimates of the deaths run from two to ten million) showed that revenue had been collected without regard for the people's welfare or for future income, and that officials were corrupt or lax.

7.2 Warren Hastings and revenue farming (Ijarah)

Warren Hastings (1772-85) did not devise a scientific system of land revenue but chose a temporary arrangement called revenue farming (Ijarah). The right to collect revenue in an area was auctioned to the highest bidder, the ijaradar (farmer). A Committee of Circuit toured the districts and the first auctions, in 1772, were for five years; later the lease became annual (about 1777 [Verification Required]). Collectors were appointed to supervise, and the revenue treasury was moved from Murshidabad to Calcutta. The reasons were practical: the Company had too few trained officials and no records of what each estate could pay, so an auction was a quick way to discover land's value; it also needed cash for trade purchases, wars and dividends; it did not wish to administer villages directly; and revenue farming was a Mughal practice, not new to Bengal.

The experiment failed. Many bidders offered more than the land could yield to win the lease, and then fell into arrears or ran away after extracting as much as they could; the Company's arrears kept growing. Hereditary zamindars, outbid or unable to meet inflated demands, lost their estates to speculators, often Calcutta bankers, Company servants' agents and moneylenders. The peasants bore the worst of it: high and arbitrary demands, extra cesses (abwabs), no security of tenure, flogging, eviction and flight. Farmers with short leases had no interest in improving land, and the Company's own income remained unpredictable. Notorious farmers such as Devi Singh (whose cruelty is linked to the Rangpur Dhing of 1783) are named in the accounts [Verification Required]. The system therefore did not bring the desired result for the Company and was exploitative for the peasants.

7.3 Cornwallis and the Permanent Settlement (introduction)

Parliament noticed the failure; Pitt's Act of 1784 directed the Company to change its revenue arrangements, and in 1786 the Court of Directors proposed a permanent settlement. Cornwallis (1786-93) replaced revenue farming first with annual settlements, then with a ten-year (decennial) settlement in 1790 with the zamindars, which was made permanent in 1793. Under the Permanent Settlement the zamindar was recognised as the owner of the land and paid a fixed sum to the Company; failure to pay by an appointed day (the "sunset law") meant the estate could be sold [Additional]. Its provisions and effects will be taken up in the next class.

7.4 Other systems, introduced only for completeness

[Additional] In the Madras and Bombay presidencies, the Ryotwari system (the revenue settled directly with the cultivator) was developed by Alexander Read and Thomas Munro and extended in Madras from about 1820, when Munro became governor. In the North-Western Provinces and parts of the Punjab, the Mahalwari system (settlement with the village community or estate, the mahal) was introduced by Regulation VII of 1822 under Holt Mackenzie and revised in 1833. These two systems are the subject of the next class.

8. Looking Ahead: the 1853 Act and Dalhousie (after 1833)

These topics fall after 1833 but were part of the 24 and 25 October classes, so they are carried here in brief; the full tables are in the daily files.

8.1 The Charter Act of 1853

The charter of 1833 ran out in 1854. Parliament held inquiries in 1852-53, and British opinion demanded reform of the Company's patronage. The Act of 1853 did not extend the charter for a fixed period, so the Company could rule as long as Parliament wished. It cut the Court of Directors from 24 to 18 (12 elected and 6 nominated by the Crown [Verification Required]), introduced open competition for the Indian Civil Service (the first examination in London in 1855; Satyendranath Tagore, elder brother of Rabindranath, was the first Indian to qualify, in 1863, joining in 1864), gave the Law Member full membership of the Executive Council and created a separate Legislative Council by adding six members (two judges and four provincial nominees [Verification Required]). The Legislative Council began to behave like a small parliament, which the government disliked. Company rule ended five years later after the Revolt of 1857.

8.2 Dalhousie (1848-56)

Lord Dalhousie annexed territory by two routes: peacefully, by the Doctrine of Lapse (a dependent state with no natural heir lapsed to the Company, an adopted heir not being recognised without its consent) and by the plea of misrule; and by war. By lapse: Satara (1848), the first; Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849); Baghal (1850); Udaipur in Chhattisgarh (1852); Jhansi (1853); Nagpur (1854); Tanjore and Arcot (1855). By war: the Punjab (1849), after the Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848-49) and, in 1852, Lower Burma (Pegu) after the Second Burmese War. Awadh was annexed on 11 February 1856 on the plea of misrule, not by lapse, after a report by the Resident, William Sleeman (who himself opposed annexation), and the Nawab Wajid Ali Shah was sent to Calcutta. Annexation by lapse added about four million pounds to the Company's annual revenue, and the insecurity it created among rulers and sepoys is counted among the backgrounds of the Revolt of 1857.

[Correction] Lapse and Awadh. Awadh is widely thought to have been annexed under the Doctrine of Lapse but was annexed for alleged misgovernment. The doctrine is associated with Dalhousie though the Directors had stated it as early as 1834.

Mains Points

From trader to sovereign. The Company's progress was gradual and opportunistic. It took advantage of Mughal weakness (1717 farman), used Indian rivalries (Carnatic succession disputes, Mir Jafar's plot), won through disciplined, paid, artillery-backed armies and, after Plassey and Buxar, used Bengal's revenues to finance further conquest. The Diwani of 1765 gave it a legal title for rule and a perpetual income.

From private company to instrument of the state. Each Act stripped away a layer of the Company's independence: 1773 (supervision), 1784 (political control), 1813 (monopoly of trade), 1833 (commerce itself), 1853 (patronage and fixed term), until the Crown took over in 1858. Pressure came from British industrial interests, free-trade thinkers, evangelicals and reformers more than from any concern for Indians.

Methods of expansion. Direct war (Mysore, Marathas, Nepal, Burma), the Subsidiary Alliance (a cheap protectorate that disbanded rulers' armies), non-intervention when the Company lacked strength (Shore, Barlow), and later lapse and misrule (Dalhousie). The Company moved between forward and cautious policy according to costs and the European situation.

Administration and society. Cornwallis's code and civil service gave a rule-bound administration but excluded Indians from higher posts; revenue policy (farming, Permanent Settlement) transformed the countryside; Bentinck's reforms and the 1833 Act pointed to a more "improving" state, but equal opportunity stayed largely theoretical.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Columbus found the Americas, Diaz the Cape (1488), Gama the sea route (1498). EIC (1600) is older than the VOC (1602), but the French were last.
  • Charter renewal is 1609, not 1615; Hawkins failed and Roe succeeded, both at Jahangir's court; Surat 1613 was the first permanent EIC factory; Fort St George, not Fort William, is the first British fort.
  • Charnock settled in 1690; 1698 is the zamindari purchase. Mahe is in Kerala, not at Pondicherry.
  • Dupleix was governor of the French Company, not of the French government; the French returned in 1763 only as unfortified traders.
  • Munro commanded at Buxar, Clive at Plassey; the Company pays the Emperor Rs 26 lakh at Allahabad, not the reverse.
  • Two dual governments: Clive's in Bengal (1765-72) and Pitt's dual control in England (1784-1858).
  • Regulating Act: GG of Bengal, casting vote only; the override power began in 1786, not 1784 or 1793; Board of Control is 1784, Supreme Court 1774.
  • 1813 ended the monopoly except tea and China; 1833 ended those and the commercial role. Governor-General of Bengal (1773) vs of India (1833): Bentinck is the first of India.
  • Hyderabad (1798), not Mysore, signed first; Treaty of Bassein is 31 December 1802; Warren Hastings and the Marquess of Hastings are different persons.
  • Vellore (1806) is under Barlow, not Minto or Hastings; Barrackpore (1824) is under Amherst; Sati Regulation (1829) is under Bentinck, not Hastings.
  • Awadh (1856) was annexed for misrule, not lapse; Satara (1848) was the first lapse; Punjab (1849) by war.

30-Second Revision

Europeans came by the Cape route (Gama 1498): Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danes, French. EIC: 1599/1600 charter; factories at Surat 1613, Madras 1639, Bombay 1668, Calcutta 1690. Mughal decline, 1717 farman, Nadir Shah 1739, Panipat 1761. Carnatic Wars (1746-63) removed the French. Bengal: Plassey 1757, Buxar 1764, Diwani 1765, Dual Government 1765-72. Acts: 1773 (GG of Bengal, Supreme Court 1774), 1781, 1784 (Board of Control), 1786 (override), 1793, 1813 (monopoly ended except tea/China), 1833 (GG of India, Law Commission). Governors-General: Hastings (Maratha I, Mysore II), Cornwallis (Mysore III, Permanent Settlement), Shore, Wellesley (subsidiary alliance, Mysore IV, Maratha II), Minto (Amritsar 1809), Hastings-Moira (Nepal 1816, Maratha III 1817-19), Amherst (Burma 1826), Bentinck (sati 1829, GG of India). Revenue: Diwani, farming (1772), Permanent Settlement (1793), then Ryotwari and Mahalwari.

By which one of the following Acts was the Governor General of Bengal designated as the Governor General of India?

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 - General Studies Paper I

Which one of the following statements does not apply to the system of Subsidiary Alliance introduced by Lord Wellesley?

  1. To maintain a large standing army at others' expense
  2. To keep India safe from Napoleonic danger
  3. To secure a fixed income for the Company
  4. To establish British paramountcy over the Indian States
Correct answer: (c). The aim was paramountcy and keeping the French out, not a fixed income (section 6.4). Verified: PMF IAS.

3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2003

With reference to the entry of European powers into India, which one of the following statements is not correct?

  1. The Portuguese captured Goa in 1499
  2. The English opened their first factory in South India at Masulipatam
  3. In Eastern India, the English Company opened its first factory in Orissa in 1633
  4. Under the leadership of Dupleix, the French occupied Madras in 1746
Correct answer: (a). Goa was captured in 1510 (section 1.2); Madras fell to the French in 1746 (section 3). Verified: PMF IAS.

At a time when empires in Europe were crumbling before the might of Napoleon, which one of the following Governors-General kept the British flag flying high in India?

  1. Warren Hastings
  2. Lord Cornwallis
  3. Lord Wellesley
  4. Lord Hastings
Correct answer: (c) Lord Wellesley (section 6.4). Verified: PMF IAS.
