1. British Occupation of Bengal

1.1 From Alivardi Khan to Siraj-ud-Daulah

  • Bengal became independent (semi-independent) during the reign of the Mughal emperor Muhammad Shah (1719-48).
  • After several years Bengal had a new Nawab, Alivardi Khan (1740-56).
  • Alivardi had three daughters but no male child, so on his deathbed he nominated his daughter's son as Nawab: Siraj-ud-Daulah (1756-57), who came to the throne after the death of his maternal grandfather.
  • Siraj was young and aggressive but lacked political vision.
  • In 1756, the year he became Nawab, a war broke out in Europe - the Seven Years' War (1756-63).
  • Expecting that the European war might spread to India, both companies (English and French) began to protect their factories. A series of steps taken (mainly by the English) strained relations between the Nawab and the Company, resulting in the Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757) (Map 1).

[Correction] Names, dates and succession. "Mohd Shah" = Muhammad Shah; "Ali Vardhi Khan" = Alivardi Khan; "Nawaab" = Nawab; "Siraj-ud-daula" = Siraj-ud-Daulah. Bengal's autonomy began under Murshid Quli Khan (Nazim from about 1717, see the successor-states table in the 10-23 file); his successors Shuja-ud-din (1727-39) and Sarfaraz Khan (1739-40) preceded Alivardi, who took power by defeating Sarfaraz in 1740 (Battle of Giria) [Verification Required] on the intermediate Nawabs and the battle. Alivardi died on 9 April 1756; he had declared Siraj his heir apparent in May 1752, not only on his deathbed, and Siraj succeeded in April 1756 aged about 23. Siraj's mother Amina Begum was Alivardi's youngest daughter (Siraj ud-Daulah, Wikipedia). His reign ended with his execution on 2 July 1757 after Plassey. Whether the grandfather's nomination mattered: Siraj's accession angered his aunt Ghasiti Begum and his cousin Shaukat Jang (see c03).

[Correction] The Seven Years' War. The blank "(17 )" in the notes is 1756-63, fought mainly between Britain and France and their allies. The Indian theatre was the Third Carnatic War (1758-63), with the French defeat at Wandiwash (1760), the fall of Pondicherry (1761) and the Treaty of Paris (1763), which returned five French settlements but barred fortifications and troops in Bengal (French India, Wikipedia; see French settlements, 10-23 file). [Additional] The French fort at Chandannagar was taken by Clive in March 1757, shortly before Plassey [Verification Required] on the date (23 March 1757).

  1. Fortification of Fort William (without the Nawab's approval).
  2. Placing cannons on the walls of the fort.
  3. Giving shelter to Krishna Ballabh, an officer under the Nawab accused of financial fraud.
  4. Supporting a rival candidate for the throne, Ghasiti Begum.
  5. Misuse of the farman of 1717 (dastaks) (see c15 in the 10-23 file).

Siraj's three charges recorded in the sources match A, C and E: that the Company strengthened Fort William without approval, abused trade privileges (causing heavy loss of customs) and sheltered his officers such as Krishnadas, son of Rajballav (Siraj ud-Daulah, Wikipedia). Textbooks list "support to Siraj's rivals" among the causes; the extent of the Company's role is [Verification Required]. [Additional] Calcutta fell to Siraj in June 1756 (briefly renamed Alinagar); the "Black Hole" imprisonment followed (night of 20-21 June 1756). The number of deaths (123 of 146 in Holwell's account) is disputed by historians and should be quoted as "according to Holwell" [Verification Required]. The Company's reinforcements under Robert Clive and Admiral Watson came from Madras, and the Treaty of Alinagar (9 February 1757) restored Calcutta [Verification Required].

The Battle of Buxar (22-23 October 1764)

  • The alliance: the fugitive Nawab Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula (Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal wazir) and the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, with Balwant Singh of Benares, united against the Company.
  • The battle: fought at Buxar in Bihar, on the Ganga about 130 km west of Patna (Map 1), between the Company's army under Major Hector Munro (about 7,072 men: about 1,800 British regulars and 5,300 sepoys) and an allied army estimated at over 40,000.
  • The result: the Company's lines reversed the allied advance and the battle was over by midday. Shuja-ud-Daula blew up his magazines and destroyed the boat-bridge over the Karmanasa after crossing, abandoning the Emperor; Mir Qasim fled (and was expelled by Shuja on 23 October 1764); the Emperor chose to negotiate.
  • The outcome: the defeated rulers submitted to the Company in 1765; Buxar made the Company the military power of north-east India and is judged more decisive than Plassey (Battle of Buxar; Mir Qasim, Wikipedia).

[Correction] Reasons for defeat - which battle? The notes list reasons for the defeat and name General Hector Munro. Munro commanded at Buxar (1764), not at Plassey, where the Company commander was Clive. Plassey was won largely through the conspiracy, so the four reasons in the notes apply best to Buxar: (1) the Company's army was better trained and disciplined; (2) the Company was a good paymaster and its soldiers had no complaints about salary, whereas Indian soldiers were paid irregularly; (3) better arms and ammunition and superior generalship (Munro); (4) the Indian side lacked unity of command - illustrated by Shuja-ud-Daula abandoning the Emperor. Ranks: Hector Munro was a Major at Buxar and became "Sir Hector Munro" later [Verification Required] on the rank in the textbook.

1.5 Dual Government of Bengal (1765-72)

  • In February 1765 the EIC signed an agreement with Najm-ud-Daula (the notes write "Nizam-ud-daula"), son of Mir Jafar, and asked him to reduce his army.
  • The treaty provided that Bengal would be administered through a deputy subedar (deputy Nawab), who was appointed by the Company and could not be removed without the Company's prior approval: the right of administration (Nizamat) thus passed into the Company's hands.
  • The Treaty of Allahabad (1765) with Shah Alam II gave the Company the Diwani of Bengal.
  • With these two treaties the Company had administrative and revenue control over Bengal but no responsibility: the dual government - power without responsibility.

[Correction] Name, date and the nature of "dual government". The son of Mir Jafar who succeeded in 1765 is Najm-ud-Daula (also spelt Najimuddaula; some books write Nizam-ud-daula) [Verification Required] on the spelling and the exact date of the treaty (February 1765). The Company ruled through deputies - Muhammad Reza Khan for Bengal and Raja Shitab Roy for Bihar [Verification Required] - so the Company collected revenue (Diwani) while the Nawab's deputy ran justice and police (Nizamat) with no funds or army. The system is usually credited to Robert Clive, back as Governor in 1765. [Additional] It ended in 1772 when Warren Hastings took over revenue administration directly [Verification Required]; it coincided with the Bengal famine of 1770, usually cited as a result of the Company's revenue policy [Verification Required].

2. Subsidiary Alliance

  • The policy was originally planned by the French "government" - Dupleix.
  • In India it was pursued aggressively by Richard Colley Wellesley (Lord Wellesley, 1798-1805) because of the Napoleonic threat to British India (Map 2).
  • Through it he wanted to create a sphere of influence over the Indian states.

[Correction] Dupleix and Wellesley. The system was first pioneered in the Carnatic by Joseph Francois Dupleix (Governor of the French Company's settlements at Pondicherry from 1742, not "the French government"): he hired out French troops to Indian rulers in return for payment or territory in the 1740s. The Company later used the same method after Plassey (Clive's terms for Mir Jafar) and Buxar (the 1765 settlement), and it reached its most systematic form under Wellesley (Governor-General 1798-1805) (Subsidiary alliance, Wikipedia). In a dispatch of February 1801 Wellesley described the aim as placing the allied states in "such a degree of dependence on the British power" that they could not form a confederacy against it. "Richard Colley Wellesley" is correct (also written Wellesley or Wesley). The Napoleonic threat - Napoleon's expedition to Egypt (1798) and Tipu Sultan's links with France - is the usual background (UPSC 2018 explanation, PYQ 1).

2.1 Conditions of the Subsidiary Alliance

  1. The Indian ruler had to keep a British force in his kingdom and pay for it (the soldiers' salary).
  2. He could not sign a treaty with any other state without the Company's approval.
  3. He had to keep a British officer - the Resident - at his court.
  4. He could not employ any European without the Company's permission.
  5. The Company agreed not to interfere in the internal affairs of the state.

[Correction] Completing the conditions. The five points are right; the sources add: (a) the ruler paid in cash or by ceding territory if he could not meet the cost; (b) he was forbidden an independent standing army (his own forces were disbanded or reduced), and (c) in return the Company protected him against external attack. On point 5: although internal administration formally stayed with the ruler, Company agents interfered increasingly in succession and governance, so "non-interference" was a promise honoured mainly in the breach (Subsidiary alliance; PMF IAS (2018)). The aims were military and political dominance and paramountcy, not a fixed income (UPSC 2018, PYQ 1).

[Correction] Lapse, misrule and war. (1) The Doctrine of Lapse held that a dependent state whose ruler died without a natural heir (an adopted heir not being recognised without the Company's consent) would "lapse" to the Company; it was articulated by the Court of Directors as early as 1834, but is most associated with Dalhousie, and it added about four million pounds sterling to the Company's annual revenue (Doctrine of lapse, Wikipedia). (2) Awadh is widely believed to have been annexed under the doctrine but was in fact annexed on the plea of misgovernance: a report by the Resident, William Sleeman, described "maladministration" (Sleeman himself opposed annexation); annexation took place on 11 February 1856 and the Nawab, Wajid Ali Shah, was exiled to Garden Reach near Calcutta (Wajid Ali Shah, Wikipedia). (3) The word "peaceful" in the notes means without war; the Punjab was annexed by war. The Second Anglo-Sikh War began with the murder of Vans Agnew and Anderson at Multan (19 April 1848); Dalhousie annexed the Punjab in 1849 (Second Anglo-Sikh War, Wikipedia). The Doctrine of Lapse is Dalhousie's (UPSC 2002, PYQ 3). [Verification Required] for the lesser states' dates against the textbook list (Jaitpur 1849, Baghal 1850, Udaipur 1852).

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Alivardi Khan (1740-56) d. 9 April 1756; Siraj-ud-Daulah Nawab April 1756, executed 2 July 1757; Seven Years' War 1756-63.
  • Plassey: 23 June 1757 (Clive; Mir Jafar's betrayal); Buxar: 22-23 October 1764 (Major Hector Munro; Mir Qasim + Shuja-ud-Daula + Shah Alam II).
  • Treaty of Allahabad, 16 August 1765: Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, Odisha; the Company pays the Emperor Rs 26 lakh; Awadh pays Rs 50 lakh; Allahabad and Kora to the Emperor.
  • Dual Government 1765-72: Company holds Diwani + army; Nawab's deputy holds Nizamat; power without responsibility; ended by Warren Hastings (1772).
  • Subsidiary Alliance: pioneered by Dupleix; systematised by Wellesley (1798-1805); first: Hyderabad 1798; Mysore 1799; Tanjore 1799; Awadh 1801; Peshwa: Treaty of Bassein, 31 December 1802; later Bhonsle 1803, Scindia 1804.
  • Anglo-Mysore: 1767-69 (Madras), 1780-84 (Mangalore), 1790-92 (Seringapatam), 1798-99 (Tipu killed).
  • Dalhousie (1848-56): Lapse - Satara 1848, Jaitpur and Sambalpur 1849, Baghal 1850, Udaipur 1852, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854, Tanjore and Arcot 1855; Awadh 1856 - misrule; Punjab 1849 - war.

Mains Points

  • Why Bengal first: its wealth, the weakness of the Nawab's court (rivalries, bankers' fears) and the Company's use of its trade privileges; the Seven Years' War pushed both Europeans to fortify.
  • Plassey vs Buxar: Plassey gave political leverage through conspiracy; Buxar gave military supremacy and the Treaty of Allahabad (Diwani) - the legal basis of Company rule.
  • Dual government: a system of power without responsibility - revenue collected by the Company, administration left to a powerless Nawab - that led to oppression of the peasantry and weakened the economy of Bengal (famine of 1770).
  • Subsidiary Alliance: a cheap way to hold the country - the ruler paid for the Company's army, lost his armed force and foreign relations, and the state was reduced to a protectorate; weakened allies, cost the Company nothing, kept the French out.
  • Dalhousie: annexation by lapse and misrule (and war) completed the expansion; the sense of insecurity among rulers and sepoys is counted among the background of the 1857 Revolt (next topic).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Hector Munro is Buxar, not Plassey (Clive); Buxar was a hard fight, Plassey was won by a conspiracy.
  • Treaty of Allahabad: the Company paid Rs 26 lakh to the Emperor; the Emperor did not pay a "pension" to the Company.
  • Ghasiti Begum was Siraj's aunt (a woman rival), while Shaukat Jang was the male cousin.
  • Siraj was named heir in 1752, not only on the deathbed; he was Alivardi's grandson through his youngest daughter.
  • Dual government = Company had Diwani and the army, Nawab had Nizamat without power; it ended in 1772.
  • Dupleix was a Governor of the French Company, not the French government; Wellesley systematised the subsidiary system.
  • First to sign: Hyderabad (1798); Treaty of Bassein = 31 December 1802 (not 30).
  • The subsidiary alliance was not aimed at "a fixed income for the Company" (UPSC 2018) but at paramountcy and keeping the French out.
  • Awadh (1856) was annexed for misrule, not by the Doctrine of Lapse; Punjab (1849) was annexed after a war; Satara (1848) was the first lapse.
  • Doctrine of Lapse existed before Dalhousie (1834 Directors) but is his name.

30-Second Revision

Bengal: Alivardi (1740-56) - Siraj (1756-57); Seven Years' War 1756-63; causes - Fort William fortification, cannons, Krishna Ballabh, rivals (Ghasiti Begum, Shaukat Jang), dastaks; betrayers - Omichand, Manik Chand, Mir Jafar, Jagat Seth; Plassey 23 Jun 1757; Mir Jafar (1757-60), Mir Qasim (1760-63; Munger; duties abolished; Patna 1763), Mir Jafar again; Buxar 22-23 Oct 1764 (Munro; Mir Qasim + Shuja + Shah Alam II); Treaty of Allahabad 1765 (Diwani; Rs 26 lakh; Awadh Rs 50 lakh); Dual Government 1765-72. Subsidiary Alliance: Dupleix; Wellesley 1798-1805; five conditions; Hyderabad 1798, Mysore 1799 (Anglo-Mysore Wars 1767-99), Tanjore 1799, Awadh 1801, Peshwa 31 Dec 1802. Dalhousie 1848-56: Lapse - Satara 1848, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854; Awadh 1856 (misrule); Punjab 1849 (war).

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 - General Studies Paper I

Which one of the following statements does not apply to the system of Subsidiary Alliance introduced by Lord Wellesley?

  1. To maintain a large standing army at others' expense
  2. To keep India safe from Napoleonic danger
  3. To secure a fixed income for the Company
  4. To establish British paramountcy over the Indian States
Correct answer: (c) To secure a fixed income for the Company. The aim was military and political dominance and paramountcy, with the cost of the army borne by the ally and the French kept out (section 2.1, c09). Verified: PMF IAS.
