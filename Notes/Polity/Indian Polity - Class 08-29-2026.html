1. Supreme Court Judgments on Tribunals

Central Question: Are Tribunals Constitutionally Valid?

The constitutional validity of tribunals established under Articles 323A and 323B was tested through a series of landmark cases.

1.1 L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

A seven-judge Constitution Bench delivered this landmark verdict. Key holdings:

  1. Tribunals are constitutionally valid as they help the judiciary by reducing its burden through specialised adjudication.
  2. However, tribunals shall always be subject to the writ jurisdiction of the High Court under Articles 226 and 227.
  3. The writ jurisdiction of HCs under Article 226 is part of the basic structure of the Constitution and cannot be taken away even through a constitutional amendment.
  4. Therefore, clauses 2(d) of Article 323A and 3(d) of Article 323B (which sought to exclude HC and SC jurisdiction over tribunals) were declared unconstitutional.
  5. Awards of tribunals functioning parallel to the HC can always be challenged through writs before a Division Bench of the HC.
  6. Tribunals functioning parallel to HCs may interpret laws governing their subject matter but cannot interpret the statute which created them (i.e., they cannot examine the constitutionality of their parent Act).
[Correction] The rough notes stated that "Under Art 136 appeal can be made only in SC, later became invalid acc to Art 226 and 227." This is imprecise. Article 136 (Special Leave Petition to the Supreme Court) was never "invalidated." What L. Chandra Kumar held was that the first tier of judicial review of tribunal orders lies with the HC under Art 226/227 (not directly to the SC under Art 136). The SC under Art 136 remains available as an appellate remedy after the HC stage, not as the sole remedy bypassing HCs.
Exam Point: L. Chandra Kumar (1997) established a two-tier review: (i) first challenge to tribunal orders before a Division Bench of the HC under Art 226/227; (ii) thereafter, appeal to the SC. This is the most frequently tested tribunal case in UPSC.

1.2 Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019)

  • The Supreme Court gave directions to the Government of India for reforming the functioning of tribunals to make them more independent of executive influence.
  • The Court directed the creation of a National Tribunals Commission (NTC) to handle:
    • Appointment and removal of tribunal members
    • Financing of tribunal requirements
  • The purpose was to end direct government involvement in these processes.
[Correction] The rough notes referred to this as "Roger Mathew's case." The correct name is Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd., Civil Appeal No. 8588/2019, decided on 13 November 2019.

1.3 Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 and Madras Bar Association

  • Parliament enacted the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 to bring reforms in tribunal functioning.
  • In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India, the Supreme Court declared key provisions of this Act as unconstitutional, finding that the reforms did not go far enough to ensure independence and in fact imposed conditions (such as short tenures and age limits) that undermined tribunal independence.
[Correction] The rough notes attributed this to "Madras Bar Association case 2025." The SC struck down provisions of the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 in Madras Bar Association v. 2. Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) [Not mentioned in the Constitution]

ADR refers to methods of resolving disputes outside the regular court system. The term "ADR" is an academic/practice classification and is not used in the Constitution itself, though Article 39A (DPSP) promotes access to justice, and various statutes provide for ADR mechanisms.

[Additional] The degree of formality increases from Negotiation → Mediation → Conciliation → Arbitration. Courts themselves may also refer disputes to ADR processes under Section 89 of the CPC, 1908.

2.1 Negotiation

  • The parties in dispute themselves bargain to arrive at a mutually settled agreement.
  • No third party is involved in the process.
  • The settlement through negotiation is not legally binding on the parties.
  • Most informal of all ADR methods.

2.2 Mediation — Mediation Act, 2023

  • A neutral third party called a mediator facilitates negotiation between the parties and helps them arrive at a mutually agreed settlement.
  • The mediator does not impose a solution — the decision remains with the parties.
  • After the enactment of the Mediation Act, 2023, a settlement obtained through mediation is legally binding on the parties, with no appeal against it.

Key Features of the Mediation Act, 2023

  • Provides for the creation of the Mediation Council of India to regulate mediation in the country.
  • Prescribes qualifications for mediators.
  • Provides timelines within which mediation shall be completed.
  • Covers all civil disputes and compoundable criminal offences of domestic nature.
  • Promotes institutional mediation and accepts online mediation as a valid process.
[Additional] The Mediation Act, 2023 was passed by Rajya Sabha on 1 August 2023 and Lok Sabha on 7 August 2023. It received Presidential assent on 14 September 2023. Not all sections are yet in force; the Act is being operationalised in phases.

2.3 Conciliation — Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996

  • A neutral third party called a conciliator helps the parties in dispute arrive at a mutually agreed settlement through negotiation.
  • Key difference from mediation: The conciliator may offer solutions of their own. If the parties accept, the settlement becomes binding.
  • There is no appeal against the settlement agreement.
  • Governed by Part III of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

2.4 Arbitration — Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996

  • A neutral third party called an arbitrator (or an arbitration tribunal) decides the dispute between the parties using an adversarial method.
  • The award is binding upon the parties.
  • Governed by Part I of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

How Arbitration Differs from Courts

  1. Not bound by procedural laws applicable to courts (CPC/CrPC); may adopt own procedures consistent with principles of natural justice.
  2. The parties themselves decide: choice of arbitrator, place of arbitration (physical venue), seat of arbitration (which court's law governs), fees, and language.
  3. The award of arbitration is final and binding on the parties.

Remedies vs Appeals in ADR

Exam Point: Mediation, conciliation, and arbitration settlements may have remedies (e.g., setting aside an arbitral award under Section 34 of the 1996 Act) but there are generally no appeals as in regular court proceedings. The distinction between "remedy" (challenging on limited grounds) and "appeal" (re-hearing on merits) is critical.

Gayatri Balaswamy Case (2025)

  • The Supreme Court held that courts may even modify the award of arbitration under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act in some exceptional cases.
[Correction] The rough notes stated that Section 34 "originally provided courts only with the power to stay the execution of an arbitration award." This is inaccurate. Section 34 provides grounds for setting aside an arbitral award (not merely staying its execution). Section 36 deals with enforcement of awards. Historically, courts could only set aside (annul) an award under Section 34, but could not modify it — the court either upheld or set aside the award. The significance of this case, if verified, is that it expanded the court's power to modify (not just annul) awards. [Verification Required] The case name "Gayatri Balaswamy" and its 2025 date could not be confirmed from official sources.

3. Lok Adalats

Constitutional and Statutory Basis

  • Established under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, enacted by Parliament.
  • The Act was enacted to implement Article 39A (DPSP), which provides that the State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity, and shall provide free legal aid to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities.
  • Article 39A was added through the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.
[Additional] The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 was enacted in 1987 but came into force on 9 November 1995. NALSA was constituted on 5 December 1995. 9 November is observed as National Legal Services Day.

Key Functions

  1. Implement free legal aid schemes at their respective levels.
  2. Provide free legal aid to eligible persons:
    • Persons below prescribed income levels
    • Women, SC/STs, children
    • Victims of disasters, trafficking, industrial disasters
    • Persons in custody
    • Mentally ill persons, persons with disabilities
  3. Organise Lok Adalats at their respective levels.
  4. Spread legal awareness among the people.

[Correction] The rough notes stated that permanent Lok Adalats use "Conciliation" and temporary Lok Adalats use "Mediation." This overly rigid distinction is misleading. Both types use conciliation and mediation techniques. The key difference is that a Permanent Lok Adalat, if conciliation fails, has the power to decide the dispute on merits (Section 22C(8)), whereas a regular Lok Adalat cannot impose a decision and must return the case to the referring court if settlement fails.

Similarities

  • Both are free of cost legal remedies (no court fee).
  • Settlements in both are legally binding with no appeal.
  • Both Lok Adalats have the powers of a civil court (Section 22(5) and 22E).
  • Awards are deemed decrees of a civil court and are enforceable as such.
Exam Point: Lok Adalat awards are final and binding with no appeal. This is a frequent UPSC question. The key trade-off: speed and cost vs appellate remedy.

4. Family Courts

  • Established under the Family Courts Act, 1984, enacted by Parliament.
  • States shall establish Family Courts in every district or group of districts where the population exceeds 1 million (10 lakhs).
  • States may create them in other districts as well, in consultation with the High Court.

Composition and Appointment

  • Each Family Court is presided over by a single judge appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court.
  • The judge must be or have been a judicial officer (district judge level or above), or an advocate with at least 7 years' experience, or a person with relevant experience in family law, sociology, or social work.
  • Preference is given to women candidates (Section 4(4)).
  • The State Government may also associate counsellors (including persons from welfare organisations) with the Family Court to assist in conciliation (Section 6).

Jurisdiction

Family Courts have exclusive jurisdiction over the following civil matters governed by personal laws:

  1. Matrimonial relief — nullity of marriage, judicial separation, divorce, restitution of conjugal rights, declaration of marital status
  2. Property disputes between spouses or arising out of marriage
  3. Maintenance — claims under Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS, or under personal law statutes (Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, Muslim personal law, etc.)
  4. Guardianship and custody of minors (under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, or the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956)
  5. Adoption
  6. Succession and inheritance (in some States, where notified)
Exam Point: Family Courts handle only civil family matters. Criminal complaints under Section 498A IPC / BNS (cruelty by husband or his relatives) or under the Domestic Violence Act, 2005 are not within Family Court jurisdiction — they are tried by Magistrate's courts.

Procedure and Key Features

  • Mandatory conciliation: The court must make efforts to settle the dispute through conciliation in the first instance (Section 9). If settlement is reached, it is recorded and has the force of a decree.
  • Counsellors: The court may refer parties to counsellors for mediation before or during proceedings (Section 6).
  • Restriction on lawyers: No party shall be entitled as of right to be represented by a legal practitioner (Section 13). The court may, in the interest of justice, permit legal representation for both parties.
  • Simplified procedure: The court may adopt its own procedure consistent with principles of natural justice. Strict rules of evidence (Indian Evidence Act) may be relaxed.
  • In-camera proceedings: Hearings may be held in private to protect the privacy of the parties.

Appeals

  • Appeals against Family Court orders and decrees lie to the High Court (Section 19).
  • Where the Family Court is presided over by a District Judge or equivalent, the appeal lies directly to the HC, bypassing intermediate appellate courts.
[Additional] The Family Courts Act was enacted following the recommendation of the Law Commission of India (59th Report, 1974), which suggested that family disputes should be treated differently from ordinary civil disputes. The Act's emphasis on conciliation mirrors the approach of ADR methods like mediation and Lok Adalats.

5. Gram Nyayalayas

  • Established under the Gram Nyayalaya Act, 2008 (Act No. 4 of 2009), enacted by Parliament.
  • The Act received Presidential assent on 7 January 2009 and came into force on 2 October 2009 (Gandhi Jayanti).
  • State Governments may establish Gram Nyayalayas as mobile courts with headquarters at the level where the intermediate-level Panchayat (Block / Taluka level) is located in the district.
  • Where there is no intermediate-level Panchayat, they may be established for a group of contiguous Panchayats.

Nyayadhikari (Presiding Officer)

  • The presiding officer is called Nyayadhikari (Sections 5-7).
  • Must be eligible to be appointed as a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class.
  • Appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court.
  • Has the same powers, salary and benefits as a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class.
  • The Nyayadhikari travels to the villages within the jurisdiction to hear disputes — hence "mobile court." The court can function at any place within its jurisdiction after giving wide publicity (Section 9).

Jurisdiction

Criminal Jurisdiction (First Schedule)

  • Gram Nyayalayas have jurisdiction over criminal offences specified in the First Schedule of the Act.
  • These include relatively minor offences such as: theft (where value of property does not exceed a threshold), criminal misappropriation, mischief, assault, public nuisance, offences relating to marriage, and certain compoundable offences.
  • Offences are tried by summary procedure in accordance with Chapter XXI of the CrPC, 1973 (now corresponding provisions of BNSS, 2023).
  • The Act also allows plea bargaining in accordance with Chapter XXIA of the CrPC (Section 20).

Civil Jurisdiction (Second Schedule)

  • Gram Nyayalayas have jurisdiction over civil disputes specified in the Second Schedule.
  • These include: disputes relating to property, land, rent, wages, trespass, water disputes, maintenance claims, and disputes arising under special Acts like the MGNREGA, Forest Rights Act, etc.
  • The pecuniary jurisdiction (monetary limit) is fixed by the respective High Court (Section 13(2)).

Power to Amend Schedules

WhoWhatProcedure

Procedure and Special Features

  • Summary trial: Criminal cases are tried by summary procedure (Chapter XXI CrPC). In a summary trial, the Magistrate records the substance of evidence (not a full record) and may convict and sentence immediately. This drastically reduces the time taken per case.
  • Day-to-day hearing: Civil suits are heard on a day-to-day basis with limited adjournments (Section 24(8)).
  • Disposal within 6 months: Civil suits must be disposed of within six months from the date of institution (Section 24(8)).
  • Conciliation first: The Gram Nyayalaya must try to settle the dispute through conciliation in the first instance (Section 26). If a settlement is reached, it is recorded and is binding.
  • Relaxed evidence rules: The Gram Nyayalaya has the power to accept certain evidence that would otherwise be inadmissible under the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (Chapter VI of the Act). This is to ensure substantive justice is not defeated by technicalities.
  • Court fees: Civil suit fees shall not exceed Rs 100, irrespective of the value of the property in dispute (Section 24). This makes access extremely affordable.
  • Special procedure in civil matters: The Gram Nyayalaya may follow special procedures as it deems just and reasonable in the interest of justice (Section 24(6)), free from strict CPC requirements.
  • Not bound by strict CPC: The court is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure but must follow principles of natural justice.
  • Legal aid: The Gram Nyayalaya shall ensure that poor and illiterate litigants receive free legal aid through the Legal Services Authorities.

Appeals

Exam Point: Unlike Lok Adalat awards (no appeal), Gram Nyayalaya judgments can be appealed — criminal to Sessions Court, civil to District Court. The one-month appeal period is shorter than the standard 30/90-day periods under CrPC/CPC.

Implementation Status

  • Despite being enacted in 2008, Gram Nyayalayas have been established in very few States due to inadequate infrastructure, staffing, financial constraints, and reluctance of lawyers and police.
  • As of available data, only about 208 Gram Nyayalayas were functional against a target of 5,000 — a stark implementation gap.
  • The Supreme Court has on multiple occasions directed State Governments to establish Gram Nyayalayas, but progress remains poor.
  • Major challenges include: lack of dedicated funding, reluctance of State Governments, shortage of judicial officers willing to serve in rural areas, and resistance from the legal profession.
[Correction] The rough notes used "Nyay Adhikari" (two words). The correct term under the Act is Nyayadhikari (one word). Also, "Judicial Magistrate class 1" should be Judicial Magistrate of the First Class.
[Additional] The Gram Nyayalaya Act's long title states its purpose: "to provide for the establishment of Gram Nyayalayas at the grass roots level for the purposes of providing access to justice to the citizens at their doorsteps and to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of social, economic or other disabilities." This mirrors the language of Article 39A (DPSP on equal justice and free legal aid).

Summary Trial vs Regular Trial

Gram Nyayalayas try criminal cases by summary procedure under Chapter XXI of the CrPC, 1973 (now corresponding provisions under BNSS, 2023). Understanding the difference is important:

FeatureRegular TrialSummary Trial (Chapter XXI CrPC)
Record of evidenceFull verbatim record of all evidenceMagistrate records only the substance of evidence; no detailed depositions
ProcedureFull trial procedure with examination, cross-examination, argumentsSimplified and abbreviated; fewer procedural steps
Maximum sentenceAs prescribed by the substantive law for the offenceCannot exceed 3 months' imprisonment in a summary trial (Section 263 CrPC)
JudgmentDetailed judgment with reasonsBrief judgment containing the substance of evidence and the finding
Who can conductAny competent Magistrate or Sessions JudgeChief Judicial Magistrate, Metropolitan Magistrate, or JMFC (for specified offences)
Used byRegular courtsGram Nyayalayas (mandatorily) and other Magistrate's courts (for petty offences)

Exam Point: Gram Nyayalayas use summary trial for criminal cases. The maximum sentence a Gram Nyayalaya can impose is limited to 3 months' imprisonment under summary procedure (even if the substantive law prescribes a higher sentence), because the Nyayadhikari acts as a JMFC conducting a summary trial. Additionally, Gram Nyayalayas allow plea bargaining (Chapter XXIA CrPC / BNSS), which regular lower courts also permit for offences punishable with up to 7 years.

Key Criminal Law Classifications

ClassificationType AType B
By settlementCompoundable: Parties can compromise and settle; court permission may be needed for some offencesNon-compoundable: Cannot be settled between parties; trial must proceed
By FIR/warrantCognizable: Police can register FIR, investigate, and arrest without warrantNon-cognizable: Police cannot register FIR or investigate without Magistrate's order

Relevance to Gram Nyayalayas and ADR

  • Gram Nyayalayas can try only offences specified in their First Schedule — these are generally minor, compoundable, and bailable offences suitable for summary trial.
  • Non-compoundable and serious offences (murder, robbery, dacoity, etc.) are excluded from Gram Nyayalaya jurisdiction.
  • ADR methods (Lok Adalats, mediation) can handle compoundable offences but not non-compoundable ones.
  • The plea bargaining option in Gram Nyayalayas further speeds up disposal by allowing the accused to negotiate a lesser sentence in exchange for a guilty plea.
[Additional] These classifications are defined in the First Schedule of the CrPC, 1973 (now BNSS, 2023). The First Schedule classifies every offence as cognizable/non-cognizable, bailable/non-bailable, and specifies whether it is triable by a Magistrate or Sessions Judge.

7. Judicial Review (Introduction)

The rough notes mention "Judicial Review" but do not provide substantive content. This topic will be covered in a subsequent class.

[Additional] Judicial review is the power of the judiciary to examine the constitutionality of legislative acts and executive orders. In India, it derives from Articles 13, 32, 131-136, 226, and 227. The Supreme Court has held judicial review to be part of the basic structure of the Constitution (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997; Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).

8. Key Facts for Prelims

  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997): Tribunals valid; HC writ jurisdiction under Art 226 = basic structure; cannot be excluded; Division Bench review of tribunal orders.
  • Rojer Mathew (2019): Directed creation of National Tribunals Commission; reduce executive influence.
  • Mediation Act, 2023: Mediator facilitates; settlement binding; no appeal; Mediation Council of India; covers civil disputes + compoundable criminal offences.
  • Arbitration & Conciliation Act, 1996: Part I = Arbitration; Part III = Conciliation. Award final and binding.
  • Section 34: Setting aside arbitral award (not staying execution). Section 36 = enforcement.
  • Art 39A (DPSP): Free legal aid + equal justice. Added by 42nd Amendment, 1976.
  • Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987: Enacted 1987; in force from 9 November 1995. Creates NALSA, SLSA, DLSA, Taluk committees.
  • Lok Adalat awards: Final and binding; no appeal; deemed decree of civil court.
  • Permanent Lok Adalat: Public utility services; up to Rs 1 crore; pre-litigation only; can decide on merits if conciliation fails.
  • Regular Lok Adalat: Civil + compoundable criminal; before or during litigation; cannot impose decision.
  • Family Courts Act, 1984: Districts with >1 million population; personal law matters (civil only, not 498A/DV); must promote conciliation; preference for women judges; lawyers not permitted as of right; appeal to HC. Recommended by Law Commission 59th Report.
  • Gram Nyayalaya Act, 2008: Mobile courts at intermediate panchayat level; Nyayadhikari = JMFC; civil + criminal per First/Second Schedules; summary trial (max 3 months sentence); plea bargaining allowed; court fees max Rs 100; relaxed evidence rules; conciliation first; disposal within 6 months; appeal within 1 month (criminal to Sessions, civil to District Court). Only ~208 functional against 5,000 target.
  • ADR formality: Negotiation (least) → Mediation → Conciliation → Arbitration (most).

9. Mains Points

  • Tribunalisation of justice: Discuss the tension between specialised adjudication and erosion of HC oversight. Use L. Chandra Kumar (two-tier review), Rojer Mathew (NTC direction), and Madras Bar Association (striking down Tribunal Reforms Act) to argue for structural independence.
  • Executive influence on tribunals: Appointment of members by government, financial dependence, short tenures, retired bureaucrats as members. NTC is the proposed solution. Discuss why an independent body is needed.
  • Access to justice: Art 39A + Legal Services Authorities Act + Lok Adalats + Gram Nyayalayas = constitutional commitment to justice for the poor. Discuss implementation gaps (GNs barely functional in most States).
  • ADR as supplement, not substitute: Courts are overburdened (5+ crore pending cases). ADR reduces burden but cannot replace courts for non-compoundable offences and constitutional questions. Discuss the balance.
  • Mediation Act, 2023: India's first standalone mediation law. Discuss how it formalises what was previously ad hoc, promotes institutional mediation, and creates an enforceable regime.
  • Arbitration reform: India's ranking in ease of enforcing contracts. Section 34 modifications, seat vs venue distinction, institutional vs ad hoc arbitration.

Lok Adalats: Permanent = public utility, pre-litigation only, can decide on merits, up to 1 cr. Regular = civil + compoundable criminal, before/during litigation, cannot impose. Both: free, no appeal, deemed civil court decree.
Family Courts: 1984 Act, >1M population districts, personal law (civil only), conciliation mandatory, counsellors assist, lawyers restricted, preference for women judges, appeal to HC, Law Commission 59th Report.
Gram Nyayalayas: 2008 Act (force 2 Oct 2009), intermediate panchayat HQ, mobile court, Nyayadhikari = JMFC, criminal (First Schedule, summary trial, max 3 months, plea bargaining) + civil (Second Schedule, day-to-day, 6 months disposal, fees max Rs 100), relaxed evidence, conciliation first, appeal within 1 month (criminal→Sessions, civil→District). ~208 vs 5,000 target.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019, General Studies Paper I

In India, the separation of judiciary from the executive is enjoined by

(a) the Preamble of the Constitution
(b) a Directive Principle of State Policy

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020, General Studies Paper I

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Constitution of India defines its basic structure in terms of federalism, secularism and democracy.
  2. The Constitution of India provides for 'judicial review' to safeguard the citizens' liberties and to preserve the ideals on which the Constitution is based.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only
(b) 2 only
(c) Both 1 and 2
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (b) 2 only
Explanation: The Constitution does not explicitly define its "basic structure" — that doctrine was judicially evolved by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973). Statement 1 is incorrect. The Constitution does provide for judicial review through Articles 13, 32, 226, etc. — Statement 2 is correct. This connects to L. Chandra Kumar (1997) where the SC held judicial review (Art 226) to be part of the basic structure.
