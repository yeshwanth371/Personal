1. Federalism

Meaning

Federalism is a constitutional arrangement in which powers are divided between two levels of government, each operating directly upon the people within its constitutionally assigned sphere.

Federalism = Vertical constitutional division of power between Union and States
[Correction] Federalism does not necessarily mean an “agreement.” An agreement among previously independent States describes the coming-together model (for example, the USA). India follows a holding-together model: powers were constitutionally distributed within an existing political territory.

Core Federal Features

FeatureIndian Position
Written and supreme ConstitutionBoth Union and States derive authority from the Constitution.
Two levels of governmentUnion and State governments have constitutionally assigned fields.
Division of legislative subjectsArticle 246 and the Seventh Schedule: Union List, State List and Concurrent List.
Partly rigid ConstitutionFederal provisions listed in the proviso to Article 368(2) require ratification by at least half of the State legislatures.
Independent judiciaryThe Supreme Court interprets the Constitution and decides Union–State disputes under Article 131.
Bicameral Union legislatureRajya Sabha represents the States, though representation is not equal for all States.
[Additional] A classic federation normally combines a dual polity, distribution of powers, constitutional supremacy, rigidity, an independent judiciary and bicameralism. India possesses these essentials but also has strong centralising provisions.

Why the Constitution Says “Union of States”

Article 1: “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.” The word “federal” does not appear in the constitutional description of India.

  • The Indian Union did not arise from an agreement among sovereign States.
  • No State has a constitutional right to secede.
  • The Union is indestructible, although Parliament may reorganise States.

Historical Setting: Provinces and Princely States

  • Before independence, British India consisted broadly of Governor’s Provinces/Provinces under direct British rule and princely States under indirect British paramountcy.
  • The Indian Independence Act, 1947 created the two independent Dominions of India and Pakistan from 15 August 1947 and ended British paramountcy over princely States.
  • Princely States acceded to India through Instruments of Accession and were subsequently integrated and reorganised.
  • Hyderabad initially resisted accession; it was integrated into India in September 1948.
[Correction] Britain did not simply transfer power “from the Dominions to the States.” The Indian Independence Act transferred sovereignty to the new Dominions and ended paramountcy; princely States then acceded and were integrated through distinct legal and political processes.

Unitary/Centralising Features

FeatureExplanation / Provision
Single citizenshipIndia has one citizenship; the USA recognises federal and State citizenship.
Single integrated judiciaryA unified hierarchy runs from subordinate courts to High Courts and the Supreme Court.
Residuary legislative power with UnionArticle 248 and Union List Entry 97 vest residuary power in Parliament.
Reorganisation of StatesUnder Articles 3–4, Parliament may alter a State’s area, boundaries or name by ordinary law. The affected State legislature must be invited to express its views, but its consent is not binding.
Governor appointed by PresidentArticles 155–156: appointed by the President and holds office during the President’s pleasure, subject to constitutional limitations.
All India ServicesArticle 312 permits creation of services common to Union and States after a Rajya Sabha resolution.
Emergency provisionsNational Emergency (Art. 352), President’s Rule (Art. 356) and Financial Emergency (Art. 360) strengthen the Union.

[Correction] States are not legally “destroyed” at will. They are commonly described as not indestructible because Parliament can reorganise them under Article 3. The President must refer the proposal to the affected State legislature for its views, but Parliament need not obtain its consent.

How to Describe Indian Federalism

India is federal in structure with a strong unitary or centralising bias.
  • K.C. Wheare: described India as “quasi-federal.”
  • Holding-together federation: a large polity constitutionally distributes power to accommodate diversity while maintaining a strong Centre.
  • Cooperative federalism: Union and States coordinate through institutions such as the GST Council, Finance Commission, Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils.
Exam Point: In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court treated federalism as part of the Constitution’s basic structure.
[Correction] The claim that Dr. Ambedkar called Indian federalism a “self-made model” or used the expression sui generis is [Verification Required]. His Constituent Assembly explanation clearly supports “Union” because the federation was not the result of an agreement and no State could secede.

3. Quorum and Types of Majority

Quorum

Quorum is the minimum number of members who must be present for a House to transact business validly.

  • Article 100(3): Until Parliament otherwise provides by law, quorum in either House is one-tenth of the total number of members of that House.
  • If there is no quorum, the presiding officer must adjourn the House or suspend the sitting until quorum is present: Article 100(4).
  • Parliament may change this by an ordinary law passed by simple majority.

Majorities at a Glance

MajorityFormulaMain Uses
Simple Majority [Not mentioned in the Constitution]More than 50% of members present and voting.Ordinary Bills, motions including no-confidence, and matters where the Constitution does not prescribe another majority. The Constitution simply says "decided by a majority of votes" (Art. 100).
Absolute Majority [Not mentioned in the Constitution]More than 50% of the total membership of the House.A political/academic term, not used in the constitutional text. The Constitution uses phrases like "a majority of the total membership" instead. Commonly used in political commentary to describe the 272-seat threshold in Lok Sabha.
Effective Majority [Not mentioned in the Constitution]More than 50% of the effective strength (total membership minus vacancies).Removal of specified presiding officers: Arts. 67(b), 90(c), 94(c), 179(c). The Constitution uses "a majority of all the then members."
Special Majority — Type 1 [Academic label]Not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.Rajya Sabha resolutions under Articles 249 and 312.
Special Majority — Type 2 [Academic label]Majority of the total membership of the House and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.Article 368 amendments; approval of National Emergency; removal procedures for SC/HC judges and certain constitutional authorities; State Assembly resolution under Article 169.
Special Majority — Type 3 [Academic label]Not less than two-thirds of the total membership of the House.Impeachment of the President under Article 61.
Counting rule: “Present and voting” excludes abstentions. “Total membership” includes vacant seats. “All the then members” means effective strength, excluding vacancies.

Special Majority — Type 1

  • Article 249: Rajya Sabha may authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest.
  • Article 312: Rajya Sabha may authorise Parliament to create one or more All India Services in the national interest.

Special Majority — Type 2

  • Constitutional amendments under Article 368, in each House separately.
  • Removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges: Articles 124(4) and 217.
  • Removal of the CAG: Article 148(1), in like manner and on like grounds as a Supreme Court judge.
  • Removal of the CEC: Article 324(5), in like manner and on like grounds as a Supreme Court judge.
  • Removal of a State Election Commissioner: Article 243K(2), in like manner and on like grounds as a High Court judge.
  • Approval/continuance of a National Emergency: Article 352(6).
  • State Legislative Assembly resolution for creation or abolition of a Legislative Council: Article 169.
[Correction] “More than 50% of total strength” should be stated in constitutional language as a majority of the total membership. For Article 169, the resolution is passed only by the Legislative Assembly, not by both Houses of the State legislature.

Special Majority — Type 3

For impeachment of the President, each House must pass the relevant resolution by not less than two-thirds of the total membership of that House.

[Correction] Presidential impeachment is under Article 61, not Article 65. Article 65 concerns the Vice-President acting as President or discharging presidential functions.

Effective Majority

Used for removal of:

  • Vice-President as ex officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha: Article 67(b) (Rajya Sabha initiates by effective majority; Lok Sabha agrees).
  • Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha: Article 90(c).
  • Speaker and Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha: Article 94(c).
  • Speaker and Deputy Speaker of a State Legislative Assembly: Article 179(c).
[Correction] Effective majority is not “more than 50% of members present.” It is more than 50% of all the then members, that is, total membership minus vacancies.

Private Member's Bill [Not mentioned in the Constitution]Any MP/MLA who is not a MinisterParliamentary classification based on the member's status; not a constitutional term.

Classification by Subject Matter

BillKey Provision / Feature
Ordinary BillGeneral legislation; may normally originate in either House.
Money BillArticle 110; contains only matters listed there. Introduced only in Lok Sabha on President’s recommendation.
Financial Bill — Category IArticle 117(1); contains a Money Bill matter plus other matters. Introduced only in Lok Sabha on President’s recommendation.
Financial Bill — Category IIArticle 117(3); involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India but contains no Article 110 matter. May originate in either House, but cannot be passed without President’s recommendation for consideration.
Constitution Amendment BillArticle 368; may originate in either House and has no joint sitting.
[Correction] “Monel Bill” is Money Bill. Financial Bills are conventionally classified as Category I under Article 117(1) and Category II under Article 117(3).

Ordinary Bill: Simplified Flow

Introduction / First Reading
↓
Second Reading — general discussion → committee stage, if referred → clause-by-clause consideration
↓
Third Reading — final consideration and voting
↓
Second House — broadly the same three readings
↓
President’s Assent under Article 111
↓
Act → publication in Official Gazette → commencement as specified
[Correction] Discussion and voting do not constitute the First Reading. First Reading concerns leave to introduce/introduction. Detailed consideration occurs mainly at the Second Reading, followed by final passage at the Third Reading.
[Additional] If the Houses deadlock over an ordinary or Financial Bill, the President may summon a joint sitting under Article 108. There is no joint sitting for a Money Bill or Constitution Amendment Bill.

Assent and Commencement

  • A Bill becomes an Act upon receiving assent.
  • Publication and commencement are distinct. An Act may commence immediately, on a specified date, or on a date appointed by notification.
  • Example: The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 received assent on 25 December 2023 and came into force on 1 July 2024, except section 106(2), whose commencement was excluded from the initial notification.

5. Constitutional Amendment

Article and Location

Article 368 in Part XX deals with Parliament’s constituent power and the procedure for amendment of the Constitution.

Procedure under Article 368

Constitution Amendment Bill introduced in either House (by a Minister or private member)
↓
Each House passes it separately by Special Majority — Type 2
↓
If proviso to Article 368(2) applies: ratification by legislatures of not less than one-half of the States
↓
Presented to the President
↓
President shall give assent
↓
Constitution stands amended
  • No joint sitting is available if the Houses disagree.
  • State legislatures cannot initiate an Article 368 amendment.
  • The motion for leave to introduce is decided by simple majority; the effective clauses and final passage require the prescribed special majority.
  • The 24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971 made presidential assent mandatory.

Provisions Requiring State Ratification

CategoryProvisions
Election of PresidentArticles 54 and 55
Extent of executive powerArticles 73 and 162
Union and State judiciaryArticle 241; Chapter IV of Part V (Supreme Court); Chapter V of Part VI (High Courts)
GST CouncilArticle 279A
Legislative relationsChapter I of Part XI
Distribution of subjectsAny of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule
Federal representationRepresentation of States in Parliament
Amendment powerArticle 368 itself
[Correction] Ratification is by the legislatures of not less than one-half of the States, generally by simple majority. In a bicameral State, both Houses participate because “Legislature” includes both Houses. Union Territories with legislatures are not States for this proviso.
[Additional] Article 368 prescribes no time limit for State ratification and provides no mechanism for a State to initiate the amendment. Parliament may amend any provision, but cannot damage the Constitution’s basic structure: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).

Three Routes of Constitutional Change

RouteMajorityExamples
Outside Article 368Simple majority, like ordinary lawFormation/reorganisation of States and consequential First/Fourth Schedule changes (Arts. 2–4); creation/abolition of State Legislative Councils (Art. 169); amendments under paragraph 7 of Fifth Schedule and paragraph 21 of Sixth Schedule.
Article 368Special Majority — Type 2Most constitutional provisions.
Article 368 + State ratificationSpecial Majority — Type 2 in Parliament + ratification by at least half the StatesSpecified federal provisions listed above.
[Correction] It is too broad to say that the entire Second, Fifth and Sixth Schedules can all be amended by an ordinary law in the same way. The Constitution contains specific enabling provisions; the clearest examples are paragraph 7 of the Fifth Schedule and paragraph 21 of the Sixth Schedule.
Conclusion: The Indian Constitution combines rigidity and flexibility. Not every textual constitutional change is made under Article 368; only amendments following Article 368 are formally numbered Constitution Amendment Acts.

6. Making of the Constitution

Formation and Composition of the Constituent Assembly

  • Formed under the Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946.
  • Members representing provinces were indirectly elected by members of the Provincial Legislative Assemblies through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • Representatives of princely States were to be nominated.
  • The elections to Provincial Assemblies had themselves been held on a restricted franchise; the Constituent Assembly was therefore not elected by universal adult franchise.
SourceOriginal Seats
British Indian Provinces292
Chief Commissioners’ Provinces4
Princely States93
Total389

After Partition, the membership of the Indian Constituent Assembly was reduced to 299.

[Correction] Elections to the Constituent Assembly were held in July–August 1946, not simply “August 1946.” The often-repeated claim that exactly “10–12%” of people voted is [Verification Required]; the reliable point is that the electorate was restricted and the Assembly was indirectly elected.

Criticism and Response

  • Criticism: It was not directly elected by universal adult franchise and was not fully representative in the modern democratic sense.
  • Response: It included major political, social, regional and minority voices and deliberated extensively through committees and open debates.
  • After 15 August 1947, it became a sovereign body and also functioned as the Dominion legislature in a separate capacity.

7. Preamble

Meaning and Origin

The Preamble is the introductory statement or preface to the Constitution. It summarises the Constitution’s source, the nature of the State, its objectives and the date of adoption.

[Correction] “Preamble” does not mean “pre + amble/movement through the Constitution.” It derives from a term meaning an introductory statement or preface.
  • It was based substantially on the Objectives Resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • It was finally adopted after the substantive provisions had been settled, on 26 November 1949.
  • It is part of the Constitution: Kesavananda Bharati (1973), overruling the contrary view in the Berubari Union opinion (1960).
  • It is not independently enforceable but helps courts interpret ambiguous constitutional provisions.

Functions of the Preamble

FunctionWords / Meaning
Source of authority“We, the People of India” — constitutional authority ultimately rests with the people.
Nature of the StateSovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic.
ObjectivesJustice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity.
Date of adoption26 November 1949.

Nature of the Indian State

TermMeaning
SovereignIndia has supreme authority over internal and external affairs. Voluntary membership of international organisations or acceptance of treaty obligations does not by itself destroy sovereignty.
SocialistCommitment to democratic socialism, social and economic justice and reduction of inequality; added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.
SecularThe State has no official religion and treats religions with principled equality; added expressly by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, though secular features existed from commencement.
DemocraticPolitical authority derives from the people through universal adult suffrage, representative institutions, periodic elections and accountable government.
RepublicThe head of State is elected directly or indirectly and is not a hereditary monarch.
[Additional] The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 added “Socialist,” “Secular” and “integrity” to the Preamble. The Preamble can be amended under Article 368, but its basic features cannot be destroyed.

Preamble Objectives

  • Justice: social, economic and political.
  • Liberty: of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
  • Equality: of status and opportunity.
  • Fraternity: assuring dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.

8. Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Note: Verify exact wording from official UPSC question papers. Years and answers are from widely documented compilations.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013

Consider the following statements:

  1. An amendment to the Constitution of India can be initiated by an introduction of a Bill in the Lok Sabha only.
  2. If such an amendment seeks to make changes in any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule, the amendment also requires to be ratified by the legislatures of half of the States.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only   (b) 2 only   (c) Both 1 and 2   (d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (b)
Explanation: Statement 1 is wrong — a Constitution Amendment Bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament. Statement 2 is correct — changes to the Seventh Schedule lists require ratification by at least half the State legislatures under the proviso to Article 368(2).

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Parliament of India can place a particular law in the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution of India.
  2. The validity of a law placed in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court and no judgement can be made on it.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only   (b) 2 only   (c) Both 1 and 2   (d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (a)
Explanation: Statement 1 is correct — Parliament can add laws to the Ninth Schedule by constitutional amendment. Statement 2 is wrong — after I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed if they violate the basic structure of the Constitution.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Constitution of India does not define the territories of the States.
  2. The Rajya Sabha has no role in the formation of a new State within the Union of India.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only   (b) 2 only   (c) Both 1 and 2   (d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (d)
Explanation: Statement 1 is wrong — territories of each State are defined in the First Schedule. Statement 2 is wrong — a Bill under Article 3 is an ordinary Bill and must pass both Houses (including Rajya Sabha), though the affected State's consent is not required.

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2015

There is a Parliamentary System of Government in India because:

(a) The Lok Sabha is elected directly by the people
(b) The Parliament can amend the Constitution
(c) The Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved
(d) The Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha

Answer: (d)
Explanation: The defining feature of a parliamentary system is collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the lower House (Article 75(3)). This distinguishes it from a presidential system.

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013

The Parliament can make any law for whole or any part of India for implementing international treaties:

(a) with the consent of all the States
(b) with the consent of the majority of States
(c) with the consent of the States concerned
(d) without the consent of any State

Answer: (d)
Explanation: Under Article 253, Parliament has power to legislate on any matter (including State List subjects) for implementing treaties, agreements or conventions, without requiring consent of any State. This is a strong centralising/unitary feature.

9. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Article 1 calls India a Union of States; the Constitution does not use “federal” to describe India.
  • Residuary legislative power belongs to Parliament: Article 248 and Union List Entry 97.
  • Quorum in either House is one-tenth of total membership unless Parliament provides otherwise: Article 100(3).
  • Articles 249 and 312 require two-thirds of members present and voting in Rajya Sabha.
  • Article 368 majority = total membership majority + two-thirds present and voting.
  • Presidential impeachment requires two-thirds of total membership of each House: Article 61.
  • A Money Bill is defined by Article 110 and can originate only in Lok Sabha.
  • No joint sitting for Money Bills or Constitution Amendment Bills.
  • State ratification under Article 368 means at least half of the States, not Union Territories.
  • Constituent Assembly: 389 originally; 299 after Partition.
  • Constitution adopted on 26 November 1949; fully commenced on 26 January 1950.
  • Originally: 22 Parts, 395 Articles and 8 Schedules. Currently: commonly counted as 25 Parts and 12 Schedules.
  • 42nd Amendment added “Socialist,” “Secular” and “integrity” to the Preamble.

10. Mains Points

  • Indian federalism balances regional autonomy with national unity; centralising provisions do not erase its federal character.
  • India’s holding-together model suits deep linguistic, cultural and regional diversity but requires cooperative institutions and fiscal fairness.
  • The Constitution’s amendment design combines stability with adaptability through three different routes.
  • The Constituent Assembly lacked universal adult franchise but gained legitimacy through deliberation, broad participation and later adoption of universal suffrage.
  • The Preamble is a compact statement of constitutional philosophy and an interpretive guide, while the basic structure doctrine protects its essential commitments.

11. Common Traps / Confusions

TrapCorrect Position
India is expressly called a federation.No. Article 1 says “Union of States.” Federalism is inferred from the constitutional scheme.
State consent is required under Article 3.No. Its views must be sought; consent is not binding.
Effective majority means present and voting.No. It means more than half of effective strength: total membership minus vacancies.
President’s impeachment is under Article 65.No. Article 61.
Article 169 resolution passes both State Houses.No. The Legislative Assembly alone passes the special-majority resolution.
All amendments require Article 368.No. Some constitutional changes are expressly made by ordinary law outside Article 368.
Every amendment requires State ratification.No. Only federal provisions listed in the proviso to Article 368(2).
A Bill becomes effective immediately on assent.Not always. Assent makes it an Act; commencement depends on its commencement clause/notification.
Preamble is enforceable by itself.No. It is part of the Constitution and aids interpretation, but is not an independent source of enforceable power or rights.

12. 30-Second Revision

Federalism: Article 1 Union of States; Seventh Schedule division; federal structure + strong Centre; basic structure.
