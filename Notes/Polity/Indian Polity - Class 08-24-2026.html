1. Indian Legal System

A legal system is the framework of laws, institutions and procedures through which a State creates, interprets and enforces law and resolves disputes. Courts are central to this framework, but the legal system also includes legislatures, the executive, police, tribunals, the legal profession and correctional institutions.

Common-Law Heritage [Not mentioned in the Constitution]

India inherited major features of the English common-law tradition during colonial rule. These include judicial precedent, adversarial procedure, reasoned judgments and an organised legal profession. Similar traditions operate in the United Kingdom, the United States and many Commonwealth jurisdictions, although each country has developed its own constitutional and statutory law.

[Correction] India did not inherit its legal system from the modern "UK" as a single unchanged package. It inherited important English common-law and colonial institutional features, which now operate under the Constitution and Indian legislation. India is therefore a common-law jurisdiction with an extensively codified legal system, not a system governed only by uncodified English common law.
[Additional] Judicial precedent is often described through the doctrine of stare decisis: like cases should ordinarily be decided alike. In India, its binding force depends on the court, bench strength, issue decided and the ratio of the judgment.

2. Questions of Fact and Questions of Law

A dispute may require a court to decide questions of fact, questions of law, or mixed questions of fact and law.

TypeMeaningExample
Question of factWhat actually happened, determined from admissible evidence and applicable standards of proof.Whether the accused was present at the scene.
Question of lawThe meaning, scope or legal effect of a constitutional, statutory or other legal rule.Whether a statutory expression includes a particular category of conduct.
Mixed questionApplication of a legal standard to facts found in the case.Whether established conduct amounts to legal negligence.
[Correction] A question of law is not limited to interpreting the words of a statute. It may also concern constitutional interpretation, jurisdiction, precedent, procedural rules or the legal consequence of established facts.

3. Courts of Record

CourtConstitutional ProvisionExpress Consequence
Supreme CourtArticle 129Court of record with all powers of such a court, including power to punish for contempt of itself.
High CourtArticle 215Court of record with all powers of such a court, including power to punish for contempt of itself.

The records and judicial acts of a court of record carry authoritative evidentiary value and are preserved as permanent judicial records according to law and record-retention rules. Its contempt power protects the administration of justice.

[Correction] "Court of record" does not mean that every document or judgment must literally never be destroyed. Preservation is governed by applicable law and court rules. Nor does the designation alone make every sentence in every judgment a binding precedent.

Binding Precedent

  • Article 141: the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India.
  • A High Court's declaration of law binds courts and tribunals subordinate to it within its territorial jurisdiction.
  • The binding element is generally the ratio decidendi, the legal principle necessary for deciding the case. An incidental observation (obiter dictum) does not ordinarily have the same binding force.
  • A smaller bench must follow a larger bench. A coordinate bench should follow an earlier coordinate-bench decision or refer the issue to a larger bench when reconsideration is necessary.

How a Precedent May Cease to Govern

MethodCorrect Position
Larger benchA larger bench of the same court may overrule the legal rule laid down by a smaller bench.
Higher courtThe Supreme Court may overrule a High Court precedent. A High Court cannot overrule the Supreme Court.
Legislative changeA competent legislature may change the legal basis prospectively or cure the defect identified by a court, subject to constitutional limits.
[Correction] A legislature cannot simply declare a judicial decision wrong or directly overrule it. It may validly amend the underlying law and remove the basis of a statutory judgment, but the amendment remains open to constitutional review. A constitutional interpretation of the Supreme Court can be altered only through a valid constitutional amendment that also respects the basic structure.
Exam Point: Court of record (Articles 129 and 215) and binding force of Supreme Court law (Article 141) are related but distinct ideas.

4. Adversarial System of Justice

Meaning [Not mentioned in the Constitution]

In an adversarial system, opposing parties present their pleadings, arguments and evidence before an impartial judge. Each side may test the other's case through procedures such as cross-examination. The judge applies the law, controls the proceedings and gives a reasoned decision.

[Correction] The judge is a neutral umpire, not a neutral "empire". More importantly, the judge is not necessarily passive. Courts may question witnesses, control evidence, prevent abuse of process and seek clarification. Justice and truth-finding are not opposites; procedural fairness gives both parties an equal opportunity while the court determines facts according to law.
Adversarial ModelInquisitorial Model
Parties principally collect, present and contest evidence; the judge acts as an impartial adjudicator.The judge or judicial authority takes a more active role in investigating and assembling the evidentiary record.
Associated historically with common-law systems.Associated historically with many civil-law systems.
India is primarily adversarial, with statutory powers that permit active judicial intervention where justice requires.India does not follow a purely inquisitorial system.
[Additional] Article 39A directs the State to secure equal justice and provide free legal aid so that economic or other disabilities do not deny opportunities for securing justice.

5. Supreme Court: Constitutional Framework

Part V, Chapter IV (Articles 124-147) deals with the Union Judiciary.

Composition and Strength

  • Article 124(1) establishes a Supreme Court consisting of the Chief Justice of India (CJI) and, until Parliament provides a larger number by law, not more than seven other judges.
  • Parliament has increased the number by ordinary legislation. Under the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956, as amended in 2019, the sanctioned strength is 33 judges other than the CJI, or 34 in total.
  • The constitutional phrase "not more than seven" is therefore the original default, not the present sanctioned strength.
[Correction] The current sanctioned composition is CJI + 33 other judges, not "CJI + 37." The number of judges actually in office may be lower when vacancies exist.

CJI and Puisne Judges

The CJI is the head of the Indian judiciary and, on the administrative side, is conventionally described as the Master of the Roster, allocating cases and constituting benches. The other Supreme Court judges are called puisne judges (pronounced "puny"), meaning judges other than the Chief Justice.

[Correction] Corrected "Puine" to puisne. Master of the Roster is a judicially recognised administrative position, not an expression used in Article 124.

Qualifications: Article 124(3)

A person must be a citizen of India and satisfy any one of the following:

  1. Has been a judge of one or more High Courts in succession for at least five years; or
  2. Has been an advocate of one or more High Courts in succession for at least ten years; or
  3. Is, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.
  • No minimum age is prescribed.
  • A Supreme Court judge holds office until attaining 65 years, unless the judge resigns or is removed earlier according to the Constitution.
  • Under Article 124(2A), a question about a Supreme Court judge's age is decided by the authority and in the manner provided by a law made by Parliament.
Lawyer versus advocate: In ordinary usage, "lawyer" is a broad term for a person trained in law. An advocate is a person enrolled under the Advocates Act, 1961 and entitled to practise subject to that Act and applicable rules. The relevant enrolment is ordinarily with a State Bar Council, not a licence issued directly by the Bar Council of India.
[Correction] A law degree alone does not necessarily make a person a practising advocate, and the Bar Council of India does not ordinarily issue the initial enrolment described in the rough note.
[Additional] The Constitution permits appointment of a distinguished jurist, but no Supreme Court judge has yet been appointed through this qualification.

6. Appointment of Supreme Court Judges

Constitutional Text: Article 124(2)

Every Supreme Court judge is appointed by the President. The President consults such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President considers necessary. For appointment of a judge other than the CJI, consultation with the CJI is mandatory.

Appointment of the CJI

Under the current Memorandum of Procedure (MoP), appointment to the office of CJI should be of the senior-most Supreme Court judge considered fit to hold the office. The outgoing CJI initiates the recommendation; the Union Minister of Law and Justice places it before the Prime Minister, who advises the President.

  • 1973: Justice A.N. Ray was appointed CJI, superseding three senior judges.
  • 1977: Justice M.H. Beg was appointed CJI, superseding Justice H.R. Khanna.
[Correction] Article 124 does not separately prescribe appointment of the CJI by mandatory consultation with Supreme Court and High Court judges. The seniority rule is a settled constitutional convention reflected in the MoP, strengthened after the supersessions of 1973 and 1977.

Evolution of the Collegium [Not mentioned in the Constitution]

CasePosition
First Judges Case
S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)		Executive primacy: "consultation" did not mean concurrence, and the CJI's opinion was not binding.
Second Judges Case
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)		Overruled the First Judges Case and established judicial primacy. The CJI's opinion had to be formed collectively with the two senior-most Supreme Court judges.
Third Judges Case
In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998		For Supreme Court appointments, consultation expanded to a collegium of the CJI and four senior-most Supreme Court judges.
Fourth Judges Case / NJAC Case
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)		By a 4:1 majority, struck down the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act; the collegium system revived.
[Correction] The Second Judges Case was decided in 1993, not 1983. The Third Judges Case was an advisory opinion under Article 143, delivered in 1998.

Under the law declared in the Judges Cases, if the collegium unanimously reiterates a recommendation after considering the objections, the appointment should be made. [Correction] The rough flow incorrectly made the Ministry of Home Affairs a universal formal stage. Supreme Court appointment proposals are processed through the Department of Justice in the Ministry of Law and Justice, then the Law Minister and Prime Minister before presidential appointment. Intelligence and other inputs may be sought where relevant, especially for candidates from the Bar.
[Additional] The collegium is a judge-made institutional mechanism; the detailed MoP is an executive-judiciary procedure. Neither the word collegium nor its present composition appears in the Constitution.

Constituent Assembly and Comparative Models

  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar opposed making the CJI's concurrence absolute, warning that the CJI could share ordinary human limitations.
  • United States: the President nominates federal Supreme Court judges and the Senate gives advice and consent.
  • United Kingdom: the modern UK Supreme Court appointments process uses an independent selection commission and a statutory process; describing it simply as appointment by the Crown on the Prime Minister's advice is incomplete.

[Additional] Both India and the United States have constitutional supremacy and judicial review. The central contrast is institutional: the US combines a dual judiciary, political confirmation and life tenure; India combines an integrated judiciary, collegium primacy and fixed retirement ages.
Mains Balance: Reform should combine independence with transparent criteria, recorded reasons, diversity, a permanent secretariat and predictable timelines. Neither unchecked executive control nor unaccountable judicial exclusivity is satisfactory.

8. NJAC and the 99th Constitutional Amendment

The Constitution (Ninety-ninth Amendment) Act, 2014 and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014 sought to replace the collegium with the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC).

Constitutional Changes

  • Inserted Articles 124A, 124B and 124C.
  • Amended Articles 124, 127, 128, 217, 222, 224A and 231.
[Correction] The amendment was not confined to Articles 124, 217 and 222. It also amended Articles 127, 128, 224A and 231 and inserted Articles 124A-124C.

Article 124A: Proposed Composition

  1. CJI as ex officio Chairperson;
  2. Two other senior Supreme Court judges;
  3. Union Minister in charge of Law and Justice; and
  4. Two eminent persons nominated by a committee consisting of the Prime Minister, CJI and Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, or leader of the single largest opposition party where no LoP is recognised.

One eminent person was to be nominated from among the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, minorities or women; an eminent person's term was three years without renomination.

Articles 124B and 124C

ArticleSubject
124BDuties of the NJAC: recommend appointments of the CJI, Supreme Court judges and High Court Chief Justices and judges; recommend transfers of High Court Chief Justices and judges; and ensure that recommended persons are capable and of integrity.
124CPower of Parliament to regulate the appointment procedure and empower the NJAC to make regulations.

Fourth Judges Case, 2015

In Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015), a Constitution Bench, by a 4:1 majority, invalidated the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act. The majority held that the arrangement damaged judicial independence, a basic feature of the Constitution, because it did not preserve the required judicial primacy and permitted two members to block a recommendation.

[Correction] The NJAC decision did not hold that any non-judicial participation is automatically unconstitutional. The majority's objection concerned the commission's particular composition, veto structure and resulting impairment of judicial independence. Justice J. Chelameswar dissented.

9. Removal of Supreme Court Judges

Article 124(4)

The President may remove a Supreme Court judge only after each House of Parliament presents an address in the same session, supported separately in each House by:

  • a majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

The only grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity. This combination is commonly called a special majority [Not mentioned in the Constitution as "Special Majority Type 2"].

[Correction] The Constitution uses the expression removal, not impeachment, for judges. "Impeachment" is common shorthand, while the formal process is a parliamentary address followed by a presidential order.

[Additional] The removal standard protects judicial independence by preventing dismissal at executive pleasure. No Supreme Court judge has been removed through the completed constitutional process. The 1993 Lok Sabha motion concerning Justice V. Ramaswami failed to obtain the required majority.
Exam Point: The President does not remove a judge independently. The President acts only after both Houses pass the address with the constitutionally required majority in the same session.

10. Key Facts for Prelims

  • India has a common-law heritage but an extensively codified constitutional and statutory legal system.
  • Supreme Court and High Courts are courts of record under Articles 129 and 215.
  • Article 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India.
  • India follows a primarily adversarial system with powers for active judicial intervention.
  • Part V, Chapter IV, Articles 124-147 deals with the Union Judiciary.
  • Sanctioned Supreme Court strength: CJI + 33 other judges = 34 total.
  • Qualifications: citizen + five years as an HC judge, ten years as an HC advocate, or distinguished jurist.
  • No minimum age is prescribed; retirement age is 65.
  • First Judges Case: 1981; Second: 1993; Third: 1998; NJAC / Fourth: 2015.
  • SC collegium for appointments: CJI + four senior-most SC judges.
  • The words "collegium," "Master of the Roster" and "Special Majority Type 2" do not occur in the Constitution.
  • 99th Amendment inserted Articles 124A-124C; it and the NJAC Act were struck down in 2015.
  • Removal requires proved misbehaviour or incapacity and the special majority in each House in the same session.
  • UK: parliamentary sovereignty and three legal jurisdictions; India: constitutional supremacy and an integrated judiciary.
  • US: dual judiciary, presidential nomination with Senate confirmation and life tenure; India: integrated judiciary, collegium primacy and fixed retirement ages.

11. Mains Points

  • Precedent promotes certainty and equality, while larger-bench reconsideration allows law to evolve.
  • Adversarial fairness requires legal aid, competent representation, disclosure, reasoned orders and an impartial but engaged judge.
  • Judicial appointments must reconcile independence, accountability, competence, diversity and timely vacancy filling.
  • The collegium insulates appointments from direct political control but needs clearer criteria, institutional support and greater procedural transparency.
  • The NJAC judgment protected judicial independence but left unresolved the democratic-accountability and opacity criticisms of the collegium.
  • A rigorous removal threshold protects decisional independence; complementary ethical and disciplinary mechanisms are needed for misconduct below the removal threshold.
  • Comparative models show that judicial independence can be institutionally protected through different combinations of selection, tenure, legislative scrutiny and constitutional review.

13. 30-Second Revision

Legal system: Indian constitutional and codified law + English common-law heritage.
Disputes: questions of fact, law, or mixed questions.
Record: SC Article 129; HC Article 215; binding SC law Article 141.
Justice model: primarily adversarial, but judges are not merely passive.
SC: Articles 124-147; 34 sanctioned judges; retirement at 65.
Collegium: 1981 executive primacy → 1993 judicial primacy → 1998 CJI + four → 2015 NJAC struck down.
Compare: UK = parliamentary sovereignty; US = dual courts + Senate confirmation + life tenure; India = constitutional supremacy + integrated courts + collegium + retirement ages.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021, General Studies Paper I

With reference to the Indian judiciary, consider the following statements:

  1. Any retired judge of the Supreme Court of India can be called back to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge by the Chief Justice of India with the prior permission of the President of India.
  2. A High Court in India has the power to review its own judgement as the Supreme Court does.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only
(b) 2 only
(c) Both 1 and 2
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (c) Both 1 and 2
Explanation: Article 128 permits the CJI, with the previous consent of the President, to request a qualified former judge to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge; the former judge's consent is also required. High Courts, as courts of record under Article 215, possess an inherent power of review, subject to law.
