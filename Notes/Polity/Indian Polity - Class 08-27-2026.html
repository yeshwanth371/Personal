1. Salary and Emoluments of High Court Judges (Article 221)

  • Judges of a High Court are entitled to such salaries as may be determined by Parliament by law.
  • The salaries, pensions and other conditions of service of judges shall not be varied to their disadvantage after appointment.
  • The salaries and allowances of HC judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State (Article 202(3)(d)).
  • The pensions of HC judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (Article 112(3)(d)(iii)).
[Correction] The rough notes stated that pensions are charged on CFI "(Written in Budget)." The parenthetical is misleading. Charged expenditure means the amount is not submitted to the vote of the legislature — it is automatically payable from the Consolidated Fund. "Written in Budget" could suggest the amount is voted upon, which is incorrect. Pensions of HC judges are non-votable charged expenditure on the CFI.
Exam Point: HC judges' salaries are charged on the State's Consolidated Fund, but their pensions are charged on the Central Consolidated Fund. This split is frequently tested. For SC judges, both salaries and pensions are charged on CFI.
[Additional] Financial Emergency exception: Under Article 360(4)(b), the President may direct reduction of salaries and allowances of all persons serving in connection with the affairs of the Union or a State, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. This is the only constitutional exception to the protection against disadvantageous variation.

2. Transfer of High Court Judges (Article 222)

Constitutional Provision

  • Article 222(1): The President may, after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, transfer a judge from one High Court to any other High Court.
  • Article 222(2): When a judge is so transferred, the judge shall be entitled to receive, in addition to salary, such compensatory allowance as may be determined by Parliament by law.

Key Case Law

Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977)

  • The Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) held that the consent of the HC judge is not required before transfer under Article 222.
  • However, it also held that transfer must be done only in the interest of administration of justice and not as a punishment.
[Correction] The rough notes referred to this as the "Sakal Seth Case 1977." The correct case name is Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977) 4 SCC 193.

Third Judges Case — In re Presidential Reference (1998)

  • The Supreme Court held that the consultation of the CJI under Article 222 shall be binding (i.e., equivalent to concurrence) upon the President.
  • For transfer of HC judges, the recommendation should be made by a collegium of the CJI and 4 senior-most judges of the Supreme Court.
  • The CJI must also consult the Chief Justices of the two High Courts concerned (the HC from which the judge is transferred and the HC to which the judge is being transferred).
[Additional] Transfer of HC judges is distinct from transfer of SC judges (there is no provision to transfer SC judges). The collegium for transfer of HC judges (CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges) is the same as for appointment of SC judges. For appointment of HC judges, the SC-level collegium is CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges.

4. Powers of the High Court (Article 225)

Article 225 provides that every High Court shall continue to have the same jurisdiction, powers and authority as it had immediately before the commencement of the Constitution, subject to:

  1. The provisions of the Constitution itself; and
  2. Any law made by the appropriate Legislature.

Thus, the Constitution does not exhaustively enumerate all HC powers. Many HC powers are derived through ordinary laws, letters patent, and pre-Constitution statutes.

[Additional] This is why different High Courts may have different original civil jurisdiction thresholds — for example, the Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras High Courts have broader original civil (and limited criminal) jurisdiction inherited from their pre-independence charters and letters patent.

5.2 Appellate Jurisdiction

Civil Appeals

  • First appeal: An appeal lies to the HC against decrees of subordinate courts involving questions of law and/or fact (Section 96, CPC 1908).
  • Second appeal: A second appeal lies to the HC only on grounds involving a substantial question of law (Section 100, CPC 1908). Questions of fact alone cannot be re-agitated in second appeal.

Criminal Appeals

  • First appeal: An appeal lies to the HC against convictions by a Sessions Court (including convictions for any sentence). Additionally, appeals from Magistrates' courts lie to the HC in cases involving imprisonment exceeding a specified threshold or in other situations prescribed by law.
  • Second appeal: Criminal second appeals to the HC are available only in cases provided by law.
  • Confirmation of death sentence: A sentence of death passed by a Sessions Court shall not be executed unless confirmed by the High Court (Section 366, CrPC 1973 / Section 416, BNSS 2023).
[Correction] The rough notes stated that criminal first appeals to the HC arise "in cases involving sentence of more than 7 yrs of imprisonment." This oversimplifies the position. An appeal from a Sessions Court conviction lies to the HC regardless of sentence duration. The "7 years" threshold is more relevant to distinguishing whether an appeal from a Magistrate's court lies to the Sessions Court or directly to the HC. The CrPC (and now BNSS) prescribes detailed provisions; the rough notes' statement is not a general rule.

5.3 Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226)

  • Every High Court may issue writs including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, and quo warranto.
  • Article 226 is wider than Article 32: the HC can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights as well as for "any other purpose".
  • Article 32 empowers the SC to issue writs only for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

[Additional] Under Article 226(2), the HC can issue writs to any person or authority even outside its territorial jurisdiction if the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises within its territory.

5.4 Court of Record (Article 215)

  • Every High Court is a court of record and has all the powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself.
  • Judgments, proceedings and acts of the HC are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony, and have evidentiary value.

6. Superintendence over Subordinate Courts (Articles 227 and 228)

Article 227 — Power of Superintendence

Every High Court has superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction (except military courts and tribunals constituted under any law relating to the Armed Forces).

Administrative Powers under Article 227

  1. Make rules with respect to the functioning of subordinate courts, subject to any law in force and with the approval of the Governor.
  2. Prescribe books, forms, and registers to be maintained by subordinate courts.
  3. May call for returns (information) from subordinate courts.
  4. Settle accounts with respect to payments made to officers of the court, advocates, clerks, etc.

Judicial Powers under Article 227

The Supreme Court has held that the superintendence under Article 227 is both administrative and judicial in nature. In exercise of this judicial superintendence, the HC may:

  1. Withdraw a case pending in a subordinate court to itself and decide it.
  2. Transfer a case from one subordinate court to another.
  3. Revise the orders or judgments of subordinate courts.
Grounds for exercising revisional/supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227:
  1. Excess of jurisdiction or failure to exercise jurisdiction
  2. Error of law apparent on the face of the record
  3. Violation of higher courts' order or judgment
  4. Violation of principles of natural justice
Exam Point: Article 227 (superintendence) is distinct from Article 226 (writ jurisdiction). Even where writs are not available, the HC may exercise supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227. After the 42nd Amendment (1976), Article 227 was restricted to administrative superintendence only. However, this restriction was struck down as part of the restoration through judicial interpretation, and judicial superintendence under Article 227 continues to exist.

Article 228 — Transfer of Constitutional Cases

  • If the HC is satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, the HC shall:
  • Withdraw the case to itself; and
  • Either decide the case itself, or determine the constitutional question and return the case to the subordinate court with its interpretation.

[Correction] The rough notes listed the criminal hierarchy as "Sessions → CDM → JM." The correct abbreviation is CJM (Chief Judicial Magistrate), not CDM. "CDM" could be confused with Chief District Magistrate, which is an executive (not judicial) post. The judicial criminal hierarchy below the Sessions Court is: Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM) → Judicial Magistrate (JM).

District Judge (Article 233 and Article 236)

Definition

Article 236 defines "district judge" broadly to include the Chief Judge of a Small Cause Court, Sessions Judge, Additional Sessions Judge, Additional District Judge, Joint Civil Judge, Assistant Sessions Judge, and any other officer referred to by any other name exercising the jurisdiction of a district judge.

Appointment of District Judges (Article 233)

Appointment, posting, and promotion of district judges is done by the Governor in consultation with the High Court.

Two modes of appointment:

  1. Promotion of judicial officers other than District Judge to the post of District Judge, by the Governor in consultation with the HC.
  2. Direct recruitment of persons who are:
    • Not already employed under the service of the Union or a State;
    • Have been an advocate for at least 7 years; and
    • Are recommended by the HC for such appointment.
[Additional] The Governor also has power over transfers, postings, and promotions of district judges, but only in consultation with the HC. The HC's control is vested through Article 235.

Judges Other than District Judges (Article 234)

  • Judges other than district judges in the judicial service of a State shall be appointed by the Governor in accordance with rules made by the Governor after consultation with the High Court and the State Public Service Commission.
[Correction] The rough notes referred to a "Bhoomika Trust case 2026" in which the SC allegedly held that advocates with minimum 1 year of experience are eligible for appointment to judicial offices other than district judges. [Verification Required] This case name and holding could not be reliably verified from any official source. The specific eligibility criteria for judicial officers other than district judges (under Article 234) are prescribed by State-level rules framed in consultation with the HC and SPSC, and vary from State to State. Readers should verify the cited case independently before relying on it.

Control over Subordinate Courts (Article 235)

  • The control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto — including posting, promotion, and grant of leave to persons belonging to the judicial service of a State and holding any post inferior to the post of district judge — is vested in the High Court.
Exam Point: Article 235 vests control of subordinate judiciary in the HC, not in the State executive. This is a key feature of judicial independence at the subordinate court level. The Governor's role in appointment (Arts 233-234) is exercised only in consultation with the HC.

9. Tribunals (Articles 323A and 323B)

Constitutional Basis

Tribunals are quasi-judicial bodies created for quick disposal of specialised cases. They were given constitutional status through the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, which inserted Part XIV-A (Tribunals) containing Articles 323A and 323B.

[Correction] The rough notes stated "42nd amendment Act 1972." The correct year is 1976. The 42nd Amendment was enacted during the Emergency and received Presidential assent on 18 December 1976. Most provisions came into force on 3 January 1977.

Key Differences between Tribunals and Courts

Article 323A — Administrative Tribunals

  • Parliament may by law provide for the establishment of administrative tribunals for adjudication of disputes and complaints with respect to recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, any State, any local or other authority, or any public sector corporation.
  • Such tribunals can be established only by Parliament (not by State Legislatures).
  • There is no provision for a hierarchy of administrative tribunals or appellate tribunals under Article 323A.

Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)

  • Established under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, enacted by Parliament under Article 323A.
  • Exercises jurisdiction over service matters of Central Government employees and employees of certain other authorities.
  • Has benches across India; the principal bench is in New Delhi.
  • Members are drawn from both judicial and administrative streams.
[Additional] State Administrative Tribunals (SATs) can also be established under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 at the request of State governments. Several States have established SATs for adjudication of State government service matters.

Article 323B — Tribunals for Other Matters

  • Parliament or State Legislatures may by law provide for tribunals for adjudication of disputes relating to:
  1. Taxation
  2. Foreign exchange, import and export
  3. Industrial and labour disputes
  4. Land reforms
  5. Ceiling on urban property
  6. Elections to Parliament and State Legislatures (excluding Articles 329 and 329A)
  7. Production, procurement, supply and distribution of foodstuffs and essential commodities
  8. Rent, tenancy rights, and related matters
Exam Point: Under Article 323A, only Parliament can establish tribunals. Under Article 323B, both Parliament and State Legislatures can establish tribunals on their respective subjects.

Functioning of Tribunals

  • When a tribunal is established, the jurisdiction of the subject matter is transferred from ordinary courts to that tribunal.
  • Tribunals have historically been provided with logistical and financial support by their respective government departments.
  • Members and chairpersons are appointed through committees, where executives have traditionally played a larger role (this has been a source of criticism).

Issues with Tribunals

  1. Executive influence:
    • The executive has had a larger role in appointment of members.
    • Subject matter experts are generally retired civil servants who might be closer to the executive.
  2. Dependence on government: Tribunals depend on the respective government departments for financial and logistical requirements.
  3. Conditions of service: Members of tribunals do not have the same protection as judges of constitutional courts.
  4. Functioning independence:
    • Several tribunals already have a high pendency of cases, defeating their original purpose.
    • Tribunals are located at limited places, creating access-to-justice barriers.
    • Creation of large numbers of tribunals in an arbitrary manner has been criticised as "tribunalisation of justice".
[Additional] In L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997), the Supreme Court held that:
  • Tribunals under Articles 323A and 323B are subject to the writ jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226/227.
  • The power of judicial review of the HC and SC cannot be excluded by creating tribunals, as judicial review is part of the basic structure of the Constitution.
  • Thus, clauses 2(d) of Article 323A and 3(d) of Article 323B (which sought to exclude HC and SC jurisdiction) were declared unconstitutional.
This is a landmark case for tribunals and is frequently tested in UPSC.
[Additional] In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2014), the Supreme Court laid down guidelines for tribunal appointments, requiring judicial members to have qualifications comparable to HC judges and fixing minimum tenure and service conditions to ensure independence.

  • Art 226: Writs for FRs + any other purpose (wider than Art 32).

  • Art 227: Superintendence — administrative + judicial.

11. Mains Points

  • Judicial independence at subordinate level: Arts 233-235 vest appointment and control in the Governor (in consultation with HC) and the HC respectively, keeping the State executive's role consultative. Discuss how Art 235 is the constitutional shield for subordinate judiciary independence.
  • Tribunalisation of justice: Discuss the tension between specialised, speedy adjudication (purpose of tribunals) and the erosion of judicial oversight. Use L. Chandra Kumar (1997) to argue that tribunals are supplementary to, not substitutes for, HCs.
  • Separation of judiciary from executive: Article 50 (DPSP) directs the State to separate judiciary from executive. At the subordinate level, this separation was achieved by separating judicial magistrates from executive magistrates through the CrPC (1973).
  • Transfer of HC judges: Discuss the collegium's role, the Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth case, and the balance between judicial independence and the need to prevent judges from becoming entrenched in one HC.
  • Art 226 vs Art 32: Discuss why Art 226 is wider in scope but Art 32 is a fundamental right and how both serve as twin pillars of judicial review.
  • Independence of tribunals: Discuss executive influence in appointments, Madras Bar Association (2014) guidelines, and the need for structural reform.

14. Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Source note: Only questions directly relevant to today's topics and traceable to UPSC Civil Services papers are included.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019, General Studies Paper I

In India, the separation of judiciary from the executive is enjoined by

(a) the Preamble of the Constitution
(b) a Directive Principle of State Policy
(c) the Seventh Schedule
(d) the conventional practice

Answer: (b) a Directive Principle of State Policy
Explanation: Article 50, a Directive Principle of State Policy (Part IV), states: "The State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State." This is directly relevant to the independence of subordinate courts, where separation was substantially achieved through the CrPC, 1973 by separating judicial magistrates from executive magistrates.

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2022, General Studies Paper I

With reference to the writs issued by the Courts in India, consider the following statements:

  1. Mandamus will not lie against a private organisation unless it is entrusted with a public duty.
  2. Mandamus will not lie against a Company even though it may be a Government Company.
  3. Any public-minded person can be a petitioner to move the Court to obtain the writ of Quo Warranto.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only
(b) 2 and 3 only
(c) 1 and 3 only
(d) 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (c) 1 and 3 only
Explanation: Mandamus lies against any authority performing a public function, including a Government Company — so Statement 2 is incorrect. Quo warranto can be filed by any person (not just an aggrieved party) since it challenges the legal authority to hold a public office. Mandamus will not lie against a purely private body unless it is entrusted with a public duty. This question tests writ jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 32.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017, General Studies Paper I

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Chief Justice of India (CJI) can assign any judge of one High Court to another High Court.
  2. If the judge of a High Court is transferred to another High Court by the President, he is entitled to receive a compensatory allowance in addition to his salary.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only
(b) 2 only
(c) Both 1 and 2
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (b) 2 only
Explanation: Under Article 222(1), the President (not the CJI) transfers HC judges after consultation with the CJI. Statement 1 is incorrect because transfer is a presidential act. Statement 2 is correct — Article 222(2) provides for compensatory allowance as determined by Parliament by law.

Q4. UPSC CSE Mains 2019, General Studies Paper II

"Do you think that the Constitution of India does not accept the principle of strict separation of powers rather it is based on the principle of 'checks and balances'? Explain."

Answer framework: Use HC superintendence over subordinate courts (Art 227), Art 228 (withdrawal of constitutional cases), Art 226 vs Art 32 (dual writ jurisdiction as a check), the role of the Governor in subordinate judiciary appointments (Arts 233-234) in consultation with the HC, and the establishment of tribunals under executive initiative but subject to HC judicial review (L. Chandra Kumar) as examples of checks and balances rather than strict separation.

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021, General Studies Paper I

With reference to the Indian judiciary, consider the following statements:

  1. Any retired judge of the Supreme Court of India can be called back to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge by the Chief Justice of India with the prior permission of the President of India.
  2. A High Court in India has the power to review its own judgment as the Supreme Court does.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only
(b) 2 only
(c) Both 1 and 2
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (c) Both 1 and 2
Explanation: Under Article 128, the CJI may, with the previous consent of the President, request a retired SC judge to sit and act as an SC judge (analogous to Art 224A for HCs). HCs also possess the power to review their own judgments — derived from Section 114 read with Order 47 of the CPC and their inherent power as courts of record under Art 215 (though the HC's review power comes from statute, not from a separate constitutional article like Art 137 for the SC).
