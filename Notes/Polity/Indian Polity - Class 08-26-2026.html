1. Contempt of Court

Constitutional Basis

  • Article 129: The Supreme Court is a court of record and has all the powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself.
  • Article 215: Every High Court is a court of record with the power to punish for contempt of itself.
  • Article 142(2): Subject to parliamentary law, the Supreme Court has powers regarding investigation and punishment of contempt of itself.
  • Article 19(2): Permits reasonable restrictions on freedom of speech in relation to contempt of court.
  • The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 defines, regulates and prescribes punishment for civil and criminal contempt.

Two Types of Contempt

TypeDefinition (Section 2, Contempt of Courts Act, 1971)
Civil ContemptWilful disobedience of a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court.
Criminal ContemptPublication (whether by words, spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representations, or otherwise) of any matter, or doing of any other act, which:
  • scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; or
  • prejudices or interferes or tends to interfere with the due course of any judicial proceeding; or
  • interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner.
[Correction] The rough notes listed "obstructing a judicial procedure" and "obstructing administration of justice" as separate grounds of criminal contempt. Under Section 2(c) of the 1971 Act, there are three limbs: (i) scandalising the court, (ii) prejudicing or interfering with judicial proceedings, and (iii) obstructing the administration of justice in any other manner. The wording above reflects the statutory definition.

Exceptions: When Criticism Is Not Contempt

The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 provides the following statutory defences:

  1. Innocent publication and distribution (Section 3): Publication made without knowledge that a proceeding was pending.
  2. Fair and accurate reporting (Section 4): Fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding.
  3. Fair criticism of judicial acts (Section 5): Fair comment on the merits of a case that has been finally decided, including criticism of the judgment itself.
  4. Truth as a defence (Section 13, inserted by the Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act, 2006): The court may permit justification by truth as a valid defence if it is satisfied that the defence is in the public interest and the request for invoking the defence is bona fide.
[Correction] The rough notes listed "criticism of the administrative functioning of the courts" as an exception. The 1971 Act's Section 5 protects fair comment on the merits of a finally decided case. Criticism of general administrative functioning of courts (delays, infrastructure, etc.) may be protected as free speech under Article 19(1)(a), but it is not a named statutory defence under the 1971 Act itself.
[Additional] Limitation: Under Section 20 of the 1971 Act, no contempt proceedings may ordinarily be initiated after one year from the date on which the contempt is alleged to have been committed.

Jurisdiction for Contempt

CourtContempt jurisdiction
Supreme CourtPunishes for contempt of itself under Article 129.
High CourtPunishes for contempt of itself and of subordinate courts under Article 215 read with the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971.
Exam Point: Articles 129 and 215 expressly mention contempt of the court "itself." The High Court's power over subordinate courts comes from the 1971 Act and the HC's superintendence power under Article 227.

Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat (1991)

The Supreme Court held that it possesses the power to punish for contempt of courts other than itself as well. This power was derived from Article 129 read with Article 142 of the Constitution.

[Additional] This case is significant because it expanded the textual scope of Article 129 (which says "contempt of itself") by reading it with Article 142's plenary "complete justice" power.

[Correction] The rough notes mentioned "approval of Attorney General" and "Advocate General." The correct term under the 1971 Act is consent, not merely approval. Additionally, the Solicitor General may also grant consent for Supreme Court contempt proceedings. Section 15 of the 1971 Act governs the procedure.
[Additional] Maximum punishment: Section 12 of the 1971 Act prescribes a maximum of simple imprisonment for six months or a fine of Rs 2,000 or both. The accused may be discharged or the punishment remitted on apology satisfactory to the court.
[Additional] Subordinate courts cannot punish for contempt on their own. Only the Supreme Court and High Courts have the constitutional power to punish for contempt. If a subordinate court is faced with contempt, it must refer the matter to the concerned High Court.
[Additional] Appeals in contempt: Under Section 19 of the 1971 Act, an appeal from a contempt order of a High Court lies to the Supreme Court. However, there is no appeal from a contempt order of the Supreme Court itself (it being the apex court), though a review or curative petition may be pursued.

Notable Contempt Cases

CaseFacts and significance
In re Prashant Bhushan (2020)The Supreme Court held advocate Prashant Bhushan guilty of criminal contempt for tweets criticising the CJI and the role of the last four CJIs. He was fined Re 1 (a token fine). The case reignited the debate on whether the "scandalising the court" ground chills free speech and fair criticism.

2. Miscellaneous Powers of the Supreme Court

Article 137: Review of Judgments (Review Petition)

The Supreme Court shall have the power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it, subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament or any rules made under Article 145.

Key Features

  • Review is a discretionary power of the Supreme Court; it may accept or reject a review petition.
  • Grounds for review (under Order XLVII of the Supreme Court Rules, analogous to Order 47 Rule 1 of the CPC):
    1. Discovery of new and important matter or evidence which, after exercise of due diligence, was not within the knowledge of or could not be produced at the time the decree was passed;
    2. Mistake or error apparent on the face of the record;
    3. Any other sufficient reason found suitable by the Court.
  • A review petition is generally heard by the same bench that decided the original case. However, it may be referred to a larger bench if required.
  • Review is ordinarily heard by circulation (without oral arguments) unless the Court orders otherwise.
[Correction] The rough notes stated that Article 137 is "subject to a law of parliament and rules of supreme court." More precisely, it is subject to provisions of any law made by Parliament or any rules made under Article 145.

Source of Review Power: Supreme Court vs High Courts

CourtSource of review power
Supreme CourtDerives review power directly from the Constitution (Article 137).
High CourtDerives review power from statute (Section 114 read with Order 47 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908) and its inherent power as a court of record (Article 215). There is no separate constitutional article granting review power to High Courts.
[Correction] The rough notes stated "High court gets the review petition power made from a supreme court judgement or a law." This is imprecise. The High Court's review power comes from statutory law (CPC) and its inherent status as a court of record, not from a Supreme Court judgment. The Supreme Court's review power has a direct constitutional source in Article 137.

Curative Petition [Not mentioned in the Constitution]

A curative petition is a judicial remedy created by the Supreme Court itself and is not mentioned anywhere in the Constitution.

Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002)

The Supreme Court held that even after a review petition has been dismissed, a curative petition may be filed in the Supreme Court in exceptional cases to prevent abuse of process and gross miscarriage of justice. The Court derived this power from Article 142 (complete justice).

Grounds for a Curative Petition

  1. Violation of principles of natural justice:
    • Audi alteram partem (right to be heard) — all parties must be given an opportunity to be heard;
    • Nemo judex in causa sua — no one shall be a judge in their own cause;
    • The decision-maker must not be biased.
  2. Abuse of the process of the court.
[Additional] Procedural requirements: A curative petition must be circulated to the three senior-most judges and the judges who delivered the impugned judgment. It must be accompanied by a certification by a senior advocate that substantial grounds exist. It is heard in chambers, not in open court, unless the judges direct otherwise.
[Correction] The rough notes cited "Hurra Vs Hurra case 2002." The correct citation is Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002) 4 SCC 388.

Article 139A: Transfer of Cases (42nd Amendment Act, 1976)

Article 139A was inserted by the Constitution (42nd Amendment) Act, 1976. It confers two powers on the Supreme Court:

  1. Transfer of cases (Article 139A(1)): Where cases involving the same or substantially the same questions of law are pending before the Supreme Court and one or more High Courts, or before two or more High Courts, the Supreme Court may, if satisfied that such questions are substantial questions of general importance, withdraw the case(s) from the High Court(s) and:
    • Dispose of all the cases itself; or
    • Determine the said questions of law and return the cases to the respective High Courts with its determination for disposal.
  2. Transfer Petition (Article 139A(2)): The Supreme Court may transfer any case, appeal or other proceeding from one High Court to another if satisfied that it is expedient for the ends of justice.
[Correction] The rough notes stated that the Supreme Court may "transfer a case from one High Court to another" as the first power and "take to itself cases" as the second. The order as per the Article is: clause (1) deals with withdrawal of cases involving the same question of law, and clause (2) deals with inter-High Court transfer. This distinction matters for precise Article reference.
[Additional] Article 139A should be distinguished from Article 222 (transfer of High Court judges) and from the inherent transfer power within a single High Court's jurisdiction. Transfer petitions under Article 139A(2) are commonly filed in matrimonial and family law disputes.

Article 141: Law Declared by the Supreme Court

"The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India."

Key Principles

  • Judgments of the Supreme Court where they have declared the law are binding on all courts in India, including High Courts and subordinate courts.
  • A smaller or equal bench of the Supreme Court is also bound by the law declared by an earlier bench. Such a bench cannot overrule or re-interpret the earlier decision.
  • If a smaller or equal bench disagrees with the earlier law, it may refer the matter to a larger bench for reconsideration.
  • Only a bench of equal or greater strength may overrule an earlier decision.
Exam Point: Article 141 binds all courts. It does not, in its terms, bind tribunals, quasi-judicial bodies or the government directly, although in practice government authorities are expected to follow the law as declared.
[Additional] The ratio decidendi (the principle of law on which the decision is based) is binding under Article 141, not the obiter dicta (incidental observations). This distinction is frequently tested.
[Additional] Prospective overruling [Not mentioned in the Constitution]: In I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967), the Supreme Court applied the doctrine of prospective overruling — declaring a new legal position but making it effective only for future cases, not disturbing past transactions. This technique is occasionally used to avoid disruption when overruling long-standing precedent.
[Additional] Sub silentio decisions: A decision is said to be sub silentio when a point of law was not argued or consciously decided. Such decisions are not binding precedent under Article 141 because the Court did not apply its mind to that question.
Exam Point: Article 141 says "law declared" — not every observation in a judgment is law. Only the ratio decidendi (the legal principle essential to the decision) has binding force. Obiter dicta have persuasive value only.

Article 142: Complete Justice

Article 142 is the most important discretionary power of the Supreme Court and the most significant basis for judicial activism in India.

Text and Scope

  • Article 142(1): The Supreme Court, in the exercise of its jurisdiction, may pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it. Such decrees or orders shall be enforceable throughout India in a manner prescribed by law made by Parliament or, until such provision is made, by order of the President.
  • Article 142(2): Subject to law made by Parliament, the Supreme Court shall have all and every power to make any order for securing the attendance of any person, the discovery or production of documents, or the investigation and punishment of contempt of itself.

Significance for Judicial Activism

Article 142 gives wide discretionary powers to the Supreme Court to issue any suitable order or judgment while deciding a case if the prevailing laws on the matter are not sufficient to provide complete justice. The Court may, through this power:

  • Issue orders that are valid only for the specific case at hand; or
  • Issue directions to be followed as law in future similar cases (effectively exercising a quasi-legislative function).

The judiciary may thereby exercise functions that are otherwise executive or legislative in nature.

Article 143: Advisory Jurisdiction

The President may seek the opinion of the Supreme Court on any question of law or fact that has arisen or is likely to arise and is of such public importance that it is expedient to obtain the Court's opinion.

[Additional] The President makes a reference under Article 143 on the advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74). It is not a personal discretionary power of the President.

Two Clauses

[Additional] Article 143(2) was omitted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 and was restored by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978. It currently exists in the Constitution.

Nature of the Advisory Opinion

  • The opinion of the Supreme Court under Article 143 is advisory and is not binding on any court, including the Supreme Court itself.
  • A minimum bench of 5 judges hears advisory references (Article 145(3)).
[Correction] The rough notes stated "The opinion of SC given under this article is not necessarily on other courts." The intended meaning is that the opinion is not binding on other courts. The opinion is advisory in nature and does not have the force of a decree or binding precedent under Article 141.

Article 144: Duty of Authorities to Assist the Supreme Court

"All authorities, civil and judicial, in the territory of India shall act in aid of the Supreme Court."

[Correction] The rough notes placed the rules of Supreme Court procedure under Article 144. This is incorrect. Article 144 is a brief provision requiring all civil and judicial authorities in India to act in aid of the Supreme Court. The rules of practice and procedure are governed by Article 145.

Article 145: Rules of Court

The Supreme Court may make rules for regulating generally the practice and procedure of the Court, subject to any law made by Parliament and with the approval of the President.

Matters Covered by Rules

  1. The manner of filing different petitions (writs, special leave petitions, review petitions, etc.).
  2. The manner of filing appeals and bail applications.
  3. The formation of benches and allocation of cases to benches.
  4. The form and content of judgments — judgments of the Supreme Court include the opinions of all judges in the bench, including any dissenting opinions.
  5. Timing, calendar and sittings of the Supreme Court.
  6. Court fees.
  7. Conditions for Advocates-on-Record (AOR) practising in the Supreme Court.

Article 145(3): Minimum Bench for Constitutional Questions

A bench of minimum 5 judges shall sit for the purpose of:

  • Deciding cases involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution; and
  • Hearing advisory references under Article 143.
Exam Point: Advocate-on-Record (AOR) [Not mentioned in the Constitution]: Only an AOR may file cases and appear on record before the Supreme Court. To become an AOR, an advocate must pass an examination conducted by the Supreme Court. Senior Advocates are designated by the Supreme Court or High Courts on the basis of ability and standing; they cannot file cases directly and must appear with an AOR. These designations are governed by the Supreme Court Rules and the Advocates Act, 1961, not by the Constitution itself.

Article 146: Officers and Servants of the Supreme Court

  • Article 146(1): Appointments of officers and servants of the Supreme Court shall be made by the Chief Justice of India or such other judge or officer as the CJI may direct. The President may require that no person not already attached to the Court be appointed without consultation with the Union Public Service Commission.
  • Article 146(2): The CJI may make rules regarding conditions of service of officers and servants, provided that rules regarding salaries, allowances, leave or pensions shall require the approval of the President.
  • Article 146(3): The administrative expenses of the Supreme Court, including all salaries, allowances and pensions payable to or in respect of its officers and servants, shall be charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
Exam Point: "Charged expenditure" means it is not subject to vote in Parliament. This protects the financial independence of the Supreme Court.

3. High Courts (Articles 214-232)

Article 214: High Courts for States

There shall be a High Court for each State.

[Additional] "Each State" does not mean each State must have a separate High Court. Article 231 allows a common High Court for two or more States.

Article 231: Common High Court for Multiple States

Parliament may by law establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and a Union Territory.

Article 216: Composition of High Courts

Every High Court shall consist of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem it necessary to appoint.

Exam Point: The Constitution does not fix the number of High Court judges. The President determines this number. In practice, the sanctioned strength is decided by the central government in consultation with the CJI.

Article 217: Qualifications, Appointment and Retirement

Qualifications

  • Must be a citizen of India; and
  • Has held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least 10 years; or
  • Has been an advocate of a High Court (or two or more such courts in succession) for at least 10 years.

Retirement Age

62 years (compared to 65 years for Supreme Court judges).

Article 222: Transfer of HC Judges

The President may, after consultation with the CJI, transfer a judge from one High Court to any other High Court. A transferred judge is entitled to receive, in addition to salary, a compensatory allowance as determined by Parliament by law.

[Additional] In practice, transfers are decided by the collegium. The judge's consent is not constitutionally required, but the collegium follows convention of recording reasons. In Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977), the SC held that transfer of HC judges is an executive act that does not require the judge's consent, but the process must be transparent and fair.

Article 224A: Retired HC Judges Sitting

The CJI may, with the previous consent of the President and the consent of the retired judge, request a duly qualified person who has been a judge of a High Court to sit and act as a judge. This is analogous to Article 128 for the Supreme Court.

Article 218: Removal of High Court Judges

The provisions of Article 124 relating to removal of Supreme Court judges apply to the removal of High Court judges. The process is identical:

  • Grounds: proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Motion signed by at least 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members.
  • Three-member inquiry committee under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.
  • Each House passes the address by a majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting, in the same session.
  • President issues the removal order.

Article 219: Oath of High Court Judges

  • A High Court judge makes and subscribes an oath or affirmation before the Governor of the State (or the President, if the principal bench is in a UT).
  • The form of oath is in the Third Schedule.
  • The oath is substantively similar to the oath of Supreme Court judges under Article 124(6).

Article 220: Restriction on Practice after Leaving Office

No person who, after the commencement of the Constitution, has held office as a permanent judge of a High Court shall plead or act in any court or before any authority in India except:

  • The Supreme Court; and
  • Other High Courts (i.e., High Courts in which that person was not a permanent judge).

4. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Two types of contempt: civil (wilful disobedience/breach) and criminal (scandalising court/interfering with justice).
  • SC punishes for contempt of itself (Art 129); HC punishes for itself and subordinate courts (Art 215 + 1971 Act).
  • Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat (1991): SC can punish for contempt of other courts too.
  • Contempt proceedings: suo motu or with consent of Attorney General (SC) / Advocate General (HC).
  • Maximum punishment: simple imprisonment up to 6 months or fine up to Rs 2,000.
  • Truth as a defence: 2006 Amendment to the Contempt of Courts Act.
  • Review petition (Art 137): discretionary; same bench; constitutional source for SC, statutory for HC.
  • Curative petition: Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002); not in the Constitution; based on Art 142.
  • Art 139A: Transfer of cases; 42nd Amendment, 1976.
  • Art 141: Law declared by SC binding on all courts.
  • Art 142: Complete justice; basis of judicial activism; cannot take away fundamental rights (Prem Chand Garg, 1963).
  • Art 143(1): Advisory opinion is discretionary. Art 143(2): Mandatory for pre-Constitution treaty disputes. Advisory opinion is not binding.
  • Art 143(2) was omitted by the 42nd Amendment (1976) and restored by the 44th Amendment (1978).
  • Art 144: All authorities shall act in aid of SC. Art 145: Rules of Court. Art 146: Officers and servants.
  • Minimum 5-judge bench for constitutional questions and advisory references (Art 145(3)).
  • SC expenses are charged on CFI (Art 146(3)).
  • HC for every State (Art 214); common HC (Art 231); composition fixed by President (Art 216).
  • HC judge qualifications: citizen + 10 years advocate or 10 years judicial officer. Retirement: 62.
  • Collegium for HC: CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges (Third Judges Case, 1998).
  • HC judge removal: same as SC (Art 218); oath by Governor (Art 219).
  • Art 220: Former permanent HC judge may practise only in SC and other HCs. Does not apply to additional/acting judges.
  • Art 222: Transfer of HC judges by President on CJI consultation. Transferee gets compensatory allowance.
  • Art 224: Additional judges (max 2 years) for arrears; acting judges for temporary vacancies.
  • Art 224A: Retired HC judges may sit and act with consent of President and the judge.
  • Art 226: HC writs for FR + any other purpose (wider than Art 32). Art 227: Superintendence over subordinate courts.
  • India has 25 High Courts. Some serve multiple States (Bombay, Punjab & Haryana, Guwahati).
  • Subordinate courts cannot punish for contempt themselves — only HC can for subordinate courts.
  • Contempt: Prashant Bhushan (2020) — guilty of criminal contempt for tweets; fined Re 1.
  • Largest SC bench: 13 judges in Kesavananda Bharati (1973). CJI is "Master of the Roster."
  • Art 141: Only ratio decidendi is binding, not obiter dicta. Sub silentio decisions are not binding.
  • Prospective overruling: Golak Nath (1967) — new position applied only prospectively.

5. Mains Points

  • Contempt jurisdiction protects judicial authority but must be balanced against free speech (Art 19(1)(a)). Over-broad use can chill legitimate criticism and public accountability.
  • Article 142 enables the Supreme Court to fill legislative gaps and ensure justice in individual cases, but its untrammelled use risks judicial overreach and erosion of separation of powers.
  • The curative petition demonstrates judicial self-correction, but its extremely narrow scope means most litigants have no effective remedy after review dismissal.
  • Advisory jurisdiction under Article 143 allows the executive to seek judicial guidance on sensitive issues, but the non-binding nature limits its practical utility.
  • The collegium system ensures judicial independence in appointments but faces criticism for lack of transparency and accountability. The NJAC's striking down highlighted the tension between democratic participation and judicial independence.
  • Common High Courts (Art 231) promote judicial economy but may create access-to-justice challenges for litigants in States without a principal bench.
  • Transfer of HC judges (Art 222) is a tool for both administrative convenience and accountability, but it has been criticised as a mechanism to "punish" independent-minded judges without the rigours of removal proceedings.
  • Article 141 creates a unique vertical hierarchy of precedent: smaller benches are bound, but the absence of a formal mechanism to efficiently overrule outdated law creates doctrinal rigidity.

7. 30-Second Revision

Contempt: Civil (disobedience/breach) + Criminal (scandalise/obstruct). SC = Art 129 (self); HC = Art 215 (self + subordinate). Procedure: suo motu or consent of AG/Advocate General.
Review: Art 137 (SC, constitutional source); same bench; grounds: new evidence, error apparent, sufficient reason.
Curative: Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002); after review dismissed; abuse of process / natural justice violation; Art 142 basis.
Transfer: Art 139A (42nd Amdt); withdrawal of cases with same question of law + inter-HC transfer.
Binding law: Art 141; ratio decidendi binds all courts; smaller bench cannot overrule.
Complete justice: Art 142; judicial activism; cannot take away rights (1963); supplement law, not contradict.
Advisory: Art 143(1) may, 143(2) shall; not binding; min 5 judges.
SC admin: Art 144 (aid SC); Art 145 (rules, 5-judge min); Art 146 (officers, charged expenditure).
High Courts: Art 214 (HC for every State); Art 231 (common HC); Art 216 (CJ + President-fixed judges); Art 217 (qualifications, 10+10, retire 62, collegium CJI+2); Art 218 (removal = SC process); Art 219 (oath by Governor); Art 220 (permanent judges: practice in SC + other HCs only); Art 222 (transfer by President/CJI); Art 224 (additional 2yr / acting judges); Art 226 (writs for FR + any other purpose); Art 227 (superintendence).

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014, General Studies Paper I

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Supreme Court of India has the power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it.
  2. The Constitution does not provide for review of its judgment/order by the Supreme Court.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only
(b) 2 only
(c) Both 1 and 2
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (a) 1 only
Explanation: Article 137 expressly provides that the Supreme Court shall have the power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it. Statement 2 is incorrect because this power is a constitutional provision, not merely a statutory one.

Q6. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017, General Studies Paper I

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Chief Justice of India (CJI) can assign any judge of one High Court to another High Court.

(c) Both 1 and 2
