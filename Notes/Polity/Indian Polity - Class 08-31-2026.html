1. Meaning and Classification of Rights

Rights are claims or interests of individuals or groups that are recognised and protected by the State. They may be classified by the source and degree of their legal protection.

[Correction] The rough notes described the right to property and right to vote simply as "legal rights" despite their constitutional mention. The precise position differs. Article 300A creates a constitutional right to property, though it is no longer a Fundamental Right and is ordinarily enforced under Article 226, not Article 32. Article 326 constitutionally requires adult suffrage, but the right to vote and contest elections in a particular manner is primarily a statutory right governed by election laws. Courts have often called electoral rights statutory while recognising their constitutional setting.
[Additional] In K.T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2011), the Supreme Court treated Article 300A as a constitutional right and held that deprivation of property must be by authority of a valid law. In Rajbala v. State of Haryana (2015), the Court reiterated that the right to vote and contest elections is not a Fundamental Right but a right regulated by statute.

2. Fundamental Rights: Purpose, Sources and Features

2.1 Meaning and Purpose

Fundamental Rights are the enforceable rights guaranteed in Part III. They protect individual dignity and liberty, restrain arbitrary State power and create conditions in which people can develop their personalities to their fullest potential.

2.2 Important Intellectual and Historical Sources

  • United States Bill of Rights: major comparative source for enforceable rights and judicial protection.
  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948: adopted shortly before the Constitution; reflected the post-war international human-rights movement.
  • French Revolution: ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity, also reflected in the Preamble.
  • Magna Carta, 1215: an early landmark in limiting arbitrary authority and protecting liberty according to law.
  • Motilal Nehru Committee Report, 1928: proposed a justiciable declaration of fundamental rights.
  • Sapru Committee Report: distinguished justiciable and non-justiciable rights.
[Correction] The Sapru Committee was appointed in 1944, but its constitutional proposals/report were published in 1945. Therefore, "Sapru Committee Report, 1944" confuses the appointment year with the report year.

2.3 Features

  • Justiciable: courts can enforce them. Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right; High Courts also act under Article 226.
  • Not generally absolute: many rights permit constitutionally specified restrictions. Some protections, such as Articles 17 and 24, are framed as direct prohibitions rather than qualified freedoms.
  • Amendable, but not destructible: Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368, but cannot damage the Constitution's basic structure.
  • Mainly civil and political: Part III principally protects equality, liberty, dignity and participation, while many socio-economic goals appear in Part IV.
  • Mostly negative, but some impose positive duties: many restrain State action; Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24 also operate against private conduct, and Article 21 has generated positive obligations.
  • Some are for citizens only: Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29(1) expressly use "citizen" or "citizens." Rights such as Articles 14, 20 and 21 protect every person; Article 30 protects religious and linguistic minorities.
  • Many are self-executing, but not all: some apply without implementing legislation, while provisions such as Articles 17, 23, 24, 25(2), 30 and 35 may require or contemplate legislation.
[Correction] The statements "most Fundamental Rights are negative" and "most are self-executable" are useful generalisations, not constitutional rules. Their operation must be checked Article by Article; modern Article 21 jurisprudence imposes substantial positive duties on the State.

3.1 Right to Property

Originally, property was protected by Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 omitted Article 19(1)(f), repealed Article 31 and inserted Article 300A: no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law.

[Correction] It is incomplete to say that the 44th Amendment merely "repealed" the right to property. It removed property from Part III and preserved it as a constitutional right under Article 300A. The amendment was enacted in 1978 and the relevant provisions came into force on 20 June 1979.

[Correction] The rough notes associated Article 34 with police officers and Article 35 with martial law. The correct sequence is: Article 33 covers armed forces, public-order forces (which can include police) and specified services; Article 34 concerns martial law; Article 35 reserves specified legislative matters to Parliament.

4. Article 12: Meaning of "State"

Article 12: Unless the context otherwise requires, "the State" in Part III includes the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and Legislature of each State, and all local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

Article 12 uses the word "includes", allowing courts to develop its scope. Whether a body is State is important because most Fundamental Rights are primarily enforceable against State action.

4.1 Local Authorities

Local authorities normally possess a separate legal existence, function within a defined local area, have a degree of local participation or control, enjoy some autonomy, perform civic/public functions and possess funds or revenue-raising capacity.

Examples: municipalities, panchayats, port trusts and cantonment boards. A notified industrial township may perform municipal functions under Article 243Q, but every industrial undertaking is not automatically a local authority.

[Correction] The rough notes attributed a six-part local-authority test to Mohammad Yasin v. Town Area Committee, Jalalabad (1952). That case treated a statutory town-area committee as State and invalidated a fee that infringed Article 19(1)(g), but the commonly cited detailed characteristics were consolidated in later decisions, especially Union of India v. R.C. Jain (1981). BHEL itself is not a "local authority" merely because it operates an industrial township.

4.2 "Other Authorities" and Instrumentalities of Government

"Other authorities" can include statutory corporations, government companies, societies and other bodies that are financially, functionally and administratively dominated by government. The legal form is not decisive; the real relationship with government is.

[Correction] Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram was decided in 1975, not 1984, and held ONGC, LIC and IFC to be authorities under Article 12. Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib was decided in 1981, not 1980. Its indicators are tests of governmental instrumentality, not six mandatory conditions.
[Additional] In Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002), a seven-judge bench held CSIR to be State. The controlling inquiry is whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under pervasive government control. The control must be particular to the body, not merely regulatory; no single Ajay Hasia indicator is invariably conclusive.

4.4 Is the Judiciary "State"?

[Correction] The rough notes attributed to A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak (1988) a broad rule that the judiciary is not State while performing judicial functions but is State in non-judicial functions. That is an overstatement and not the precise holding of Antulay. The case held that a court's own earlier direction had unlawfully deprived the accused of statutory and constitutional protections, and the Court corrected that injustice.

The safer exam position is:

  • Administrative and rule-making actions of courts are subject to Fundamental Rights and judicial review, for example recruitment or service decisions.
  • Judicial orders of superior courts are ordinarily challenged through appeal, review or curative jurisdiction, not by treating the judge as an executive authority and filing a fresh Article 32 petition.
  • In Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra (1966), the Supreme Court held that a judicial order of the High Court could not be attacked through Article 32 in the manner attempted. Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002) similarly held that a final Supreme Court judgment is not challenged by a fresh Article 32 petition, while creating the narrow curative-petition remedy.

5. Article 13: Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

ClauseRuleDoctrine / Effect
13(1)Pre-Constitution "laws in force" inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of inconsistency.They are not erased for the period before 26 January 1950. The doctrines of severability and eclipse may apply.
13(2)The State shall not make a law that takes away or abridges Part III rights; a contravening law is void to the extent of contravention.Post-Constitution law is invalid from inception to the extent of contravention, subject to the precise right, claimant and later constitutional developments.
13(3)(a)"Law" includes an Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, and a custom or usage having in India the force of law.The definition is inclusive and covers delegated legislation and legally binding custom.
13(3)(b)"Laws in force" includes unrepealed pre-Constitution laws even if not then operating everywhere or at all.Prevents dormant or territorially limited old laws from escaping Part III review.
13(4)Nothing in Article 13 applies to a constitutional amendment made under Article 368.Inserted by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971; amendments remain reviewable under the Basic Structure Doctrine.
[Correction] The phrase "void ab initio" is a useful shorthand for a post-Constitution law that violates Article 13(2), but the Constitution itself says "to the extent of the contravention". Severable valid portions can survive. Also, the doctrine of eclipse principally concerns pre-Constitution laws; its use for post-Constitution laws is exceptional and context-specific.

5.1 Core Doctrines under Article 13

  • Severability: only the unconstitutional part is removed if it can be separated and the legislature would have enacted the remainder independently.
  • Eclipse: an inconsistent pre-Constitution law becomes dormant against the Fundamental Right; it is not dead for all purposes and may revive if the constitutional obstacle is removed.
  • Waiver: a person generally cannot waive Fundamental Rights so as to validate unconstitutional State action (Basheshar Nath, 1959).
  • Judicial review: courts compare "law" and State action with Part III and declare the inconsistent portion void.

6. Article 13 vs Article 368: Evolution of the Basic Structure Doctrine

The conflict arose because Article 13 restrains the State from making laws that abridge Fundamental Rights, while Article 368 grants Parliament constituent power to amend the Constitution. Land reforms and other measures implementing Directive Principles generated repeated challenges under equality and property rights.

[Correction] The First Amendment did not add Article 31C. The First Amendment Act, 1951 amended Articles 15 and 19 and inserted Articles 31A, 31B and the Ninth Schedule. Article 31C was inserted later by the 25th Amendment Act, 1971.
Land reform and DPSP legislation → challenges under equality/property rights → constitutional amendments protecting reforms → dispute over "law" in Article 13 and amending power in Article 368 → Basic Structure Doctrine

6.1 Case-Law Timeline

Case / AmendmentBench / ResultConstitutional Position
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)Five-judge Constitution Bench; unanimous.Upheld the First Amendment. A constitutional amendment under Article 368 is an exercise of constituent power, not "law" made through ordinary legislative power for Article 13(2). Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.
Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)Five-judge bench; 3:2.Upheld the 17th Amendment and reaffirmed Shankari Prasad. Dissents by Justices Hidayatullah and Mudholkar raised doubts; Justice Mudholkar asked whether the Constitution had basic features.
I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967)Eleven-judge bench; 6:5.Overruled the earlier approach. Treated constitutional amendment as "law" for Article 13 and held Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. Used prospective overruling, leaving earlier amendments undisturbed.
24th Amendment (1971)Amended Articles 13 and 368.Inserted Article 13(4), expressly affirmed Parliament's constituent power to amend any provision and made presidential assent mandatory.
25th Amendment (1971)Inserted Article 31C.Protected laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c) from specified Fundamental Rights challenges and sought to limit review of the declared nexus.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)Thirteen-judge bench; 7:6 on the controlling limitation.Parliament may amend every part, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter or destroy the basic structure or framework. It overruled Golak Nath's absolute bar but imposed a structural limitation.
42nd Amendment (1976)Expanded Article 31C and inserted Article 368(4)-(5).Attempted to protect laws implementing any DPSP and declare Parliament's amending power unlimited and amendments beyond court challenge.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)Five-judge bench; majority.Invalidated the expansion of Article 31C and Article 368(4)-(5). Limited amending power, judicial review, and harmony between Parts III and IV are basic features. Parliament cannot use a limited power to convert itself into an unlimited power.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)Nine-judge bench; unanimous.Laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested for damage to the basic structure through their impact on Fundamental Rights that form part of that structure.

6.2 Shankari Prasad: Issue, Arguments and Order

  • Background: the First Amendment inserted Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule to protect land-reform enactments from Fundamental Rights challenges.
  • Petitioners' argument: a constitutional amendment is "law" under Article 13(2); therefore Parliament cannot abridge Part III. They also challenged the amendment process and Parliament's competence.
  • Union's argument: Article 368 confers constituent power; "law" in Article 13 refers to ordinary legislative law, not an amendment to the Constitution itself.
  • Holding: the Supreme Court accepted the Union's distinction. Article 13(2) restrains ordinary law-making, not constituent power under Article 368.
  • Order: the petitions were dismissed and the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 was upheld.
Exam Point: The present position is neither Shankari Prasad's unlimited amendability nor Golak Nath's complete immunity of Fundamental Rights. Under Kesavananda Bharati, all provisions are amendable, but the basic structure is not destructible.

7. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Part III extends from Articles 12 to 35; the substantive groups of rights are principally Articles 14-32.
  • Article 12 defines State for Part III and uses an inclusive definition.
  • Sukhdev Singh (1975): ONGC, LIC and IFC were State; Ajay Hasia (1981): legal form is not decisive.
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas (2002): CSIR was State; cumulative financial, functional and administrative domination is central.
  • Zee Telefilms (2005): BCCI was not State under Article 12, but public duties may be reviewed under Article 226.
  • Article 13(3)(a) includes Ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications and legally binding custom/usage.
  • Article 13(4) was inserted by the 24th Amendment, 1971.
  • The First Amendment inserted 31A, 31B and the Ninth Schedule, not Article 31C.
  • Article 31C came through the 25th Amendment, 1971.
  • The 44th Amendment removed property from Part III and inserted Article 300A.
  • Article 33: specified forces/services; Article 34: martial law; Article 35: Parliament's exclusive legislative power in listed Part III matters.
  • Shankari Prasad: amendment not "law" under Article 13; Golak Nath: amendment treated as law; Kesavananda: amendments permitted subject to basic structure.
  • The Basic Structure Doctrine was formulated by a 13-judge bench in 1973.

8. Mains Points

  • Fundamental Rights combine liberty with constitutional government: they protect personal development while supplying judicial standards against arbitrary public power.
  • Article 12 follows substance over form: government cannot evade Fundamental Rights by transferring its functions to corporations or societies; equally, public importance alone does not automatically convert every private body into State.
  • Article 13 constitutionalises the rule of law: ordinary law, delegated legislation and binding custom remain subordinate to Part III, with severability preventing unnecessary destruction of valid provisions.
  • Rights and DPSPs are complementary: Part III supplies enforceable means and Part IV supplies governance goals. Minerva Mills treats their harmony, not the absolute primacy of either, as part of the basic structure.
  • Basic structure reconciles continuity and change: Parliament can adapt every provision to social needs, but cannot use amendment to remove constitutional identity, limited government or judicial review.

10. 30-Second Revision

Rights hierarchy: FRs are Part III constitutional rights; Article 300A is constitutional but non-fundamental; voting has Article 326 support but is primarily statutory in exercise.
Part III: Arts. 12-35; equality 14-18, freedom 19-22, exploitation 23-24, religion 25-28, culture/education 29-30, remedies 32.
Article 12: governments + legislatures + local/other authorities. Sukhdev 1975; Ajay Hasia 1981; Pradeep Biswas 2002; BCCI not State in Zee Telefilms 2005.
Article 13: pre-law 13(1), post-law 13(2), definitions 13(3), amendment exclusion 13(4). Remember severability and eclipse.
Special provisions: 31A categories; 31B + Ninth Schedule; 31C = Art. 39(b)-(c); 33 forces; 34 martial law; 35 Parliament.
Amendment cases: Shankari Prasad allowed → Golak Nath barred FR amendment → 24th restored power → Kesavananda allowed amendment but protected basic structure → Minerva Mills confirmed limited power.

11. Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Source note: The following questions are directly relevant to today's topics and are traceable to UPSC Civil Services papers. Official UPSC archives are linked in the final section.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017, General Studies Paper I

Right to vote and to be elected in India is a

(a) Fundamental Right
(b) Natural Right
(c) Constitutional Right
(d) Legal Right

Answer: (c) Constitutional Right.
Explanation: Article 326 provides the constitutional basis for elections on adult suffrage. The detailed entitlement and procedure are regulated by the Representation of the People Acts. Judicial decisions often describe voting as a statutory right, but among these options UPSC's accepted classification is constitutional right because the Constitution itself establishes its foundation.

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021, General Studies Paper I

What is the position of the Right to Property in India?

(a) Legal right available to citizens only
(b) Legal right available to any person
(c) Fundamental Right available to citizens only
(d) Neither Fundamental Right nor legal right

Answer: (b) Legal right available to any person.
Explanation: Article 300A says that no person may be deprived of property except by authority of law. It is not confined to citizens and is no longer a Fundamental Right. In precise constitutional-law terminology it is a constitutional right, while option (b) is the closest and accepted UPSC answer.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020, General Studies Paper I

A constitutional government by definition is a

(a) government by legislature
(b) popular government
(c) multi-party government
(d) limited government

Answer: (d) Limited government.
Explanation: Fundamental Rights, judicial review and the Basic Structure Doctrine limit public power. Constitutional government means government operating within legally enforceable constitutional limits, not merely an elected or popular government.

Q4. UPSC CSE Mains 2013, General Studies Paper II

"The Supreme Court of India keeps a check on arbitrary power of the Parliament in amending the Constitution." Discuss critically.

Answer framework: Begin with Article 368 and the Article 13 conflict. Trace Shankari Prasad, Golak Nath, the 24th Amendment and Kesavananda Bharati. Explain judicial review of amendments through basic structure, then use Minerva Mills and I.R. Coelho. Critically note that the doctrine preserves constitutional identity but its open-ended content raises concerns about judicial discretion.

Q5. UPSC CSE Mains 2019, General Studies Paper II

"Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is a limited power and it cannot be enlarged into absolute power." In the light of this statement explain whether Parliament under Article 368 of the Constitution can destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution by expanding its amending power?

Answer framework: No. Kesavananda Bharati limits amendment by basic structure. Minerva Mills struck down Article 368(4)-(5), inserted by the 42nd Amendment, because a donee of limited power cannot convert that power into an unlimited one. Limited amending power and judicial review are themselves basic features.
