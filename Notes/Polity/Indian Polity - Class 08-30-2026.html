1. Judicial Review

1.1 Meaning

Judicial review is the power of the judiciary to examine the constitutional validity of legislative enactments and executive actions and to declare them void if they are found to be inconsistent with the Constitution. It is the mechanism through which the judiciary acts as the guardian of the Constitution and enforces the limits that the Constitution places on all organs of government.

[Correction] The rough notes stated that the Constitution has "not explicitly used the phrase Judicial Review in any Art." This is imprecise. While the original Constitution did not contain the phrase, the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 inserted Article 13(4) which provides that nothing in Article 13 shall apply to any amendment made under Article 368 — and the expression "judicial review" was used in the statement of objects and reasons and in constitutional commentary around these provisions. More importantly, the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 expressly restored judicial review protections. However, the core point is correct: the phrase "judicial review" does not appear as a named power in any operative Article — the power is derived from several constitutional provisions read together.

1.2 Constitutional Basis

The power of judicial review is not located in any single Article but flows from several provisions read together:

1.3 Scope — Grounds for Judicial Review in India

Through a series of judgments, the Supreme Court has established that laws and executive actions may be reviewed and declared invalid on the following grounds:

  1. Violation of Fundamental Rights (Part III) — Any law that takes away or abridges a Fundamental Right is void under Art. 13(2). This is the most common ground.
  2. Legislature exceeds its substantive powers (legislative competence) — If Parliament legislates on a matter exclusively in the State List, or a State Legislature legislates on a Union List matter, the law is ultra vires. The doctrine of pith and substance and colourable legislation are used to determine this.
  3. Violation of any other constitutional provision — Even if Fundamental Rights are not directly engaged, a law may be struck down if it violates another mandatory constitutional requirement (e.g., Art. 14 equality, Art. 265 taxation only by authority of law, Art. 300A right to property).
[Additional] The doctrine of procedure established by law (Art. 21) was expanded to include elements of due process in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978). The SC held that any law restricting personal liberty must satisfy the tests of reasonableness, fairness and non-arbitrariness — thereby broadening the grounds of judicial review significantly.
[Additional] Presumption of constitutionality: When a law is challenged, the judiciary presumes it is valid until the challenger proves otherwise. The burden of proof lies on the person who challenges the law. This is a well-established principle of constitutional adjudication and reflects judicial restraint.

1.5 Doctrine of Basic Structure and Judicial Review

Key Case: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)
A 13-judge bench (the largest in SC history) held by a 7:6 majority that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Art. 368 is not unlimited. Parliament cannot alter, damage or destroy the basic structure or framework of the Constitution through an amendment. This doctrine is the foundation of judicial review of constitutional amendments in India.

The doctrine of basic structure is not used to review ordinary laws. It applies only to constitutional amendment Acts passed under Art. 368. For ordinary laws, the standard grounds (Fundamental Rights, legislative competence, other constitutional provisions) apply.

[Additional] Elements of the basic structure identified across various judgments include: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, unity and sovereignty of India, individual freedom, equality, rule of law, judicial review, free and fair elections, limited amending power, independence of the judiciary, and harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

2. Judicial Activism

2.1 Meaning

Judicial activism [Not mentioned in the Constitution] describes a situation where the judiciary becomes assertive and proactive in forcing the executive and legislature to perform their constitutional functions effectively. While doing so, the judiciary may incidentally issue directions of executive or legislative nature — filling gaps left by the other two branches.

It is not merely the exercise of judicial review (which is constitutionally provided), but a more expansive use of judicial power to address social issues, protect rights, and ensure governance — sometimes going beyond traditional adjudication.

2.2 Origin and Development

  • As a concept, judicial activism originated in the United States. The term was coined by Arthur Schlesinger Jr. in a 1947 Fortune magazine article describing two approaches of US Supreme Court justices.
  • In India, judicial activism gained prominence in the late 1970s and 1980s, driven by the expansion of Fundamental Rights jurisprudence, the liberalisation of locus standi (standing), and the invention of Public Interest Litigation (PIL).
  • Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer are widely regarded as the pioneers of judicial activism in India. They relaxed procedural barriers, expanded Art. 21 to include a wide range of rights, and promoted PIL as a tool for the disadvantaged to access justice.
[Correction] The rough notes referred to "Justice JVK Krishna Iyer." The correct name is Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer (Vaidyanathapuram Rama Iyer).

2.3 How the Judiciary Performs Executive and Legislative Functions

Judiciary Acting as Legislature (Quasi-Legislative Directions)

[Additional] Need for judicial activism: Judicial activism often arises when (i) the legislature fails to make necessary laws on important matters of public interest, (ii) the executive fails to implement existing laws or constitutional mandates, (iii) Fundamental Rights are threatened and other branches are unresponsive, or (iv) there is a governance vacuum that affects vulnerable sections of society. The judiciary steps in as the last resort for constitutional protection.

2.4 Factors Leading to Judicial Activism

  1. Protection of Fundamental Rights: The judiciary becomes assertive when Fundamental Rights under Part III are threatened or violated and the executive or legislature is unwilling or unable to act.
  2. Executive inaction: When the executive fails to discharge its constitutional and statutory duties, the judiciary may issue mandamus or executive directions to compel performance (e.g., pollution control, implementation of welfare schemes).
  3. Legislative vacuum: When Parliament or State Legislatures fail to legislate on important matters of public interest, the judiciary may issue guidelines that function as interim law (e.g., Vishaka Guidelines on sexual harassment before the 2013 Act).
  4. Coalition politics and governance instability: In periods of coalition governments where political stability is prioritised over governance reforms, the judiciary may step in to protect public interest.
  5. PIL as a tool: The relaxation of locus standi through PIL allowed any public-spirited person to approach the court on behalf of disadvantaged sections, vastly expanding the scope of judicial intervention.
  6. Expansion of Article 21: The judicial interpretation of "life and personal liberty" under Art. 21 was expanded enormously (beginning with Maneka Gandhi, 1978) to include the right to livelihood, education, health, shelter, clean environment, privacy, dignity, and more — giving the judiciary a wide canvas for activism.
Note: Factors 1–4 and 6 can also be considered merits of judicial activism, as they represent situations where activism serves a positive constitutional purpose.

Additional factor:

  1. Personal convictions of judges: Sometimes judges may deliver judgments influenced by their own personal convictions, social philosophy or moral views rather than strictly established legal principles. While this can occasionally lead to progressive outcomes, it introduces unpredictability and subjectivity into the judicial process.

2.5 Issues and Demerits of Judicial Activism

  1. Violates separation of powers: In principle, judicial activism breaches the doctrine of separation of powers because the judiciary enters the domain of the executive or legislature. Art. 50 (DPSP) directs separation of judiciary from executive, but activism reverses this by making the judiciary perform executive or legislative functions.
  2. Lack of executive expertise: Judges are trained in legal interpretation, not in public administration, economic planning or policy implementation. When courts issue executive directions (e.g., on environmental standards, urban planning, or welfare delivery), they may lack the technical expertise and institutional capacity to design and monitor effective solutions.
  3. Democratic deficit in PIL-made law: Laws created through PIL-based directions are not preceded by the public deliberation, committee scrutiny and legislative debate that ordinarily underpin law-making in a democracy. They may lack the democratic legitimacy that comes from being enacted by elected representatives.
  4. Increased burden on higher judiciary: The proliferation of PILs and activist cases has significantly added to the pendency of cases in the Supreme Court and High Courts, contributing to delays in the disposal of regular cases involving ordinary litigants.
  5. Negative economic impact: Judicial directions on industrial policy, environmental regulation, resource allocation and commercial activity (e.g., mining bans, liquor bans, industrial closures) can have significant unintended economic consequences, including loss of employment and disruption of industries, without the cost-benefit analysis that normally accompanies executive or legislative policy-making.
  6. Inconsistency and unpredictability: Activist judgments may vary depending on the bench, creating doctrinal inconsistency. What one bench sees as activism, another may see as overreach.
  7. Accountability gap: Judges are not electorally accountable. While this independence is essential for impartial adjudication, it becomes problematic when the judiciary effectively makes policy, because there is no mechanism for the public to hold judges accountable for policy failures resulting from judicial directions.

3. Judicial Overreach

3.1 Meaning

Judicial overreach [Not mentioned in the Constitution] describes a situation where the judiciary clearly takes over functions of the executive or legislature in circumstances where the other branches should ideally have been allowed to function and the judiciary should have exercised restraint. The line between activism and overreach is narrow and often subjective — what one perspective considers legitimate activism, another may view as overreach.

Judicial Activism = judiciary proactively protecting rights and filling governance gaps within acceptable limits.
Judicial Overreach = judiciary crossing those limits and assuming functions that properly belong to other branches.

3.3 Activism vs Overreach — The Balance

Judicial activism, if used responsibly, can help protect the values of the Constitution and fulfil its goals — especially when other branches fail. However, activism beyond acceptable limits results in overreach, which can create a constitutional crisis by undermining the separation of powers and democratic governance.
Exam Point: The critical distinction is: activism is justified when other branches have failed and constitutional values are at stake; overreach is when the judiciary intrudes into executive or legislative territory despite those branches functioning or being available to act. Both terms are [Not mentioned in the Constitution] — they are academic and political concepts.

4. Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

4.1 What is PIL?

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) [Not mentioned in the Constitution] is a judicially innovated mechanism that allows any public-spirited person or organisation to approach the Supreme Court (Art. 32) or a High Court (Art. 226) for the enforcement of rights of a group or class of persons who are unable to approach the court themselves due to poverty, disability, ignorance or other disadvantage.

PIL was a radical departure from the traditional rule of locus standi (standing), which required that only a person whose own legal right was directly affected could approach the court. In PIL, the petitioner need not be the victim — any member of the public acting bona fide may file the petition.

4.2 Origin and Key Cases

CaseYearSignificance
Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdulbhai Faizullabhai1976Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer first articulated the concept of liberalised standing for public causes.
S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (First Judges Case)1981Justice P.N. Bhagwati stated: "Any member of the public having sufficient interest can maintain an action for judicial redress." This is considered a foundational PIL judgment.
Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, Bihar1979An early PIL where the SC took cognisance of under-trial prisoners languishing in Bihar jails for periods longer than the maximum sentence for the alleged offence. Led to the recognition of the right to speedy trial and the release of thousands of under-trials.
Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India1984PIL on bonded labour. SC held that even a letter or postcard addressed to the Court could be treated as a writ petition if it disclosed a violation of Fundamental Rights of disadvantaged persons.
M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak)1986PIL on industrial pollution. SC developed the doctrine of absolute liability (going beyond the English rule of Rylands v. Fletcher) for hazardous industries.
Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan1997PIL on sexual harassment at the workplace. SC issued binding guidelines that functioned as law until the 2013 Act.

4.3 Types of PIL

Suo motu (by the court itself)The court takes cognisance of a matter on its own, based on a news report, letter, or other information.SC taking cognisance of jail conditions, natural disasters, or child trafficking based on media reports.

4.4 Guidelines and Safeguards

To prevent misuse of PIL, the Supreme Court has laid down guidelines:

  • The petitioner must act bona fide (in good faith) and not for personal gain, publicity or to settle political scores.
  • PILs involving private disputes disguised as public interest are not maintainable.
  • Courts may impose costs on frivolous or vexatious PILs to deter misuse.
  • The court retains discretion to admit or refuse a PIL.

4.5 Issues and Criticism

  1. Misuse and frivolous PILs: The original purpose of PIL was to give voice to the voiceless. However, PILs are now filed for publicity, political motives, commercial interests, or to harass opponents. Some judges have described this as a "PIL industry."
  2. Overburdening of the judiciary: The flood of PILs adds significantly to the Supreme Court and High Court dockets, delaying regular cases.
  3. Democratic deficit: Directions issued through PILs effectively become law without parliamentary scrutiny or public debate.
  4. Inconsistency: Different benches may take different approaches to similar PIL issues, creating doctrinal uncertainty.
  5. Judicial governance: Continuing mandamus and monitoring orders in PIL cases (e.g., forest conservation, pollution) can turn courts into permanent administrative agencies, undermining executive responsibility.
[Additional] In State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010), the SC outlined detailed guidelines for filing PILs, requiring courts to verify the credentials and bona fides of petitioners and to discourage PILs motivated by personal interest.

5. Judiciary — Issues and Reforms

5.1 Key Issues

#IssueDetails
1Judicial vacanciesChronic vacancies in the Supreme Court, High Courts and subordinate courts. As of recent data, HCs operate at roughly 30–40% below sanctioned strength. Vacancies delay justice and increase pendency.

Frequent adjournments, lack of day-to-day hearings, and multiple appellate stages contribute to the 5+ crore pending cases across Indian courts.
9Needless litigation by legislationSometimes laws enacted by the legislature are poorly drafted, creating ambiguity and increasing litigation. Additionally, laws may impose requirements that generate unnecessary disputes.
10Lack of awareness of laws and ADRMany citizens, especially in rural areas, are unaware of their legal rights, available remedies, and Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) mechanisms like Lok Adalats, mediation and arbitration. This results in either no access to justice or the overburdening of regular courts for disputes that could be settled out of court.
11Poor accountability of higher judiciaryThe in-house mechanism for addressing complaints against HC and SC judges is opaque. The removal threshold (Art. 124(4) — proved misbehaviour or incapacity, requiring a parliamentary supermajority) is extremely high, leaving a gap for misconduct below the removal threshold. No SC or HC judge has ever been removed through the completed constitutional process.

5.2 Reforms Recommended or Proposed

  • National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC): Struck down in 2015, but the need for a reformed appointment mechanism that combines judicial independence with transparency and accountability persists.
  • All India Judicial Service (AIJS): Proposed under Art. 312 to recruit subordinate court judges through a centralised national examination, ensuring quality and uniformity. Opposed by several States and High Courts on grounds of federalism and local legal requirements.
  • National Tribunals Commission (NTC): Directed by the SC in Rojer Mathew (2019) to insulate tribunals from executive influence.
  • Increase sanctioned judicial strength: Filling existing vacancies promptly and increasing sanctioned posts at all levels.
  • Fast-track courts and special courts: For specific categories (e.g., commercial disputes, sexual offences, land disputes) to reduce burden on regular courts.
  • Greater use of ADR: Mandatory pre-litigation mediation (Mediation Act, 2023), active Lok Adalats, and institutional arbitration to divert cases from courts.
  • Technology: End-to-end digitisation, AI-assisted case management, live transcription, and video-conferencing for witness examination.
  • Judicial accountability: A transparent in-house complaints mechanism or a Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill (proposed but lapsed) to address misconduct below the removal threshold.
[Additional] The National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG) provides real-time data on pendency, disposal and institution of cases across all courts in India. It is a tool of the e-Courts project and is accessible at njdg.ecourts.gov.in.

6. Key Facts for Prelims

  • The phrase "judicial review" does not appear as a named power in any operative Article of the Constitution. It flows from Arts. 13, 32, 131, 132, 136, 226, 227 and others.
  • Art. 13 is the foundational Article for judicial review: laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are void.
  • In USA, judicial review was established in Marbury v. Madison (1803) — not expressly provided in the US Constitution.
  • India = Constitutional Supremacy; USA = Judicial Supremacy; UK = Parliamentary Sovereignty.
  • Basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) applies only to constitutional amendment Acts, not ordinary laws.
  • Burden of proof in challenging a law lies on the challenger; laws enjoy a presumption of constitutionality.
  • Judicial activism originated in USA; gained prominence in India in the late 1970s.
  • Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer are the pioneers of judicial activism and PIL in India.
  • PIL relaxed locus standi — any public-spirited person can approach the court on behalf of those unable to do so.
  • A letter or postcard can be treated as a writ petition: Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984).
  • Shyam Narayan Chouksey (2016) — national anthem in cinemas; modified in 2018.
  • India has ~21 judges per million population; recommended: 50 per million (245th Law Commission Report).
  • SC works approximately 190 days per year.
  • NJAC (99th Amendment) struck down in 2015; collegium restored.
  • No SC or HC judge has been removed through the completed Art. 124(4) process.

7. Mains Points

  • Judicial review is the Constitution's self-defence mechanism — it ensures that no law or executive action violates the supreme law. In India, it draws from express constitutional provisions, unlike the USA where it was judicially created.
  • India occupies a middle ground between American judicial supremacy and British parliamentary sovereignty. The basic structure doctrine ensures that even Parliament's constituent power has limits, but the judiciary itself is bound by the Constitution.
  • Judicial activism serves an important constitutional purpose when other branches fail, but it risks undermining separation of powers, democratic legitimacy and executive accountability when taken too far.
  • PIL has been transformative in expanding access to justice and protecting the rights of marginalised communities, but its misuse threatens to overburden courts and bypass democratic deliberation.
  • Judicial overreach is a governance risk: the judiciary lacks the institutional capacity, technical expertise and democratic accountability to substitute for executive and legislative functions on a sustained basis.
  • Judicial reforms require a multi-pronged approach: filling vacancies, increasing judges, adopting technology, promoting ADR, reforming procedural laws, improving infrastructure, ensuring judicial accountability, and securing transparent appointments.

Q1. UPSC CSE Mains 2017, General Studies Paper II

"The Supreme Court of India keeps a check on the arbitrary power of the legislature and executive through judicial review." Discuss.

Answer framework: Define judicial review; constitutional basis (Arts. 13, 32, 131-136, 226). Discuss how SC reviews laws for FR violation (Art. 13), legislative competence (7th Schedule), constitutional compliance, and amendments for basic structure. Use examples: striking down Sec. 66A IT Act (FR violation), NJAC (basic structure), Maneka Gandhi (expanding Art. 21). Discuss limitations: presumption of constitutionality, self-restraint, political question doctrine. Conclude with India's balance between judicial and parliamentary supremacy.

Q2. UPSC CSE Mains 2019, General Studies Paper II

"Do you think that the Constitution of India does not accept the principle of strict separation of powers rather it is based on the principle of 'checks and balances'? Explain."

Answer framework: Directly relevant because judicial review, judicial activism, and PIL are key examples of checks and balances. Use Art. 142 (judiciary exercising quasi-legislative functions), Art. 143 (executive seeking judicial opinion), Art. 123 (executive legislating through ordinances), collegium (judiciary controlling appointments), removal of judges (Parliament's role). Judicial activism examples show judiciary performing executive/legislative functions. Kesavananda Bharati: separation of powers is basic structure but not absolute.

Q3. UPSC CSE Mains 2020, General Studies Paper II

"Judicial legislation is antithetical to the doctrine of separation of powers as envisaged in the Indian Constitution." Discuss.

Answer framework: Define judicial legislation through PIL-made law (Vishaka Guidelines), highway liquor ban, CNG conversion orders. Argue it violates separation of powers (Art. 50, implicit doctrine). Counter-argue: judicial legislation fills legislative vacuum, protects Fundamental Rights, and is justified under Art. 142 (complete justice). Discuss limits: Prem Chand Garg (1963) — Art. 142 cannot take away FRs; SC Bar Association v. UoI (1998) — supplement, not contradict law. Conclude with balance: judicial creativity is needed but must respect institutional boundaries.

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019, General Studies Paper I

In India, the separation of judiciary from the executive is enjoined by

(a) the Preamble of the Constitution
(b) a Directive Principle of State Policy
(c) the Seventh Schedule
(d) the conventional practice

Answer: (b) a Directive Principle of State Policy.
Explanation: Article 50, a DPSP in Part IV, directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive. This is directly relevant to the discussion of judicial activism and overreach, where the judiciary enters the executive domain.

"Constitutionally guaranteed judicial independence is a prerequisite of democracy. Comment."

Answer framework: Directly relevant to today's discussion of judicial accountability vs independence. Link judicial independence to rule of law, Fundamental Rights, and the power of judicial review.
