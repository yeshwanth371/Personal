Topic: Foundational Concepts — Law, State, Constitution, Constitutionalism, Separation of Powers, Forms of Government, and Federalism

1. Law

What is Law?

Law is a system of rules and regulations recognised, created, and enforced by the State to regulate human conduct within a politically organised society. Unlike mere customs or moral codes, law carries the coercive authority of the State — non-compliance invites penalties enforced through the machinery of government (police, courts, prisons).

Law = Rule of conduct + Recognition by the State + Enforcement through State authority

Key thinkers:

  • John Austin (Positivist school): "Law is the command of the sovereign, backed by sanction." Law is what the sovereign commands; disobedience attracts punishment.
  • Salmond: "Law is the body of principles recognised and applied by the State in the administration of justice."
  • Natural Law school: Law must conform to higher moral principles — unjust law is not truly law.

Functions of Law in a Political System

  • Maintains social order and stability — prevents the Hobbesian "state of nature" where life is "nasty, brutish, and short."
  • Regulates relations between individuals, groups, institutions, and the State.
  • Protects rights and freedoms — guarantees individual autonomy against encroachment.
  • Prevents arbitrary use of power — constrains those who wield political authority.
  • Provides dispute resolution mechanisms — courts, tribunals, arbitration.
  • Promotes social justice and public welfare — redistributive and welfare legislation.
  • Facilitates social change — abolition of untouchability, land reforms, Right to Education.

2. State

In political science, the State is the most fundamental unit of political organisation in the modern world. It is not the same as "government" or "country" — it is a broader concept encompassing all politically organised life within a territorial unit.

Definition

A State is a politically organised community occupying a definite territory, having an organised government, and possessing sovereignty (supreme and independent authority).

According to Garner: "The State is a community of persons, more or less numerous, permanently occupying a definite portion of territory, independent of external control, and possessing an organised government to which the great body of inhabitants render habitual obedience."

Four Essential Elements of the State

  1. Population — Without people, there is no State. There is no prescribed minimum or maximum (e.g., India: 1.4 billion; Vatican City: ~800). The population must have a sense of political community.
  2. Defined Territory — A State must occupy a specific geographical area with recognised boundaries. Size is irrelevant (Russia vs. Maldives). Disputed borders do not negate statehood (e.g., India-Pakistan on Kashmir).
  3. Government — The institutional machinery through which the will of the State is expressed and enforced. Form of government (democratic, monarchic, authoritarian) is irrelevant to statehood.
  4. Sovereignty — The supreme and absolute authority of the State:
    • Internal sovereignty: Supreme authority over all persons and associations within its territory.
    • External sovereignty: Independence from foreign control; recognised equality among States in international law.
State = Population + Territory + Government + Sovereignty
Note: Without any one of these four elements, a political entity cannot be called a State in the strict political science sense. For example, Palestine has population, territory, and government, but its sovereignty is contested — hence its statehood remains disputed internationally.

Example: India as a State

  • Population → Over 1.4 billion people of diverse ethnic, linguistic, and religious backgrounds
  • Territory → 3.28 million sq. km. comprising 28 States and 8 Union Territories
  • Government → Parliamentary democratic republic with Union and State governments
  • Sovereignty → Supreme internal authority (Constitution is supreme); externally independent since 15 August 1947

Key insight: India (the State) has existed continuously since 1947, even though dozens of different governments have held power at the Centre. The State is permanent; governments are temporary agents.

3. State vs Country vs Nation

These terms are used interchangeably in common parlance but have distinct political science meanings.

Country

A geographical and cultural expression — refers primarily to a territory and the people historically associated with it. It carries emotional and cultural connotations (homeland, motherland). It is not a legal/political concept in the strict sense.

State

A political and legal concept — refers to a sovereign political entity possessing the four elements (population, territory, government, sovereignty). It is defined in terms of law and political authority.

Nation

A cultural and psychological concept — a large group of people united by common language, culture, history, ethnicity, or shared consciousness. A nation may or may not have a State (e.g., the Kurdish nation spans multiple States but has no State of its own).

Critical Distinctions

ConceptBasisExample
CountryGeographical/cultural"India is a beautiful country" — refers to the land, culture, people
StatePolitical/legal (sovereignty)"India is a sovereign State" — refers to political authority
NationCultural/psychological (shared identity)"Indian nation" — refers to shared civilisational consciousness
GovernmentInstitutional/administrative"The Government of India" — refers to those currently in power
UPSC Important: Nation ≠ State ≠ Government ≠ Country. A Nation-State is one where national (cultural) and State (political) boundaries coincide — e.g., Japan, Iceland. India is a multi-national State (many nations/communities within one State).

4. Constitution

Definition

A Constitution is the fundamental law of a political system — the supreme legal document (or body of rules) that establishes the framework of government, defines and limits governmental powers, distributes authority among institutions, and guarantees the rights of citizens.

According to Bryce: "Constitution is the aggregate of laws and customs under which the life of the State goes on."

According to K.C. Wheare: "Constitution is the whole system of government of a country — the collection of rules which establish and regulate the government."

What does a Constitution do?

It answers the most fundamental political questions:

  1. Who governs? — Identifies the organs of government and how they are constituted.
  2. What powers do they have? — Defines the extent and limits of governmental authority.
  3. How are powers exercised? — Establishes procedures, processes, and accountability mechanisms.
  4. What are the limits? — Prevents abuse through fundamental rights, judicial review, and checks.
  5. What rights do citizens possess? — Guarantees justiciable and non-justiciable rights.
  6. How is power distributed? — Between organs (separation of powers) and levels (federalism).
  7. How can the system be changed? — Provides amendment procedures for orderly constitutional evolution.

Why do we need a Constitution?

A Constitution serves as the social contract between the governed and those who govern. It is necessary because:

  • In any society, there must be agreement on basic rules before collective political life can function.
  • Power without limits tends towards tyranny — the Constitution is the primary restraint.
  • In diverse societies like India, the Constitution provides the framework for coexistence of different communities, languages, religions, and regions.
  • It provides legitimacy to the exercise of governmental power — authority flows from the Constitution, not from the barrel of a gun.

5. Functions of the Constitution

1. Provides Minimum Coordination

Any political society consists of diverse individuals, groups, classes, communities, regions, and interests — often with conflicting goals. The Constitution provides the minimum necessary framework within which these diverse elements can coexist, cooperate, and resolve differences peacefully.

Without a Constitution, there would be no agreed-upon rules for political life — leading to chaos, conflict, or rule by force.

Constitution → The basic "rules of the game" for political life

2. Provides Political Identity

The Constitution gives citizens a shared political and civic identity that transcends their particular ethnic, linguistic, religious, or regional identities.

The Preamble of the Indian Constitution establishes India's identity as a:

Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic

This constitutional identity is the common thread binding 1.4 billion diverse people into one political community. It creates a sense of constitutional patriotism — loyalty not just to the land, but to the values and principles enshrined in the Constitution.

3. Establishes and Organises Government Institutions

The Constitution creates the institutional architecture of governance:

  • Legislature: Parliament (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha), State Legislatures
  • Executive: President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers, Governor, State CM
  • Judiciary: Supreme Court, High Courts, Subordinate Courts
  • Constitutional bodies: Election Commission, CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission, etc.

It defines their composition, qualifications, tenure, powers, functions, and inter-relationships.

4. Limits Government Power (Constitutionalism)

The Constitution prevents government from becoming totalitarian by imposing multiple restraints:

  • Fundamental Rights (Part III) — enforceable limits on State action
  • Judicial Review (Art. 13, 32, 226) — courts can invalidate unconstitutional laws/actions
  • Separation of Powers — no single institution has unlimited authority
  • Federalism — power divided between Union and States
  • Rule of Law — government itself must act according to law

5. Distributes and Allocates Powers

The Constitution allocates power in two dimensions:

DimensionNatureConstitutional Mechanism
HorizontalAmong organs at the same levelLegislature, Executive, Judiciary (Separation of Powers)
VerticalBetween levels of governmentUnion, States, Local Bodies (Federalism — 7th Schedule)

6. Protects Rights and Liberty

The Constitution guarantees individual liberty against arbitrary State action. In India, Fundamental Rights (Articles 12–35) are justiciable and enforceable through the Supreme Court (Art. 32) and High Courts (Art. 226). The State cannot violate these rights except through procedures established by law that satisfy constitutional standards.

7. Provides Mechanisms for Peaceful Political Change

Without a Constitution, political change often comes through violence, revolution, or coup. The Constitution channels political change through legitimate, peaceful mechanisms:

  • Regular elections (Art. 324–329)
  • No-confidence motions (removal of government)
  • Constitutional amendments (Art. 368)
  • Impeachment/removal procedures for constitutional functionaries
  • Judicial remedies for redress of grievances

6. Types of Constitution

Constitutions are classified on two independent axes:

  1. Written vs Unwritten (Uncodified) — based on the form in which constitutional rules exist
  2. Rigid vs Flexible — based on the difficulty of the amendment procedure
Critical UPSC Point: These are two SEPARATE classifications. Do NOT assume Written = Rigid or Unwritten = Flexible. A written Constitution can be flexible (e.g., Indian Constitution for many provisions) and an unwritten system can develop rigid conventions.

7. Written Constitution

A written Constitution is one in which the fundamental constitutional rules are deliberately formulated and formally documented in a single instrument or a set of documents adopted at a particular time through a conscious, deliberate process.

Characteristics

  • Product of a deliberate act of constitution-making — usually by a Constituent Assembly at a definite point in time.
  • Constitutional rules are formally documented and codified in a single instrument.
  • The constitutional document has special legal status — it is the supreme law.
  • Amendment usually requires a special procedure different from ordinary legislation.
  • Provides relative clarity and certainty about what the constitutional rules are.

Examples

  • India — Constitution adopted on 26 November 1949 by the Constituent Assembly; came into force on 26 January 1950. Longest written Constitution in the world (395 Articles, 12 Schedules in the original; now 470+ Articles, 25 Parts).
  • USA — Constitution adopted in 1787 by the Philadelphia Convention. Shortest written Constitution of any major country (7 Articles + 27 Amendments).
  • France, Germany, Japan, South Africa — all have written Constitutions.
Important nuance: Even a "written" Constitution is supplemented by judicial interpretations, conventions, and practices that are not found in the text itself. For example, the office of Leader of Opposition, collective responsibility of the Cabinet, and the role of the Speaker as neutral presiding officer are all conventions supplementing the Indian Constitution's text.

8. Unwritten / Uncodified Constitution

The term "unwritten Constitution" is technically misleading. It does not mean that constitutional rules are nowhere written down. Rather, it means that constitutional rules are not codified in a single formal document adopted at a particular time.

The better term is Uncodified Constitution — constitutional rules exist in scattered sources.

Sources of an Uncodified Constitution

  • Statutes of constitutional significance — e.g., Magna Carta (1215), Bill of Rights (1689), Parliament Acts (1911, 1949), Human Rights Act (1998)
  • Judicial decisions/precedents — court rulings establishing constitutional principles
  • Constitutional conventions — unwritten rules of political practice (e.g., the PM must command majority in the Commons)
  • Historical documents and treaties
  • Works of constitutional scholars — e.g., Dicey, Bagehot

Example: United Kingdom

The UK has no single document called "The Constitution." Its constitutional system has evolved over centuries through statutes, judicial decisions, conventions, and royal prerogatives. Parliament is sovereign — there is no formal hierarchy between "constitutional law" and "ordinary law" as in India.

Other examples: New Zealand, Israel (partially).

9. Rigid Constitution

A Constitution is rigid when its amendment requires a special procedure that is more difficult than ordinary legislation. The constitutional law is placed on a higher plane than ordinary statutory law — it cannot be changed as easily.

Purpose of Rigidity

  • Protects fundamental constitutional values from temporary majorities.
  • Ensures stability and continuity of the constitutional framework.
  • Prevents hasty or partisan amendments.
  • Maintains the supremacy of the Constitution over ordinary legislation.

Example: USA

Amendment requires: (a) proposal by 2/3 majority of both Houses of Congress OR by a Convention called by 2/3 of State legislatures; AND (b) ratification by 3/4 of State legislatures. Result: Only 27 amendments in 237+ years.

India: A Mixed Case

The Indian Constitution is neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible:

  • Some provisions can be amended by simple majority of Parliament (not even under Art. 368) — e.g., creation of new States, citizenship laws.
  • Most provisions require special majority (2/3 of members present and voting + majority of total membership) under Art. 368.
  • Some provisions additionally require ratification by at least half the State legislatures — e.g., election of President, distribution of powers between Union and States.

10. Flexible Constitution

A Constitution is flexible when constitutional rules can be changed through the same procedure as ordinary legislation. There is no legal distinction between constitutional law and ordinary law in terms of the process of change.

Example: United Kingdom

Since Parliament is sovereign and there is no codified supreme Constitution, any Act of Parliament can alter constitutional arrangements. The Parliament Acts, the Human Rights Act, or the Fixed-term Parliaments Act were all passed and can be repealed through the same ordinary legislative process.

Advantages and Disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Adapts easily to changing circumstancesLess stable — fundamental rules can be changed on a whim
Avoids constitutional deadlocksRights of minorities/citizens less protected against temporary majorities
Reflects popular will more directlyMay lack the certainty and supremacy that a rigid Constitution provides
Do NOT equate:
Written = Rigid ✗
Unwritten = Flexible ✗
These are two independent classifications. India has a written but partly flexible Constitution. The UK has an unwritten but relatively stable constitutional tradition.

11. Constitutionalism

Having a Constitution is not the same as having constitutionalism. Many authoritarian regimes have elaborate written Constitutions that they routinely violate. Constitutionalism is a political philosophy and practice — it is about whether governmental power is actually limited and accountable in practice.

Constitution vs Constitutionalism

BasisConstitutionConstitutionalism
NatureA document or body of rulesA political philosophy and practice
FocusDescribes the structure, institutions, and procedures of governmentInsists that governmental power must be limited, accountable, and exercised according to law
CharacterPrimarily descriptive — what institutions exist, what powers they havePrimarily normative/prescriptive — how power ought to be exercised
RelationshipThe means/instrumentThe end/goal — limited government, rule of law, protection of liberty
ExampleThe USSR had a Constitution guaranteeing extensive rightsBut it lacked constitutionalism — the Communist Party exercised unlimited power
Constitution = Framework/Document
Constitutionalism = The practice of limited, accountable, rule-bound government
Key insight for UPSC: India has both a Constitution AND constitutionalism. China has a Constitution but limited constitutionalism. The UK has constitutionalism but no single codified Constitution. North Korea has a Constitution but no constitutionalism at all.

Core Elements of Constitutionalism

  • Government power is derived from and limited by the Constitution.
  • No individual or institution is above the law.
  • Power is distributed — not concentrated in one person or institution.
  • Citizens have enforceable rights against the government.
  • There exist independent mechanisms to check governmental excess (independent judiciary, free press, civil society).
  • Political change occurs through legitimate constitutional processes, not through force.

12. Essential Features of Constitutionalism

1. Rule of Law

The Rule of Law means the supremacy of law over arbitrary power. No person — whether the President, Prime Minister, or ordinary citizen — is above the law. Governmental power must operate within legal limits.

A.V. Dicey's formulation (three principles):

  1. Supremacy of law — No person can be punished except for a breach of law established before an ordinary court. Arbitrary punishment is prohibited.
  2. Equality before the law — All persons, regardless of rank or status, are subject to the same law administered by the same courts.
  3. Constitution as a result of judicial decisions — Constitutional law is not the source but the consequence of the rights of individuals as enforced by courts (specific to the UK).

Latin Expression

Lex Rex → "The Law is King" (as opposed to Rex Lex — "The King is Law")

This encapsulates the shift from absolutism to constitutionalism — even the ruler is subject to law.

Rule of Law vs Rule by Law

Rule of LawRule by Law
Government itself is subject to and limited by lawGovernment uses law as a tool/instrument to govern — but is not itself bound
Law serves as a check on governmental powerLaw serves as a weapon of governmental control over citizens
Citizens can challenge government actions in independent courtsCourts are subservient to government; no meaningful judicial independence
Example: India, UK, USAExample: Authoritarian regimes that pass oppressive laws and use them against citizens while remaining above accountability

2. Separation of Powers

Governmental power should not be concentrated in one institution or person. The three primary functions — law-making, law-implementation, and law-interpretation — should be distributed among different organs to prevent tyranny.

3. Independent Judiciary

An independent judiciary is the guardian of the Constitution and the sentinel of liberty. It must be free from executive or legislative pressure to:

  • Protect citizens' fundamental rights against governmental violations.
  • Exercise judicial review — strike down unconstitutional laws and executive actions.
  • Interpret the Constitution authoritatively.
  • Adjudicate federal disputes between Union and States.
  • Act as a check on legislative and executive overreach.

4. Free and Independent Media

Often called the "Fourth Estate" — the media acts as a watchdog on governmental power by:

  • Informing citizens about government actions and policies.
  • Exposing corruption, abuse of power, and maladministration.
  • Facilitating informed public debate and democratic participation.
  • Holding government accountable between elections.

Freedom of press in India is derived from Article 19(1)(a) — Freedom of speech and expression (Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras, 1950).

5. Civilian Control over Defence Services

The military must remain subordinate to elected civilian authority. In a constitutional democracy, the armed forces serve the Constitution and the elected government — they do not become an independent political force or decide who governs.

Military → Serves the constitutional civilian government, not the other way around

This distinguishes democracies from military dictatorships (e.g., Pakistan's repeated military coups vs. India's consistent civilian control).

13. Separation of Powers

Meaning and Rationale

The doctrine of Separation of Powers holds that the three primary functions of government — legislative, executive, and judicial — should be performed by separate and distinct organs, so that no single institution possesses unlimited authority. The concentration of all three powers in the same hands is the very definition of tyranny.

The Three Organs and Their Functions

ExecutiveImplements and administers laws; formulates policy; runs the daily governancePresident, PM, Council of Ministers, bureaucracyGovernor, CM, Council of Ministers, state bureaucracy
JudiciaryInterprets laws; adjudicates disputes; protects rights; exercises judicial reviewSupreme CourtHigh Courts and subordinate courts

14. Montesquieu and Separation of Powers

Although the idea of distributing governmental power has roots in Aristotle and Polybius, its modern systematic formulation is primarily associated with Baron de Montesquieu (1689–1755), the French political philosopher.

In his seminal work The Spirit of the Laws (1748), Montesquieu argued:

"When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty... Again, there is no liberty if the judicial power be not separated from the legislative and executive."

Montesquieu's Core Argument

  1. If one person or body makes laws AND enforces them → tyranny (no check on arbitrary rule-making)
  2. If one person or body enforces laws AND judges disputes → oppression (executive becomes judge in its own cause)
  3. If one person or body makes laws AND judges disputes → citizens' liberty and property are at the mercy of one authority

Main Objective

Prevent concentration of power → Prevent arbitrariness → Protect individual liberty
Context: Montesquieu was writing in the context of French absolutism (Louis XIV — "L'état, c'est moi" / "I am the State"). He used the English system as his model of separated powers — though his understanding of the English system was somewhat idealised.

15. Benefits of Separation of Powers

  1. Checks and Balances — Each organ can restrain the others from exceeding their constitutional limits. No organ is omnipotent.
  2. Prevents Arbitrary Government — When no single institution has unlimited power, arbitrary rule becomes structurally difficult.
  3. Protects Individual Liberty — Citizens are protected because there are multiple institutional safeguards against abuse.
  4. Promotes Constitutionalism — Government is compelled to operate within constitutional limits because other organs can hold it accountable.
  5. Functional Specialisation and Efficiency — Each organ develops expertise in its sphere — legislators in deliberation and law-making, executives in administration, judges in legal interpretation.
  6. Ensures Accountability — Legislature scrutinises the executive; judiciary reviews both; executive implements judicial orders.

16. Separation of Powers is NOT Absolute

In practice, no modern democratic system follows a complete or watertight separation of powers. Absolute separation would make government dysfunctional — the organs must interact, cooperate, and occasionally overlap to function effectively.

Modern constitutional systems use:

Functional Separation + Institutional Overlap + Checks and Balances

Each organ has a primary function but also plays a role in the functioning of other organs — this creates a system of mutual accountability.

Separation of Powers ≠ Complete Institutional Isolation
Separation of Powers = Distribution of primary functions + mutual checks

17–18. Separation of Powers in the USA

Structure

The USA has a comparatively rigid separation of powers. The first three Articles of the US Constitution allocate powers distinctly:

  • Article I → All legislative powers vested in Congress
  • Article II → All executive power vested in the President
  • Article III → Judicial power vested in the Supreme Court and inferior courts

Since the USA follows a Presidential system, the President is independently elected and is NOT a member of Congress. Cabinet members (Secretaries) are also not members of Congress. This creates institutional separation between legislature and executive.

Exceptions and Overlaps (Checks and Balances)

Congress → PresidentCan impeach and remove the President; controls the budget; Senate confirms appointments and ratifies treaties
President → JudiciaryNominates federal judges including Supreme Court justices
Congress → JudiciarySenate confirms judicial appointments; Congress can impeach judges; can propose constitutional amendments to override judicial decisions
Judiciary → BothJudicial review — can declare laws of Congress or executive actions unconstitutional (Marbury v. Madison, 1803)
USA = Stronger/More Rigid Separation + Robust Checks and Balances

19–22. Separation of Powers in India

India's Approach

India follows a Parliamentary system, which inherently involves a fusion/overlap between the executive and legislature. The Indian Constitution does not expressly provide for separation of powers in terms as clear as the US Constitution, but the doctrine is implicit in its scheme.

Key Feature: Executive Drawn from Legislature

  • The Prime Minister must be (or within 6 months must become) a member of Parliament.
  • Ministers must be members of either House of Parliament.
  • The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75).

This means the executive and legislative functions are not institutionally separated as in the USA — they are intertwined.

India = Functional Separation + Considerable Overlap + Institutional Checks and Balances

Supreme Court on Separation of Powers

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): The Supreme Court held that separation of powers is part of the "basic structure" of the Constitution and cannot be destroyed even by constitutional amendment.

One-line UPSC Statement

India does not follow a rigid or absolute separation of powers; rather, it follows a system of functional separation with significant institutional overlap and robust checks and balances. The doctrine is implicit in the constitutional scheme and forms part of its basic structure.

29–37. Federalism

Meaning

Federalism is a constitutional arrangement in which governmental power is vertically distributed between two or more levels of government — each level deriving its authority directly from the Constitution, not from the other level.

Federalism = Constitutional division of power between Union/Centre + States/Regions

Unlike a unitary system (where all power flows from the centre and can be taken back), in a federal system both levels have constitutionally guaranteed spheres of authority.

Essential Features of a Federal System

FeatureExplanationIndian Provision
Two levels of governmentUnion + States (+ local bodies as third tier)Arts. 1, 152, 245
Constitutional division of powersEach level has constitutionally defined sphere7th Schedule — Union List, State List, Concurrent List
Supremacy of the ConstitutionBoth levels are subordinate to the ConstitutionArt. 13, Preamble
Written ConstitutionClear documentation of power distributionLongest written Constitution
Rigidity (for federal provisions)Amendment of federal structure requires special procedureArt. 368 — some amendments need State ratification
Independent JudiciaryArbiter of federal disputesArts. 124–147 (SC), 214–231 (HC)

Why Federalism? (Rationale)

  • Accommodates diversity — linguistic, ethnic, cultural, religious, regional differences can be managed through autonomous sub-units.
  • Prevents concentration of power — vertical distribution adds another layer of checks against central tyranny.
  • Brings government closer to the people — local/regional governments are more responsive to local needs.
  • Maintains unity in diversity — allows regions to retain identity while being part of a larger whole.
  • Enables policy experimentation — states can be "laboratories of democracy" (Justice Brandeis).

Indian Federalism — A Unique Case

Article 1: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."

Note: The Constitution uses "Union" (not "Federation") — deliberate choice by Dr. Ambedkar to indicate that:

  1. The Union is not the result of an agreement/compact between States.
  2. No State has the right to secede.
  3. The Union is indestructible.

India is often described as:

"Quasi-Federal" (K.C. Wheare) [Academic term]
"Federal in structure but unitary in spirit" (Ivor Jennings) [Academic term]
"Federation with a strong centralising tendency" (D.D. Basu) [Academic term]
"Cooperative federalism" (Granville Austin) [Academic term]

Federal Features of India

  • Written and supreme Constitution
  • Constitutional division of powers (7th Schedule — 3 Lists)
  • Independent judiciary with power of judicial review
  • Bicameral Parliament (Rajya Sabha represents States)
  • Rigidity for federal provisions (State ratification needed)
  • Two levels of government each with constitutional authority

Unitary/Centralising Features of India

  • Strong Centre — Union List is longer; residuary powers with Union (Art. 248)
  • Single citizenship — no separate state citizenship
  • Integrated judiciary — single hierarchy from SC to subordinate courts
  • All India Services — IAS, IPS, IFS serve both Union and States
  • Emergency provisions — Centre can override States completely during emergency (Arts. 352, 356, 360)
  • Governor — appointed by the President (Centre's agent in the State)
  • Parliament can alter State boundaries — States have no veto (Art. 3)
  • Parliament can legislate on State List matters — under certain conditions (Arts. 249, 250, 252, 253)
  • Single Constitution for Union and States
  • Financial dependence — States depend heavily on central transfers

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020

Which of the following are the discretionary powers given to the Governor by the Constitution of India?

  1. Sending a report to the President of India for imposing the President's rule.
  2. Appointing the Ministers.
  3. Reserving certain bills passed by the State Legislature for consideration of the President of India.
  4. Making the rules to conduct the business of the State Government.

(a) 1 and 2 only   (b) 1 and 3 only   (c) 2, 3 and 4 only   (d) 1, 2, 3 and 4

Answer: (b)
Explanation: The Governor exercises discretion in sending a report under Article 356 and reserving Bills under Article 200. Appointing Ministers is done on the CM's advice (Art. 164). Rules of business are made under Art. 166 on the CM's advice.

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2015

There is a Parliamentary System of Government in India because:

(a) The Lok Sabha is elected directly by the people
(b) The Parliament can amend the Constitution
(c) The Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved
(d) The Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha

Answer: (d)
Explanation: The defining feature of a parliamentary system is collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the lower House — Article 75(3). This distinguishes it from a presidential system where the executive is not removable by the legislature through a no-confidence motion.

Q3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019

Consider the following statements:

  1. The 44th Amendment to the Constitution of India introduced an Article which empowered the President to send back once the advice of the Council of Ministers for reconsideration.
  2. The Supreme Court of India struck down the 99th Amendment to the Constitution of India as unconstitutional.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only   (b) 2 only   (c) Both 1 and 2   (d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (c)
Explanation: The 44th Amendment inserted Article 74(1) proviso allowing the President to return advice once for reconsideration. The 99th Amendment (NJAC) was struck down in Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015) as violating the basic structure (judicial independence).

Q4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013

The Parliament can make any law for whole or any part of India for implementing international treaties:

(a) with the consent of all the States
(b) with the consent of the majority of States
(c) with the consent of the States concerned
(d) without the consent of any State

Answer: (d)
Explanation: Under Article 253, Parliament can legislate on any matter — including State List subjects — to implement treaties, agreements or conventions, without requiring consent of any State. This is a strong centralising/unitary feature of Indian federalism.

Q5. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Parliament of India can place a particular law in the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution of India.
  2. The validity of a law placed in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court and no judgement can be made on it.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only   (b) 2 only   (c) Both 1 and 2   (d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (a)
Explanation: Statement 1 is correct — Parliament can add laws to the Ninth Schedule by constitutional amendment. Statement 2 is wrong — after I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be judicially reviewed if they violate the basic structure.

Q6. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014

Consider the following statements regarding the Preamble to the Constitution of India:

  1. The Preamble is not a part of the Constitution.
  2. The Preamble can neither be amended nor repealed.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only   (b) 2 only   (c) Both 1 and 2   (d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (d)
Explanation: Both are wrong. The Preamble is a part of the Constitution — Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) overruled the Berubari Union (1960) advisory opinion. The Preamble can be amended under Article 368, as demonstrated by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 which added "Socialist," "Secular" and "integrity." 42. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Law is a system of rules recognised, created and enforced by the State — backed by coercive authority.
  • Four essential elements of a State: Population, Territory, Government, Sovereignty.
  • Constitution = supreme fundamental law; all other laws must conform to it (Art. 13).
  • Constitutionalism = limited, accountable, rule-bound government — not the same as having a Constitution.
  • Rule of Law (A.V. Dicey): equality before law, supremacy of law, no arbitrary punishment.
  • Separation of Powers is not explicitly stated in the Indian Constitution but is recognised as part of the basic structure — Kesavananda Bharati (1973).
  • India: Parliamentary system — Council of Ministers collectively responsible to Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)).
  • USA: Presidential system — President independently elected with fixed 4-year tenure.
  • Article 1: India = "Union of States." Word "federal" is not used in the Constitution.
  • K.C. Wheare called India "quasi-federal" — an academic label, not constitutional language.
  • Residuary legislative powers with Parliament: Art. 248 (India) vs States: 10th Amendment (USA).
  • Governor appointed by President (Arts. 155–156); States have no veto over boundary changes (Art. 3).

43. Mains Points

  • The State is distinguished from government, country and nation — each has a different conceptual basis.
  • Constitutionalism goes beyond having a written Constitution; it demands substantive limits on power through rights, judicial review and rule of law.
  • India's separation of powers is functional rather than rigid — executive is drawn from and accountable to the legislature, but the judiciary is structurally independent.
  • Indian federalism is unique: a holding-together model with a strong Centre, unlike the coming-together model of the USA.
  • The quasi-federal character allows national unity to coexist with regional diversity, but creates tensions around Centre–State financial and legislative relations.

44. Common Traps / Confusions

TrapCorrect Position
India has absolute separation of powers like the USA.No. India has functional separation with considerable overlap; the executive is part of the legislature in a parliamentary system.
"Quasi-federal" is a constitutional description.No. It is K.C. Wheare's academic characterisation. Article 1 says "Union of States."
"Dual Executive" is mentioned in the Constitution.No. The Constitution describes presidential and ministerial powers separately (Arts. 53, 74, 75); "dual executive" is an academic label.
State = Government.No. The State is permanent (population + territory + government + sovereignty). Government is only the institutional agency that exercises State power — temporary and changeable.
India follows the coming-together model of federalism.No. India follows the holding-together model — power was distributed from an existing unified territory, not by independent entities joining together.
Separation of Powers is explicitly stated in the Indian Constitution.No. It is implicit in the constitutional scheme and recognised as part of the basic structure.
