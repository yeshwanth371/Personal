Indian Polity - Class Notes | 16 September 2026

Topics: President's judicial, financial, military, diplomatic and emergency powers; ordinances; vetoes; mercy powers and Supreme Court rulings.

Labels used: [Correction] explains a substantive correction. [Additional] marks relevant supplementary material. [Verification Required] flags an unresolved claim. [Not mentioned in the Constitution] distinguishes textbook classifications from constitutional wording.

1. Judicial Powers and Functions

  • The President formally appoints Supreme Court Judges under Article 124(2) and High Court Judges under Article 217(1), subject to the applicable constitutional and judicially settled appointment procedure.
  • The President may seek the Supreme Court's advisory opinion under Article 143.
  • The President exercises clemency powers under Article 72, explained in section 8.
[Correction] "Appoints and removes judges" does not mean removal at presidential pleasure. Under Article 124(4), removal requires an address by each House in the same session, supported by a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting, on proved misbehaviour or incapacity, followed by the President's order. The procedure applies to High Court Judges through Articles 217(1)(b) and 218.
[Additional] Under 143(1), a question of law or fact of public importance may be referred, and the Court may report its opinion. Under 143(2), the specified disputes excluded by the proviso to Article 131 may be referred, and the Court shall report its opinion. An advisory reference is not an appeal against a judgment.

3. Military and Diplomatic Powers

3.1 Military Powers

Article 53(2): supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union is vested in the President, and its exercise is regulated by law. The President is the formal supreme commander and formally appoints the Service Chiefs, acting through the constitutional government machinery.

[Correction] "The President declares war and concludes peace subject to Parliament's approval" is textbook shorthand. Decisions are taken through the Union executive on ministerial advice, subject to law, parliamentary financial control and political accountability. Article 53(2) does not prescribe a separate parliamentary approval resolution for every declaration of war or peace. Do not confuse such decisions with approval of an Article 352 proclamation.

3.2 Diplomatic Powers

The President represents India as head of State, accredits Indian diplomatic representatives abroad and receives foreign diplomatic representatives. Treaty-making and international relations are conducted by the Union executive.

[Correction] "All treaties and agreements are signed in the President's name" should not be read as a requirement that the President personally signs every instrument. Authorised representatives conclude international agreements for India or its government. Treaty-making must be distinguished from changing domestic law to implement a treaty.

[Additional] Article 253 empowers Parliament to legislate for implementing treaties, international agreements and relevant international decisions, including on matters otherwise in the State List. A treaty alone cannot be used to override contrary domestic legislation; necessary domestic legal changes require the appropriate legislative process.

4. Emergency Powers

The President issues and revokes emergency proclamations under the applicable constitutional provisions, on constitutional advice and subject to the prescribed safeguards.

[Additional] Part XVIII: Article 352 concerns an emergency due to war, external aggression or armed rebellion; Article 356 concerns failure of constitutional machinery in a State; Article 360 concerns financial emergency. "National Emergency" and "President's Rule" are conventional labels [Not mentioned in the Constitution] as the names of Articles 352 and 356.

5. Ordinance-Making Power: Article 123

5.1 Conditions and Nature

  • The President may promulgate an Ordinance except when both Houses of Parliament are in session. Either one House or both Houses being out of session satisfies this session condition.
  • The President must be satisfied that circumstances exist requiring immediate action. An Article 352 emergency is not necessary.
  • This is a legislative power exercised by the executive, on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, not a personal discretionary power.
  • An Ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament while operative, subject to constitutional restrictions.
[Correction] "Law made by the executive" is too broad as a definition: executive rules and notifications are not all Ordinances. An Ordinance is legislation promulgated using a specific constitutional ordinance-making power. "Emergent situation" means circumstances requiring immediate action, not necessarily a formally proclaimed emergency.

5.2 Duration and Early Termination

EventConsequence under Article 123(2)
Parliament reassemblesThe Ordinance must be laid before both Houses; it ceases at the expiration of six weeks from reassembly.
Houses reassemble on different datesCount six weeks from the later date.

Exam Point: One House's disapproval alone does not trigger Article 123(2)(a)'s early termination. But either House can prevent passage of an ordinary replacement Bill, allowing the Ordinance to lapse at the constitutional deadline.
[Correction] The life is not simply "six weeks from promulgation". The six-week clock starts on parliamentary reassembly, using the later date when the Houses return on different dates. "Six months and six weeks" is the usual textbook outer-limit explanation combining Articles 85 and 123, not a guaranteed term or a fixed period expressly written in Article 123. Actual duration depends on sitting dates and earlier termination; Article 85 measures the inter-session gap from the last sitting to the next session's first sitting.

5.4 Constitutional Limits

  • An Ordinance cannot amend the Constitution under Article 368.
  • Under Article 123(3), a provision Parliament would not be constitutionally competent to enact is void.
  • An Ordinance is "law" for Article 13 and must respect Fundamental Rights.

[Additional] An Ordinance can amend an ordinary Central Act and can operate retrospectively, subject to constitutional limits. For example, Article 20(1) bars retrospective creation of criminal liability and imposition of a greater penalty than the law permitted when the offence was committed. Amending a Central Act is not the same as amending the Constitution.

[Correction] The bar on violating Fundamental Rights is not simply a consequence of the inability to amend the Constitution. It follows from Articles 13 and 123(3). Like an ordinary law, an Ordinance may impose constitutionally permitted restrictions; it cannot validly abridge rights in violation of Part III.

5.5 Re-promulgation and Survival of Past Actions

[Correction] "Can be re-promulgated any number of times" is constitutionally misleading. There is no numerical cap in Article 123, but repeated re-promulgation to bypass the legislature is a fraud on the Constitution. D. C. Wadhwa acknowledged exceptional circumstances such as a genuinely overloaded or short session; that is not permission for routine renewal. Krishna Kumar Singh reaffirmed legislative control and rejected the repeated Bihar practice.
[Correction] "All actions remain valid after lapse, rejection or withdrawal" is too broad. Cessation is not automatically invalidity from the beginning, but there is also no automatic survival of every right, liability or completed transaction. Under the majority in Krishna Kumar Singh, survival is determined through constitutional interpretation, public interest and constitutional necessity, including the irreversibility of consequences. A replacement Act and its saving provisions may also matter; expiry must not be confused with repeal.
[Additional] A resolution approving an Ordinance does not by itself turn it into permanent legislation. A Bill must pass through the applicable legislative process and receive assent to replace it with an Act.

5.6 Statement Explaining the Ordinance

Lok Sabha Rule 71(1): when a Bill replacing an Ordinance, with or without modification, is introduced, a statement explaining the circumstances necessitating immediate legislation by Ordinance must accompany it.

[Additional] Rule 71(2) also requires such an explanatory statement at the commencement of the next session when an Ordinance incorporates, wholly or partly or with modification, provisions of a Bill already pending before the House. This is a parliamentary rule, not the text of Article 123.

7. Veto Powers of the President

7.1 Article 111: Assent to Parliamentary Bills

Bill passed by Parliament → Presented to President
Assent → Becomes an Act
Withhold assent → Does not become law
Return a non-Money Bill with a message → Parliamentary reconsideration
↓
Passed again with or without amendment and presented again → President shall not withhold assent

Assent is the constitutional step by which the Bill becomes an Act; commencement of particular provisions may be on a separate date specified in the Act. Withholding assent is often summarised as "the Bill is dead": it means this Bill is not enacted, not that fresh legislation on the subject is prohibited.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] Absolute veto, suspensive veto, pocket veto and qualified veto are classifications used in political science and textbooks. Article 111 uses the language of assent, withholding assent and return for reconsideration. These labels do not confer personal powers independent of Article 74 and responsible government.

[Additional] [Not mentioned in the Constitution] Government Bill means a Bill introduced by a minister; Private Member's Bill means one introduced by a member who is not a minister. These are parliamentary classifications, not labels used by Article 111. A private member may belong to the ruling party or the opposition.

7.2 Types of Veto

TypeMeaningIndian position
AbsoluteWithholding assent, preventing the Bill from becoming law.Textbook examples include a private member's Bill opposed by the government, or a Bill of an outgoing ministry which the successor ministry advises the President not to assent to.
SuspensiveReturning a Bill for reconsideration.Available for a non-Money Bill. After Parliament passes it again, with or without amendment, and presents it again, assent cannot be withheld. No special veto-override supermajority is imposed by Article 111.
PocketKeeping a Bill pending without assenting, withholding assent or returning it.Conventional description of historical inaction, associated with the absence of a fixed numerical deadline in Article 111. Not an express constitutional licence for indefinite obstruction.
QualifiedA veto overridden by a constitutionally specified supermajority.Not part of India's Article 111 system. The US uses a two-thirds vote in each House to override a returned veto.
[Correction] A government losing its majority does not automatically invalidate a Bill already passed. The relevant textbook scenario is that a new ministry advises withholding assent. Likewise, private member status does not automatically cause rejection.
[Correction] "There is no time limit" needs qualification: Article 111 has no fixed number of days, but its return proviso says "as soon as possible". Absence of a numerical deadline must not be equated with unlimited lawful discretion to obstruct legislation.

7.4 India and the United States

[Additional] Under US Article I, Section 7, a Bill not returned within ten days, Sundays excepted, becomes law without signature unless Congress's adjournment prevents its return; in that situation it does not become law, the US pocket veto. A returned veto may be overridden by two-thirds in each House, after which presidential assent is not required.

[Correction] US pocket veto is not simply "a veto exercised for ten days". The adjournment preventing return is essential. A qualified veto means a specified supermajority, not any vote count higher than the Bill's original vote.

8. Pardoning / Mercy Powers: Article 72

8.1 Three Categories

  • Court martial: all cases where punishment or sentence is by a court martial.
  • Union executive sphere: offences against laws relating to matters to which the Union's executive power extends.
  • Death sentences: all cases where the sentence is a sentence of death.

Article 72 authorises pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment and suspension, remission or commutation of sentences. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers. This is an executive constitutional power, not another judicial appeal.

[Correction] "Only after exhausting all judicial options" is not an express universal precondition in Article 72, which refers to a person convicted of an offence. In death-sentence practice, mercy proceedings ordinarily follow the judicial process; applicable procedural law and court orders must also be respected. Do not turn that ordinary sequence into additional constitutional wording.

8.2 Forms of Clemency

FormMeaningIllustration
PardonThe fullest form of clemency: releases the person from punishment and the penal consequences covered by the pardon.A full pardon in the conviction concerned; not an automatic pardon for unrelated convictions.
CommutationSubstitutes a less severe form of punishment.Death sentence changed to life imprisonment; rigorous imprisonment changed to simple imprisonment.
RemissionReduces the punishment to be undergone without changing its character.Five years of rigorous imprisonment reduced to three years of rigorous imprisonment.
ReprieveTemporary stay of execution of a sentence, particularly a death sentence.Execution postponed to allow consideration of clemency.
RespiteLesser punishment in view of special circumstances.Relief because of pregnancy, serious illness or physical disability, as appropriate to the case.
[Correction] Reprieve and respite are forms of relief granted, not merely a convict's demands. Commutation changes the kind of punishment; remission changes the amount to be undergone without changing its kind.
[Correction] A pardon is not an appellate judgment reversing the conviction. Kehar Singh distinguishes its practical effects from the judicial record, which is not amended, modified or superseded by presidential clemency. Avoid saying it "erases every conviction and the entire court record".

8.3 Supreme Court on Mercy

[Additional] The following cases explain why clemency is a constitutional safeguard against injustice, but not an unreviewable personal favour.

CasePrincipleApplication
[Additional] Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980)Constitutional clemency is exercised on ministerial advice, not personal presidential or gubernatorial choice; it cannot be arbitrary.Separate constitutional clemency from statutory remission, and from an independent personal discretion.
[Additional] Kehar Singh v. Union of India
16 December 1988; reported as (1989) 1 SCC 204		The President can scrutinise evidence and merits and reach a different conclusion for clemency without superseding the judicial record. No right to insist on an oral hearing.Refusing to consider merits on the mistaken belief that Article 72 prohibits it is reviewable. Courts review legal limits, not the clemency merits as a fresh appeal.

[Additional] Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of A.P. (11 October 2006): clemency decisions under Articles 72/161 are reviewable for non-application of mind, mala fides, extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations, exclusion of relevant material and arbitrariness. The Court set aside the challenged remission and left reconsideration open.

[Additional] Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (21 January 2014): undue, inordinate and unreasonable executive delay in mercy disposal may violate Article 21 and justify commutation of a death sentence, assessed case by case. Supervening circumstances such as established insanity are also relevant. The Court does not reopen the conviction's merits in this review. Its safeguards include a minimum fourteen-day interval between communication of mercy rejection and scheduled execution.

[Correction] "No oral hearing" means no enforceable right to insist on one, not a prohibition on providing one. Kehar Singh allows the decision-maker to call for material and give a hearing where useful. Its familiar "1989" date is the law-report year.
[Additional] Article 72(2) preserves statutory powers of armed-forces officers to suspend, remit or commute court-martial sentences. Article 72(3) preserves a Governor's statutory power to suspend, remit or commute a death sentence. Thus, "President has death-sentence clemency" does not mean the Governor has no role whatsoever in death-sentence relief.

Mains Points

  • Constitutional head, responsible government: powers formally vested in the President operate through ministerial advice, legislative accountability and constitutional limits.
  • Ordinances balance urgency and deliberation: immediate law-making during recess is useful, but routine renewal undermines parliamentary scrutiny, public debate and the separation of functions.
  • Judicial review checks misuse: Wadhwa condemns repeated renewal; Krishna Kumar Singh strengthens laying and accountability, without courts judging the adequacy of the executive's policy material.
  • Assent permits reconsideration: distinguish a parliamentary non-Money Bill's return from withholding assent, the US supermajority override, and the separate Article 201 process.
  • Mercy combines humanity with the rule of law: it can remedy injustice or consider humanitarian circumstances without becoming an appellate judgment. Relevant reasons, timely processing and limited judicial review restrain arbitrariness.
  • [Additional] Reform arguments: explain urgency publicly; place Ordinances promptly before Parliament; prioritise replacement Bills and scrutiny; use transparent mercy-processing stages and accountability for unexplained delay. These are suggested safeguards, not a claim that new constitutional requirements have been enacted.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2025 - General Studies Paper I

With reference to the Indian polity, consider the following statements:

  1. An Ordinance can amend any Central Act.
  2. An Ordinance can abridge a Fundamental Right.
  3. An Ordinance can come into effect from a back date.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only
(b) 2 and 3 only
(c) 1 and 3 only
(d) 1, 2 and 3

Correct answer: (c) 1 and 3 only. Under Article 123, an Ordinance has the force of an Act and can amend an ordinary Central Act, subject to Parliament's constitutional competence. It can be retrospective subject to limits such as Article 20(1). Articles 13 and 123(3) prevent unconstitutional abridgment of Fundamental Rights. This does not permit amendment of the Constitution under Article 368.

Question and answer source: Vajiram & Ravi: Ordinance-making power, 2025 Prelims PYQ.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 - General Studies Paper II

Resorting to ordinances has always raised concern on violation of the spirit of separation of powers doctrine. While noting the rationales justifying the power to promulgate ordinances, analyse whether the decisions of the Supreme Court on the issue have further facilitated resorting to this power. Should the power to promulgate the ordinances be repealed?

Suggested answer points, using the legal position available in 2015:

  • Rationale: Articles 123/213 bridge a temporary legislative recess when immediate action is necessary. Legislative competence, rights and post-reassembly legislative control remain applicable.
  • Concern: repeated executive law-making avoids normal debate, committee scrutiny and legislative responsibility; separation of powers in India is functional, not absolutely watertight.
  • Evaluate the judgments: R. C. Cooper discussed constitutional conditions but left review of satisfaction unresolved. D. C. Wadhwa condemned routine re-promulgation, while its exceptional-circumstances observation left some operational room. A balanced answer should not claim that the Court either universally approved or entirely prohibited Ordinances.
  • Conclusion: retention with strict accountability is more proportionate than repeal: show genuine urgency, lay the measure before the legislature, prioritise replacement legislation and prevent serial renewal.
[Additional] Later update: Krishna Kumar Singh (2017) strengthens this analysis on laying, re-promulgation, judicial review and surviving effects. It post-dates the question and should not be presented as a judgment available to a candidate in 2015.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2014 - General Studies Paper II

Instances of President's delay in commuting death sentences has come under public debate as denial of justice. Should there be a time limit specified for the President to accept/reject such petitions? Analyse.

Suggested answer points:

  • Constitutional setting: Article 72 is an advice-bound executive safeguard, separate from judicial appeal. It does not specify a numerical deadline for the President's decision.
  • Case for timely disposal: prolonged uncertainty causes psychological suffering, can impair dignity under Article 21 and weakens consistent administration of justice.
  • Judicial basis: Shatrughan Chauhan (2014) recognises undue, inordinate and unreasonable executive delay as a ground for commutation in appropriate cases; delay does not mechanically produce relief in every case.
  • Countervailing concern: irreversible punishment requires careful examination of records and humanitarian circumstances; rigid automatic rejection on expiry would defeat clemency's purpose.
  • Balanced conclusion: recommend accountable processing schedules, tracking, reasoned explanations for exceptional delay and judicial remedies. Distinguish a proposed constitutional decision deadline from procedural filing or forwarding periods under applicable law.

Both Mains question texts and years: Drishti IAS: Polity PYQs, 2015 and 2014 sections.
