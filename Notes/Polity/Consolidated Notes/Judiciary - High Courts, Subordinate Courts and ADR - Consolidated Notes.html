1. High Courts: Historical Formation

A High Court is a superior constitutional court for its assigned territory. Its history predates the Constitution, but its present authority must be understood within the Constitution, not merely colonial charters.

1.1 Two colonial streams

The British developed Crown courts in presidency towns and Company courts in the surrounding territories. Different personnel, procedures and competing jurisdiction created conflict and inconvenience.

Memory: Mayor's Courts (1726) → presidency Supreme Courts and Company adalats → High Courts Act (1861) → first three High Courts (1862) → constitutional High Courts (1950).

2. Constitutional Framework and Composition

Part VI, Chapter V, Articles 214-231 deals with High Courts in the States. Part VI, Chapter VI, Articles 233-237 deals with subordinate courts. Article 232 is omitted; do not invent a current subject for it.

  • Article 214: There shall be a High Court for each State. This does not require a separate institution for every State because Article 231 permits a common HC.
  • Article 216: Each HC consists of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary to appoint. There is no uniform constitutional number for all HCs.
  • Article 230: Parliament may extend a HC's jurisdiction to, or exclude it from, a Union Territory.
  • Article 231: Parliament may establish a common HC for two or more States, or for two or more States and a UT.
  • Article 241: Parliament may constitute a HC for a UT or declare an existing court there to be a HC for constitutional purposes.

2.1 Territorial examples

Examples in the class framework include Bombay HC serving Maharashtra, Goa and the UT of Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu; Punjab and Haryana HC serving both States and Chandigarh; and Gauhati High Court serving Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh. The court's official name is Gauhati, although the city is commonly written Guwahati.

The class notes record 25 High Courts, with separate Andhra Pradesh and Telangana arrangements from 1 January 2019. Treat this as the recorded institutional position, not a live count freshly checked for this compilation. A new bench is not necessarily a new High Court.

2.3 Strength, seats and benches

Sanctioned strength means authorised posts; working strength means judges actually serving. Vacancies explain the difference. A principal seat and regional benches belong to the same HC unless a law establishes a separate court.

Single-judge, Division Bench and Full Bench arrangements follow the relevant rules and judicial business. The Chief Justice allocates the roster. The SC's five-judge minimum under Article 145(3) is not a general five-judge requirement for constitutional cases in High Courts.

Constitutional position: The HC is not a department of the State Government and is not administratively managed by the SC as a district court is controlled by its HC. It is an independent constitutional court, subject to binding SC precedent and the prescribed appellate framework.

3. Appointments, Judges Cases, Collegium and NJAC

3.1 Constitutional text: Article 217

The President appoints an HC judge by warrant under the President's hand and seal, after consultation with the CJI, the Governor and, for appointment of a judge other than the HC Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of that HC. The Governor is a consultee, not the appointing authority. The constitutional executive acts through the applicable ministerial process.

The word collegium does not appear in Article 217. It describes the collective judicial consultation system developed through judgments and implemented through the Memorandum of Procedure (MoP).

The practice of selecting a Chief Justice from outside the HC promotes institutional independence, but is a convention/policy, not an express Article 217 qualification. Seniority matters, alongside suitability; promotion to CJ or elevation to SC is not an automatic right.

3.4 What was NJAC?

The proposed six-member body comprised the CJI, two senior-most SC judges, the Union Law Minister and two eminent persons. A selection committee consisting of the Prime Minister, CJI and Lok Sabha opposition leader (or leader of the largest opposition party, as provided) selected the eminent persons. The scheme provided representation requirements for one eminent-person position and permitted two members to block a recommendation.

Supporters sought transparency, wider participation and accountability. The majority judgment found the design incompatible with judicial independence. The dissent favoured the reform. The decision invalidated this particular constitutional/statutory scheme; it does not mean every possible appointments reform is constitutionally forbidden.

Mains balance: Judicial primacy protects independence, but opaque selection, delays, diversity gaps and unclear reasons justify demands for reform. Published criteria, reasoned decisions, timely processing and institutional support can improve accountability without executive domination.

4. Qualifications, Oath, Tenure, Removal and Salaries

4.1 Eligibility: Article 217(2)

A person must be an Indian citizen and must have either held judicial office in India for at least ten years, or been an advocate of a High Court, or two or more such courts in succession, for at least ten years. Article 217's explanations govern counting of qualifying periods.

  • Ten years of judicial office does not mean ten years only as a District Judge.
  • The Constitution prescribes no minimum age for appointment as an HC judge.
  • There is no distinguished-jurist route for HC appointment. That route belongs to SC eligibility under Article 124(3).
  • SC eligibility separately includes five years as an HC judge, ten years as an HC advocate, or distinguished jurist in the President's opinion. Do not omit its judicial route.

4.2 Oath: Article 219 and Third Schedule

Before entering office, an HC judge makes and subscribes an oath or affirmation before the Governor or a person appointed by the Governor for this purpose, subject to applicable constitutional/statutory adaptations for common HCs and UT arrangements. There is no universal rule that a UT's HC judges must take the oath before the President.

The oath covers allegiance to the Constitution; upholding India's sovereignty and integrity; faithful performance of duties to the best of ability, knowledge and judgment, without fear or favour, affection or ill-will; and upholding the Constitution and laws.

4.3 Tenure and cessation

Both HC Chief Justices and other HC judges retire at 62. The 15th Amendment, 1963 raised the original age of 60 to 62. SC judges, including the CJI, retire at 65. There is no general fixed six-year term for a permanent HC judge.

An HC judge may resign by writing addressed to the President. Office may also cease through removal, appointment to the SC or transfer to another HC as constitutionally provided. Under Article 217(3), an age dispute is decided by the President after consultation with the CJI; the constitutional text makes that decision final.

4.4 Removal: Articles 217, 218 and 124(4)-(5)

The grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity, not merely an unpopular judgment, policy disagreement or administrative preference. Article 218 applies the SC removal provisions to HC judges.

  1. A notice of motion under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 requires at least 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members.
  2. The Speaker/Chairman decides admission. Admission is not itself removal or a finding of guilt.
  3. A three-member inquiry committee includes a SC judge, an HC Chief Justice and a distinguished jurist, selected as prescribed by the Act.
  4. If the statutory process establishes the ground and the motion proceeds, each House must adopt an address by a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting.
  5. The address is presented to the President in the same session; the President issues the removal order.

Example: If a House has 245 total members and 180 members present and voting, the two thresholds are 123 and 120; at least 123 affirmative votes are necessary. Both tests apply, not whichever is easier.

Historical distinction: Proceedings concerning Justice V. Ramaswami did not secure the required Lok Sabha majority in 1993. Justice Soumitra Sen resigned in 2011 after the Rajya Sabha had adopted a removal motion but before completion in both Houses. A motion, inquiry, one-House vote, resignation and completed removal are different events.

4.6 Practice after office: Article 220

A former permanent HC judge may plead or act only in the SC and other HCs, subject to Article 220. The restriction excludes subordinate courts and authorities in India, not just the judge's former HC. It is not identical to Article 124(7), which bars a former SC judge from pleading or acting in any court or before any authority in India.

The constitutional wording concerns former permanent HC judges. Do not infer from it an unrestricted permission for every former additional/acting judge: professional rules and other applicable restrictions must also be considered. Acting as an arbitrator is different from practising as an advocate before a court.

5. Transfers, Special Judges and Independence

5.1 Transfer under Article 222

The President may transfer an HC judge to another HC after consultation with the CJI. Judicial interpretation requires the collective consultation process, ordinarily the CJI and four senior-most SC judges, with relevant views including those of the two concerned HC Chief Justices. The judge's views and personal circumstances may be considered, but consent is not a constitutional prerequisite.

Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977) held that the judge's consent is not required; transfers must serve public interest/administration of justice, not punishment. The later Judges Cases strengthened collective judicial consultation. K. Ashok Reddy v. Government of India (1994) illustrates the limited scope of judicial scrutiny of transfers.

Article 222(2) provides a compensatory allowance as determined by Parliament by law. Transfer is not removal and cannot lawfully substitute for the removal process as a punishment. A transfer of a judge is also different from transfer of a case under Article 228, Article 139A, CPC or BNSS.

5.2 Independence safeguards

  • Security of tenure and difficult removal, protecting judges from dismissal for inconvenient decisions.
  • Collective judicial participation in appointments and transfers, rather than unilateral executive selection.
  • Charged expenditure, protected service entitlements and HC control over its establishment.
  • Article 211 bars discussion of SC/HC judges' discharge of duties in State Legislatures; Article 121 limits such discussion in Parliament, with the removal-motion exception.
  • Article 235 judicial control over subordinate courts and Article 50's separation objective.
  • Contempt powers protect administration of justice; fair criticism and accountability remain necessary.

Risks: Vacancies, delayed appointments, unexplained transfers, executive influence over budgets, opaque internal processes and post-retirement expectations may weaken practical independence. Independence protects impartial adjudication, not immunity from lawful investigation, reasoned criticism or accountability.

6. High Court Jurisdiction, Powers and Judgments

6.1 Original jurisdiction

Original means the matter begins in that court, rather than arriving as an appeal. It does not mean unlimited power to hear every lawsuit. Article 225 preserves pre-Constitution jurisdiction subject to the Constitution and laws made by the competent legislature.

  • Ordinary original civil suits: Available in specified HCs and territorial/pecuniary settings under their governing law, Letters Patent and amendments. Bombay, Calcutta, Madras and Delhi are familiar examples; they do not all have identical limits.
  • Writ petitions: Begin directly in the HC under Article 226, though commonly studied separately as writ jurisdiction.
  • Election petitions: Challenges to Parliament/State Legislature elections go to the HC under Section 80A, Representation of the People Act, 1951. President/VP election disputes belong to the SC under Article 71.
  • Special subjects: Admiralty, testamentary/probate and other jurisdictions depend on law. Insolvency/company matters cannot all be assigned to HCs without considering transfers to statutory tribunals.
  • Criminal original jurisdiction: Ordinary serious criminal trials usually begin in Sessions Courts, not HCs. Avoid the absolute claim that a HC can never exercise any original criminal jurisdiction; special statutory powers and contempt must be distinguished.

6.2 Appellate jurisdiction

Civil first appeal: May involve facts and law under the governing statute; the appellate forum depends on the original court, valuation and State law. Second appeal under Section 100 CPC: Requires a substantial question of law, not merely a second opportunity to contest facts. Intra-court/Letters Patent appeals are subject to exclusions and local rules; not every Single Judge order is appealable to a Division Bench.

Criminal appeals: Under BNSS Section 415(2), convictions in trials held by a Sessions Judge/Additional Sessions Judge go to the HC, subject to statutory restrictions. The provision also covers a trial by another court in which imprisonment exceeding seven years is imposed on the convicted person or another person convicted at the same trial. Ordinary magistrate-conviction appeals generally go to the Sessions Court under Section 415(3).

Do not memorise: "HC hears criminal appeals only if punishment exceeds seven years." That is false for Sessions/Additional Sessions trials. Nor does this provision give every magistrate authority to impose more than seven years.

Death confirmation: A Sessions Court's death sentence must be submitted to the HC and cannot be executed unless confirmed. BNSS Section 407 corresponds to CrPC Section 366. This mandatory reference is distinct from the convict's appeal. The HC independently examines the case and may confirm, alter the outcome, order a new trial or acquit as law permits.

6.3 Writ jurisdiction: Article 226

A HC may issue directions, orders and writs for Fundamental Rights and other legal rights. "For any other purpose" does not mean unrestricted power to decide any political or personal disagreement. There must be a legally cognisable basis and the appropriate public-law connection.

Territory: Article 226(2) permits jurisdiction where the cause of action arises wholly or partly, even if the respondent's seat is outside the territory. The petitioner's residence alone does not create the necessary cause of action. Article 226(4) preserves the SC's Article 32 power.

Discretion: Delay, disputed facts, alternative statutory remedies and equitable considerations may affect relief. An alternative remedy is generally a rule of judicial restraint, not an absolute jurisdictional bar. Important exceptions include Fundamental Rights enforcement, violation of natural justice, lack of jurisdiction and a challenge to legislative validity.

Interim protection: Article 226(3) contains a specific time-bound mechanism for vacating certain ex parte interim orders when the affected party makes the prescribed application and supplies a copy. Do not confuse interim stay with final success.

Emergency distinction: A National Emergency does not automatically abolish Article 226. However, a valid presidential order under Article 359 can suspend the right to move any court to enforce the specified Part III rights for the specified period; Articles 20 and 21 are protected from that suspension. Other legal grounds and the terms of the order matter. The class table's absolute claim that HC writ enforcement "cannot be suspended" is therefore misleading.

6.4 Superintendence and control

Article 227 administrative particulars: The HC may call for returns from subordinate courts, make general rules and prescribe forms regulating their practice/proceedings, prescribe the form of books, entries and accounts kept by their officers, and settle tables of fees allowed to specified officers and practitioners. The relevant rules, forms and fee tables must be consistent with law and require the Governor's previous approval under the Article's proviso. "Settle accounts" is not an accurate substitute for the specific power to settle fee tables.

Judicial supervision: Keeping courts within jurisdiction, correcting grave procedural injustice and ensuring obedience to governing law are different from reassessing every factual finding. Identify the specific legal basis for withdrawal, transfer or revision: Article 228, CPC transfer/revision provisions and criminal-procedure provisions have distinct conditions; Article 227 should not be used as a catch-all substitute.

Emergency-era history: The 42nd Amendment restricted Article 227, including insertion of a restriction concerning superintendence over judicial decisions. The 44th Amendment removed the relevant restrictions and restored the broader wording; describing the restoration simply as a judicial striking-down is inaccurate.

6.5 Court of record and contempt

Article 215 gives the HC court-of-record status, including contempt of itself. Section 10 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 addresses contempt of subordinate courts. Authoritative records and precedent are related but distinct: not every sentence recorded in a judgment becomes binding law.

Record function: Judgments, proceedings and official acts are preserved as authoritative records for lasting memory and testimony and have evidentiary value. This is distinct from whether the ratio of a decision binds another court. Records of subordinate courts also have legal evidentiary value under the applicable law; they do not become worthless merely because those courts lack the HC's constitutional court-of-record status.

Civil contempt: Wilful disobedience of a court order or wilful breach of an undertaking. Criminal contempt: The statutory categories of scandalising/lowering authority, prejudicing judicial proceedings or obstructing administration of justice.

Fair and accurate reporting and fair criticism receive statutory protection; truth may be permitted as a defence when public interest and bona fides are satisfied. Section 13 requires the relevant substantial interference threshold for punishment. Section 12 states a maximum of six months' simple imprisonment, Rs 2,000 fine, or both, with its qualifications. These are statutory limits, not a claim that criticism is automatically punishable.

The Advocate General's written consent requirement under Section 15 concerns specified private motions for criminal contempt outside the court's presence; it is not a universal prerequisite for civil contempt, court-initiated proceedings or all subordinate-court references. Under Section 19, appealable contempt decisions of a Single Judge go to an HC bench of at least two judges; those of a bench go to the SC. Not every procedural order or refusal to initiate contempt carries that statutory appeal.

Additional safeguards: Section 3 protects innocent publication/distribution under its conditions; Section 4 protects fair and accurate reporting, subject to statutory exceptions; Section 5 concerns fair comment on the merits of a finally decided case. Section 13's truth defence was added in 2006 and depends on public interest and bona fides. A satisfactory apology may support discharge or remission under Section 12; a qualified apology is not automatically unacceptable if bona fide.

Limitation: Section 20 ordinarily bars initiation after one year from the alleged contempt. It is a limit on initiation, not a requirement to finish the case within one year. What counts as initiation and questions such as continuing contempt or concealed fraud require the applicable case law, including Pallav Sheth v. Custodian (2001).

Subordinate courts: They do not possess Articles 129/215's general constitutional contempt power and may refer appropriate matters to the HC. However, criminal-procedure law gives courts specified powers concerning certain offences committed in their presence and related misconduct. Thus "subordinate courts have no power to respond to any courtroom misconduct" is also wrong.

Class case examples: E.M.S. Namboodiripad v. T.N. Nambiar (1970) upheld a contempt finding concerning statements imputing class bias; its facts should not be converted into a ban on all sociological criticism. In re Prashant Bhushan (2020) involved a criminal-contempt finding for specified tweets and a Re 1 fine, prompting debate about speech and judicial authority. Read both with P.N. Duda and the statutory safeguards rather than memorising "criticism equals contempt".

6.6 Judicial review, review of judgments and inherent power

Judicial review tests legislation/executive action against constitutional and legal limits: legislative competence, Fundamental Rights, natural justice, improper purpose and other applicable grounds. Review of a judgment asks the same court to correct its own decision on limited recognised grounds; civil review rests on Section 114 and Order XLVII CPC, while constitutional jurisdiction has its own recognised review/recall principles.

BNSS Section 528 preserves the HC's inherent powers to give effect to orders under the Sanhita, prevent abuse of court process or secure the ends of justice (formerly CrPC Section 482). It is not a licence to conduct a mini-trial while considering quashing. Criminal judgment review is subject to specific restrictions; do not import the civil review rules wholesale.

6.7 Establishment: Article 229

Appointments of HC officers and servants are made by the HC Chief Justice or the judge/officer directed. The Governor may require consultation with the State PSC in the specified recruitment circumstances. Service rules concerning salaries, allowances, leave or pensions need the Governor's approval. HC administrative expenses, including staff remuneration/pensions, are charged on the State Consolidated Fund; fees and other money taken by the court form part of that Fund.

9.3 Old CrPC versus BNSS

The BNSS came into force on 1 July 2024, with savings for specified pending proceedings. Under the older CrPC Section 29, the JMFC fine ceiling was Rs 10,000 and the second-class ceiling Rs 5,000. The corresponding general imprisonment limits were three and one years.

The older CrPC Section 28(3) included an Assistant Sessions Judge with no power to impose death, life imprisonment or imprisonment exceeding ten years. Do not copy that as a separate general BNSS Section 22 category: the BNSS provision identifies Sessions and Additional Sessions Judges. Older metropolitan-magistrate terminology also should not be indiscriminately reproduced as a separate nationwide BNSS hierarchy.

Worked examples: A JMFC hearing an offence carrying up to five years does not acquire a five-year sentencing power merely because the offence is triable there; the lawful procedure for a sentence beyond competence must be followed. 9.4 Appeals and other checks

Magistrate convictions ordinarily go to the Sessions Court; Sessions/Additional Sessions trial convictions go to the HC under Section 415, with guilty-plea, petty-case and other statutory restrictions. Acquittal appeals, victim appeals and enhancement appeals have their own provisions. Bail, revision, appeal, quashing and death confirmation are not interchangeable remedies.

In Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980), the SC upheld the death penalty framework subject to the rarest-of-rare standard. Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983) elaborated considerations. The relevant sentencing law, individualised hearing and constitutional safeguards apply; confirmation is not a clerical formality.

10. Legal Services Authorities and Free Legal Aid

10.1 Constitutional aim and statutory machinery

Article 39A, inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, directs the State to secure equal justice and provide free legal aid so that economic or other disabilities do not deny access to justice. It is a DPSP, not a separate Fundamental Right by its own wording. Articles 14 and 21 and fair-trial jurisprudence reinforce the enforceable protections involved.

The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, brought into force on 9 November 1995, provides the institutional structure. National Legal Services Day is observed on 9 November. NALSA is a statutory body, not a constitutional body; Article 39A supplies the objective, while the Act creates the machinery.

10.2 Eligibility: Sections 12 and 13

Section 12 covers listed categories, including members of SC/ST communities; victims of trafficking or begar; women and children; persons with disabilities within the statutory category; persons in circumstances of undeserved want arising from listed disasters/violence and similar events; industrial workmen; persons in custody; and persons below the prescribed income threshold.

Category-based eligibility is not merely an income test. A woman or person in custody does not have to qualify only through the low-income clause. Section 13 also requires the authority to be satisfied about a prima facie case to prosecute or defend. Income thresholds differ by governing rule/forum and should not be memorised as one universal amount.

10.3 What assistance includes

Legal advice, representation by a panel lawyer, preparation of pleadings, eligible litigation expenses and access to certified documents may be provided under the scheme. Legal literacy, prison legal services, front offices, para-legal volunteers and Lok Adalats support access. The Legal Aid Defence Counsel System is an organised criminal legal-aid model, not a replacement for courts or independent prosecution.

Example: An undertrial unable to afford representation may approach the jail legal-aid clinic, DLSA or court for counsel. A woman seeking maintenance may approach the relevant legal-services institution. The authority assists with legal services; it does not itself grant divorce, acquit the accused or decide the maintenance claim.

10.4 Important cases and challenges

  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979): undertrial detention, speedy trial and access to justice.
  • Khatri (II) v. State of Bihar (1981): free legal aid obligations arise at critical early stages, not only once the trial is underway.
  • Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh (1986): failure to provide legal aid can vitiate a conviction; ignorance of the right is not an adequate answer.

Challenges: Lack of awareness, uneven lawyer quality, low remuneration, language and disability barriers, weak follow-up and pressure to treat legal aid as a formality. Reforms: Early access to counsel, adequate funding, training, client feedback, accessible forms and effective monitoring of assistance rather than merely counting appointments.

11. Family Courts

The Family Courts Act, 1984 creates specialised courts to promote conciliation and secure speedy settlement of marriage and family disputes. The Law Commission's 59th Report (1974) supported a less adversarial approach. A Family Court is a court capable of adjudication, not merely a mediation centre.

11.1 Establishment and judges

Under Section 3, the State Government, after consultation with the HC and by notification, shall establish a Family Court for every area comprising a city or town whose population exceeds one million, and may establish one for other areas. The population criterion is not "every district with ten lakh inhabitants".

Judges are appointed by the State Government with the concurrence of the HC under Section 4. A court may have one or more judges; where there are several, a Principal Judge and an Additional Principal Judge may be designated as provided. It is not necessarily a single-judge institution.

Section 4 qualification routes include at least seven years of qualifying judicial office or specified tribunal/legal posts, at least seven years as an HC advocate (including successive HCs), or other qualifications prescribed by the Central Government with the CJI's concurrence. Social-work experience alone does not automatically satisfy these statutory routes. Preference is given to women and to suitability for protecting marriage, promoting children's welfare and facilitating settlement. The Act sets an upper age of 62 for holding office.

11.2 Jurisdiction: Section 7

  • Nullity, restitution of conjugal rights, judicial separation and dissolution of marriage.
  • Declarations about validity of marriage or matrimonial status.
  • Property disputes between parties to a marriage, and specified orders/injunctions arising from a marital relationship.
  • Legitimacy, maintenance, guardianship, custody and access to minors within the provision.
  • Specified summary maintenance jurisdiction historically under Chapter IX CrPC, with the corresponding BNSS framework including Section 144, and other jurisdiction conferred by enactment.

Scope caution: Not every inheritance, adoption or family-linked dispute automatically belongs here. Adoption jurisdiction under other laws, including the juvenile-justice framework, must be identified separately. Prosecution for matrimonial cruelty ordinarily belongs to the competent criminal court, but Section 26 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 permits specified civil protective reliefs to be sought in relevant proceedings before civil, family or criminal courts. Thus "Family Courts can never grant DV Act relief" is too broad.

11.3 Procedure and appeals

Section 8: exclusive allocation and transfer. Where a Family Court is established, the ordinary district/subordinate civil courts and magistrates lose the corresponding jurisdiction in its area to the extent specified in Sections 7-8; covered pending proceedings transfer to the Family Court. This is subject-specific exclusivity, not removal of all civil/criminal work involving relatives. Sections 5-6 support association of welfare organisations and provision of counsellors; those helpers do not replace the judge.

Section 9: Duty to assist and persuade parties toward settlement in the first instance where possible consistently with the nature and circumstances of the case. There is no power to force reconciliation or unsafe compromise. Counsellors and experts may assist; failed settlement permits adjudication.

Section 11: In-camera proceedings may be held at the court's discretion and shall be held if either party so desires. Section 13: No entitlement as of right to legal representation; the provision and relevant rules/case law govern permitted assistance. Section 14: Flexible reception of material assists effective adjudication; fairness and the chance to answer adverse material still matter.

Section 19: Specified non-interlocutory judgments/orders are appealable to the HC on facts and law, with a bench of two or more judges. The Act states a 30-day appeal period; interaction with other matrimonial statutes and applicable case law should be checked for actual filing. Consent decrees/orders and Chapter IX maintenance orders are excluded from that ordinary appeal; the latter attract the specified HC scrutiny under Section 19(4). Not every Family Court order has the same remedy.

Example: Parents agree on school-week custody and weekend access with counselling assistance, subject to the child's welfare and court approval. If agreement fails, the court determines custody under the governing law; it cannot treat a parent's refusal to compromise as automatic unfitness.

Benefits and limits: Specialised handling, privacy and counselling can reduce conflict; understaffing, delayed interim maintenance, inadequate counsellors and coercive reconciliation practices can defeat that purpose.

12. Fast Track Courts and Fast Track Special Courts

12.1 What does fast track mean?

A Fast Track Court (FTC) is an arrangement for prioritised, expeditious disposal of assigned cases, usually within the existing subordinate judiciary. It is not a new constitutional level above Sessions Courts and does not acquire extra sentencing powers merely from the label.

The early nationwide FTC programme began around 2000 with Eleventh Finance Commission support to clear long-pending cases, particularly Sessions cases. Later continuation and coverage depended on Central/State programmes. It is incorrect to describe every FTC as exclusively a POCSO court or as governed by one universal "Fast Track Courts Act".

Dedicated judges, fewer adjournments, witness scheduling, video links where lawful and improved case management can reduce delay. Presumption of innocence, disclosure, cross-examination, representation, reasoned decisions and lawful sentencing remain essential.

13. Gram Nyayalayas and Criminal Procedure Essentials

13.1 Accessible statutory courts

The Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 is Act 4 of 2009, receiving assent on 7 January 2009 and commencing on 2 October 2009. State Governments may establish these courts after consultation with the HC, at the intermediate-panchayat level or for prescribed groups of contiguous panchayats. Headquarters and mobile sittings bring justice closer to rural residents.

The presiding Nyayadhikari is appointed by the State Government in consultation with the HC and must qualify for appointment as a Judicial Magistrate First Class. This is a judicial court, not the Gram Panchayat, Gram Sabha, khap body or an informal village settlement council.

13.2 Jurisdiction and procedure

Local geography: Zilla Parishad is the district-level Panchayat body; Panchayat Samiti/Block Panchayat is the intermediate level; Gram Panchayat is the village level. State naming and the existence of an intermediate tier vary. The Gram Nyayalaya's notified jurisdiction and headquarters use the statutory local-area framework; the court is not administratively subordinate to these elected Panchayat bodies.

Mobile sittings and service: Section 9 provides for visits and proceedings in villages within jurisdiction, with advance public notice. The Nyayadhikari's salary, allowances and service conditions follow the Act's JMFC-equivalence framework as prescribed, while subject jurisdiction continues to be defined by the Gram Nyayalayas Act and schedules.

Criminal jurisdiction derives from the First Schedule and civil jurisdiction from the Second Schedule, with legally made amendments. General descriptions such as land, wages, water or minor offences do not independently confer jurisdiction. Pecuniary limits for civil matters are specified by the HC in consultation with the State Government under Section 13(2).

Criminal cases ordinarily use summary procedure under the Act, but Section 19 allows conversion to the prescribed non-summary procedure where warranted. Civil proceedings use simplified procedures consistent with natural justice. Section 26 requires efforts at conciliation where possible, consistently with the nature and circumstances of the case. Failure to settle does not deprive the court of its adjudicatory role.

Schedule changes: Section 14 provides notification-based powers for the Central and State Governments over the relevant parts of the schedules within their legislative fields, with the required consultation and legislative-laying procedures. A local officer cannot expand jurisdiction simply by considering a dispute minor. Section 20 expressly accommodates the applicable plea-bargaining framework; this is separate from civil conciliation and is subject to criminal-law eligibility and safeguards.

Civil case management: Day-to-day hearing, justified adjournments, simplified procedure and the six-month disposal endeavour work together. Flexible admission of material does not remove relevance, fair hearing or reasoned adjudication; a summary record does not mean instant conviction without a defence opportunity.

The Act provides low prescribed civil court fees capped at Rs 100, legal aid, flexible evidence provisions and an endeavour toward disposal of civil applications within six months. The six-month aspiration is not a reason to deny a fair hearing or declare automatic success.

Appeals: Criminal appeals under Section 33 go to the Sessions Court; civil appeals under Section 34 go to the District Court, generally within 30 days, with statutory exclusions and condonation provisions. Do not say every order is appealable or describe one month as inherently shorter than 30 days. Further ordinary appeals are restricted, but constitutional remedies remain distinct.

13.3 Regular and summary trials

Summary trial is an abbreviated lawful trial with a shorter record, not instant punishment without evidence. Under BNSS Section 285, imprisonment in a summary conviction cannot exceed three months; the old equivalent is CrPC Section 262(2), not Section 263. The substantive offence maximum, trial eligibility and procedural sentence cap are three distinct limits.

Correction: "A Gram Nyayalaya can never impose more than three months" is too absolute because the Act provides for conversion out of summary procedure. Similarly, a seven-year offence threshold appearing in summary-trial eligibility provisions does not authorise a seven-year summary sentence.

It hears specified Union service/recruitment disputes and those of covered/notified authorities, not every dispute involving any government employee. Its membership draws on judicial and administrative streams under applicable law.

Exclusions: Section 2 of the 1985 Act excludes the specified armed-forces personnel, officers/servants of the SC or HCs, and the specified parliamentary/State-legislature secretariat staff. Civilian defence-service employees should not automatically be equated with excluded armed-forces members. A State employee does not enter CAT jurisdiction merely because CAT is a national institution.

SATs and joint tribunals: The Central Government may establish a State Administrative Tribunal on the State's request under the parliamentary Act. The Act also permits a joint administrative tribunal for two or more States through its statutory arrangement. A State request is not the same as a State legislature creating an Article 323A tribunal under its own law. Not every State has an operational SAT.

Article 323B list: Taxation; foreign exchange/import/export across customs frontiers; industrial/labour disputes; land reforms; ceiling on urban property; parliamentary/State-legislature elections subject to the constitutional exceptions; production/procurement/supply/distribution of foodstuffs and specified essential goods and related price control; rent/tenancy and landlord-tenant matters; offences and fees relating to the listed matters; and incidental matters. The appropriate legislature acts within its competence. A constitutional enabling provision does not prove that a tribunal currently exercises each listed jurisdiction: parliamentary and State-legislature election petitions remain allocated to HCs by the RPA framework discussed earlier.

14.2C Constitutional scrutiny and the parent-Act distinction

S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987) considered the administrative tribunal as an effective alternative institutional mechanism. L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997), a seven-judge decision, held that HC review under Articles 226/227 and SC jurisdiction under Article 32 form part of the basic structure; their exclusion was unconstitutional.

In the L. Chandra Kumar framework, tribunals continue as courts of first instance for assigned matters, with their decisions subject to scrutiny before a Division Bench of the territorial HC. This does not create a universal identical statutory appeal for every tribunal in India. Parent statutes may prescribe appeals on different grounds to different forums, while constitutional review remains.

Exact holding: Article 323A(2)(d) and Article 323B(3)(d), and corresponding statutory exclusions, were invalid to the extent they excluded the protected constitutional jurisdiction. Tribunals may examine constitutional challenges within their assigned field, subject to HC scrutiny, but the L. Chandra Kumar framework excepts a challenge to the constitutional validity of their own parent statute, for which the HC may be approached directly. A tribunal necessarily interprets its parent Act to apply it; "cannot interpret the Act that created it" is an erroneous class-note shorthand.

Case distinctions: Madras Bar Association (2014) struck down the National Tax Tribunals Act, 2005 on constitutional grounds concerning the transfer of adjudicatory functions and independence; it did more than issue general appointment guidelines. Rojer Mathew (2019) invalidated the 2017 tribunal-service rules and addressed independence, while referring the Finance Act's Money Bill issue for larger-bench consideration. The July 2021 Madras Bar Association ruling addressed provisions of the 2021 Ordinance; it cannot chronologically be described as a ruling on the later August 2021 Act. Subsequent challenges must be identified by the particular instrument and judgment date.

Union of India v. R. Gandhi, President, Madras Bar Association (2010), the Madras Bar Association line of cases and Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019) underline judicial independence, appropriate qualifications, appointments, tenure and protection from excessive executive influence. Reform provisions must be studied with the judgments affecting them, not solely their enactment date.

14.3 Evaluation

Advantages: Expertise, focused procedures and potential relief to regular courts. Risks: Vacancies, short or insecure tenure, sponsoring-ministry control, fragmented appeal routes and inadequate infrastructure. Suggested reforms include independent appointments/administration, stable tenure, permanent facilities, rationalised jurisdictions and accessible regional hearings.

Do not memorise an unverified reform as enacted law: The class discussion's references to a "Tribunal Reform Act 2026" or a functioning "National Tribunals Commission" have not been established by the sources checked for this document. A commission is discussed as a reform proposal here. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 is a separate enacted measure whose provisions must be read with applicable judicial rulings and later amendments.

Class proposal retained for coverage, not as current law: The 29 August notes describe an NTC handling appointments/removal, performance review, funding and a tribunal data grid; they propose a Chairman plus two judicial and two technical members and refer to coverage of 16 tribunals. Those composition, appointment and coverage claims remain unverified. The policy rationale is independent, coordinated tribunal administration; do not cite the proposed body or a "National Tribunal Data Grid" as operational on that basis. This is distinct from the established National Judicial Data Grid for courts.

15.1 Meaning and organising principle

Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) describes processes used to resolve disputes outside ordinary full-scale courtroom adjudication or alongside it. Some are private; others are court-connected or statutory. "Alternative" does not mean outside the law, and ADR does not always eliminate judicial involvement.

15.2 One dispute, four different methods

Facts: Asha supplies furniture worth Rs 1 lakh to Bharat. Bharat withholds payment, alleging defects in two chairs. The figures below are independent illustrations, not mandatory stages in one case.

  1. Negotiation: They talk directly and agree on Rs 85,000 plus replacement of two chairs. There is no required third party. Neither may impose a discount on the other.
  2. Mediation: A neutral learns that Asha needs immediate cash while Bharat needs usable furniture before reopening. They agree on Rs 80,000 immediately and replacement by a fixed date. The mediator helped uncover interests; the parties chose the terms.
  3. Conciliation: A conciliator proposes Rs 82,000 and a replacement timetable. The proposal becomes a settlement only if accepted and properly recorded. A conciliator's suggestion is not an award against an unwilling party.
  4. Arbitration: Under a valid arbitration clause, an arbitrator hears invoices, defect evidence and arguments, then awards Rs 88,000. The losing party's fresh approval is not required for the award to bind, subject to available legal remedies.

No compulsory ladder: Negotiation → mediation → conciliation → arbitration is not the legal sequence for all disputes. A contract may specify staged escalation, and a statute may require a particular pre-litigation process; otherwise the appropriate route depends on consent, jurisdiction and law.

15.3 Negotiation in detail

Parties identify issues, exchange proposals, assess evidence and alternatives, then agree or walk away. It is flexible, inexpensive in simple cases and useful for ongoing relationships. Representatives and lawyers may participate; "no compulsory neutral" is not "lawyers prohibited".

Weaknesses include unequal bargaining power, withheld information and stalemate. A lawful settlement needs capacity, free consent, lawful terms and any required formalities. In a pending civil suit, a lawful written and signed compromise may be recorded under Order XXIII Rule 3 CPC, leading to a compromise decree. A private email bargain is not automatically a court decree.

15.4 Mediation in detail

A typical process includes suitability screening, appointment of a neutral, agreement on procedure/confidentiality, joint and separate meetings, issue identification, option generation and a precise written settlement or closure without settlement. Court referral does not entitle the mediator to compel a party to accept terms.

The mediator may test assumptions and suggest options; the neat textbook claim that a mediator "can never suggest" while a conciliator "always dictates" is wrong. The central shared limit is no imposed settlement. The Mediation Act, 2023 uses a broad definition that includes processes called conciliation.

Family property, commercial payment, neighbourhood and employment disputes may benefit, subject to applicable law. Protective screening matters in domestic abuse, dependency or severe power imbalance. Confidentiality is governed by the relevant rules and exceptions; it is not a universal licence to hide fraud or defeat statutory duties.

15.5 Conciliation in detail

Part III of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 provides a statutory conciliation framework, including invitation/acceptance, appointment, the conciliator's assistance and formulation of settlement terms. Under Sections 73-74, a qualifying signed settlement authenticated by the conciliator is final and binding and has the status/effect of an arbitral award on agreed terms.

Thus "conciliation is non-binding" is incomplete: the proposed solution is not binding before acceptance; a properly concluded statutory settlement is binding. Failed conciliation produces no imposed merits award. Read this framework with the commencement and amendment caution in the next chapter.

15.6 Arbitration in detail

Parties ordinarily agree to submit defined disputes to an arbitrator or tribunal. That consent to adjudication does not mean that the eventual award requires renewed consent. The tribunal decides jurisdiction within the statutory framework, hears both sides, evaluates the case and gives an award.

Institutional arbitration uses an institution's rules and administrative support; ad hoc arbitration is organised by the parties/tribunal without that administration. An institution ordinarily administers, while the appointed tribunal decides. Domestic/international commercial and India-seated/foreign-seated are different classifications.

Seat versus venue: The juridical seat anchors supervisory arbitration law and courts; a hearing venue is simply where a hearing occurs. Contract substantive law, arbitration procedural law and institutional rules are separate choices, subject to legal constraints.

Not all disputes are arbitrable. Criminal prosecution, matrimonial status and other reserved/public-law matters cannot simply be transferred to private arbitration by agreement. Individual property or contractual claims must be assessed under the statute and case law; the civil label alone is insufficient.

  1. Section 7: A legally sufficient arbitration agreement; mere willingness to negotiate is not an arbitration clause.

Do not promise that every arbitration ends within a single fixed number of months or is cheaper than litigation. 16.3 Can courts modify an arbitral award? Gayatri Balasamy (2025)

Gayatri Balasamy v. M/S ISG Novasoft Technologies Ltd., decided 30 April 2025, 2025 INSC 605, was a five-judge Constitution Bench decision. The 4:1 majority, led by CJI Sanjiv Khanna, recognised narrowly limited modification powers under Sections 34/37. Justice K.V. Viswanathan dissented on the statutory modification power.

The majority's summary in paragraph 85 identifies:

  1. Severance: Separate an invalid portion where it is legally and practically separable from the valid part.
  2. Obvious errors: Correct apparent clerical, computational or typographical mistakes without merits re-evaluation.
  3. Post-award interest: Modify it in appropriate circumstances within the judgment's limits.
  4. Article 142: The SC may use its constitutional power cautiously and within constitutional bounds; this is not a power conferred on every Section 34 court.

Correct takeaway: Neither "courts can never modify an award" nor "courts may freely rewrite an award" captures the majority holding. An ordinary merits reassessment remains impermissible. Do not present the dissent as the Court's controlling decision.

Example: An apparent addition error in a stated calculation is different from deciding that a witness deserved more credibility and increasing damages. The first may fall within narrow correction; the second is ordinarily an impermissible merits reconsideration.

16.4 Mediation Act, 2023: enacted design versus operative law

The Act was enacted in September 2023. Its initial October 2023 commencement notification brought specified provisions into force, not the entire substantive framework at once. This compilation does not establish the complete commencement position as of September 2026. Before stating that a particular section currently governs a case, check its commencement notification, rules and transitional provisions.

Enacted design: Section 5 provides a voluntary general pre-litigation mediation framework by mutual consent, while preserving the commercial Section 12A requirement. Section 6 and the Schedule address disputes not fit for mediation. The Act includes mediation agreements, mediators, confidentiality, online mediation, institutional governance and mediated settlements. Its enacted Sections 27-28 address enforcement and limited challenge of covered mediated settlement agreements.

The enacted timetable is generally 120 days from the date fixed for first appearance before the mediator, extendable by agreement up to a further 60 days. That is a feature of the enacted framework, not an assertion that every mediation under every law currently follows this clock. Community mediation has a distinct purpose and its settlement is not to be conflated with an automatically enforceable decree under the commercial/civil settlement route.

Three corrections: (1) The Act does not make mediation compulsory for all civil cases. (2) Mediation was not legally unenforceable before 2023: contract, compromise-decree and special statutory routes already existed. (3) The 2023 Act's consequential amendments must be read with commencement; do not declare every earlier conciliation provision displaced solely because the Act was passed.

Mediation governance and process choices

Mediation Council of India: The enacted 2023 framework provides for a statutory Council concerned with standards, mediator registration and recognition/regulation of relevant mediation institutions and training arrangements. Its existence in the Act is distinct from a verified claim that every appointment, registration system or rule is operational. It is not a court hearing ordinary merits appeals from settlements, and a mediator need not invariably be a retired judge.

Formality is a guide, not a rigid ranking: Negotiation is often least formal and arbitration more adjudicatory, but a complex mediation may be highly structured while an agreed arbitration may use streamlined procedure. Parties' choices of neutral, language, timetable, place and fees operate within mandatory law, institutional rules and conflict-of-interest safeguards.

16.5 Mandatory commercial pre-institution mediation

Section 12A applies to covered commercial suits not contemplating urgent interim relief. The plaintiff must exhaust the prescribed pre-institution mediation process. The statute specifies completion within three months from application, extendable by two months with consent, and excludes the relevant mediation period for limitation purposes. A compliant settlement has the status/effect specified for an award on agreed terms.

Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022) affirmed the mandatory character of Section 12A and the consequence of non-compliance, subject to the judgment's prospective directions. Whether urgent interim relief is genuinely contemplated is legally assessed, not established merely by adding a routine urgent-relief sentence to the plaint.

Mandatory process versus voluntary result: The plaintiff may have to initiate and exhaust the statutory route, but nobody must accept an offer. A non-starter report or failure report is different from a settlement; litigation may then proceed as law permits.

17. Ordinary and Permanent Lok Adalats

17.1 Meaning, origin and purpose

Lok Adalat means people's court. Its modern organised development is associated with the Gujarat initiatives of 1982, followed by statutory recognition in the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. It combines access to justice, low cost and consensual settlement. It is not identical to an informal village panchayat.

National, State, district, taluk, mobile and e-Lok Adalat events describe levels or delivery formats. A National Lok Adalat is not a national appellate court. A frequently sitting ordinary Lok Adalat does not become a Chapter VI-A Permanent Lok Adalat merely because it operates regularly.

17.2 Ordinary Lok Adalat: Sections 19-22

Organisation: Competent State/District legal-services authorities, Supreme Court/High Court legal-services committees and Taluk committees organise Lok Adalats as the Act provides. Composition includes serving or retired judicial officers/judges and other prescribed persons. "Always exactly three members including one social worker and one lawyer" is not a universal statutory formula for every ordinary Lok Adalat.

Jurisdiction: Cases pending before a court and eligible pre-litigation matters within the relevant competent court's jurisdiction. Examples include civil recovery, motor-accident compensation, matrimonial monetary issues, utility/loan settlements and compoundable criminal cases, subject to legal suitability. It has no jurisdiction over offences not compoundable under law.

Reference: A pending case may be referred under Section 20 on the specified agreement/application/court-satisfaction conditions, with appropriate hearing. Pre-litigation reference may follow an application by one party and hearing to the other. Reference need not always begin with both parties jointly applying, but the final settlement requires genuine agreement.

No adjudication on disputed merits: An ordinary Lok Adalat cannot determine who is right and impose a payment simply because settlement seems desirable. In State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh (2008), the SC affirmed its conciliatory, not adjudicatory, function.

17.3 Award, costs, powers and remedies

Under Section 21, its award is deemed a decree of a civil court or, as applicable, an order of another court; it is final and binding on the parties and no statutory appeal lies. A settlement award is not merely moral advice.

There is no fee for bringing a matter before the Lok Adalat. Court fees paid in a referred case are refundable where settlement is reached, as the statutory provisions prescribe. This does not guarantee that every private expense incurred earlier will be reimbursed.

Section 22(1) grants specified procedural powers of a civil court, including summoning witnesses and requiring documents. That provision does not confer power to conduct a contested merits trial in an ordinary Lok Adalat. The class reference to Section 22(5) for these powers should be replaced with Section 22(1).

Finality is not immunity from fraud: Although an ordinary appeal is barred, limited constitutional challenge may be available for matters such as absence of consent, fraud or jurisdictional defects. It is not an ordinary second opportunity to renegotiate a valid settlement. Parties should understand the terms before signing.

17.4 Permanent Lok Adalat: Chapter VI-A, Sections 22A-22E

The 2002 amendment introduced Permanent Lok Adalats (PLAs) for public utility services. Under Section 22B, the Central Authority or a State Authority establishes them for specified areas and services. They are standing statutory bodies with a conciliation-first and conditionally adjudicatory role.

Composition: A Chairman who is or has been a District Judge, Additional District Judge or holder of a higher judicial office, plus two other persons with adequate public-utility-service experience appointed through the statutory process. This specific three-member model should not be projected onto every ordinary Lok Adalat.

Public utility services: The statutory definition includes transport of passengers/goods by air, road or water; postal/telegraph/telephone services; power, light or water supply; public conservancy/sanitation; hospital/dispensary services; insurance; and other services validly notified. A relevant service may be supplied by a private provider; the statute does not limit every PLA dispute to government ownership.

Pre-litigation only: Either party may apply before the dispute is brought before a court. Once an application is made, Section 22C restricts parties from invoking court jurisdiction for the same dispute as specified. PLAs are not the venue for transferring every already-pending suit.

Pecuniary limit: The original statutory ceiling was Rs 10 lakh; a 2015 Central notification raised it to Rs 1 crore. This is a dated reference, not confirmation that no subsequent notification has changed it. Check the current notification and how the value of the property in dispute is assessed.

17.5 Two stages, with important offence limits

  1. Conciliation: Receive statements and documents, help parties reach an independent and impartial settlement, formulate possible terms where appropriate, and invite observations. If parties agree, record the settlement and award.
  2. Adjudication after failed conciliation: If agreement fails, Section 22C(8) permits determination on merits only where the dispute does not relate to any offence, and the other jurisdictional conditions are satisfied.

Two different criminal limits: The PLA has no jurisdiction over a matter relating to a non-compoundable offence at the entry stage. Even for an otherwise eligible matter relating to an offence, failed conciliation does not authorise merits adjudication under Section 22C(8). The phrase "can adjudicate all matters except non-compoundable offences" misses this second restriction.

PLAs follow natural justice, objectivity, fair play and equity rather than being bound by the CPC and evidence-code formalities in the ordinary trial manner. This does not authorise arbitrary findings or skipping the mandatory conciliation stage. Under Section 22E, awards are final and binding and deemed civil-court decrees; ordinary appeal is not provided. Limited constitutional review remains distinct.

Bar Council of India v. Union of India (2012) upheld the PLA scheme. Its adjudicatory power after failed conciliation distinguishes it from the ordinary Lok Adalat considered in Jalour Singh.

17.7 Worked examples and assessment

Bank recovery before an ordinary Lok Adalat: A bank accepts instalments and reduced interest. The borrower's informed consent is needed; members cannot impose a compromise because a disposal target is approaching.

Insurance-service dispute before a PLA: An eligible pre-litigation claim within the value limit proceeds through conciliation. If conciliation fails and the statutory conditions are met, including the no-offence condition for adjudication, the PLA may decide it. Private ownership of the insurer is not by itself a jurisdictional bar.

Strengths: Affordability, speed, informality, enforceable outcomes and easier access. Concerns: Settlement pressure, unequal bargaining power, token participation, unsuitable referrals and inflated disposal statistics. Safeguards include legal advice, intelligible terms, proper consent records and judging success by fair compliance, not just awards counted.

19. Judicial Review, PIL, Activism and Reforms

This chapter connects the HC and district-level discussion to the broader concepts covered in the SC companion. Constitutional courts protect rights and legality; lower courts, specialised forums and ADR make those protections practically accessible.

19.1 Judicial review, activism and overreach

Judicial review: Testing legislation, constitutional amendments within the basic-structure doctrine, and executive action against constitutional/legal limits. Articles 13, 32 and 226 are important foundations; judicial review is not confined to these provisions alone.

Judicial activism: A description of an assertive judicial approach, often using broader interpretation, public-interest standing or continuing directions to protect rights. It is not a separate constitutional jurisdiction. Judicial overreach: A criticism that the court has crossed legitimate limits by taking over policy or administration without sufficient legal basis. Disagreement with a judgment alone does not establish overreach.

Constitutional map: Article 13 addresses laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights; Articles 32 and 226 provide rights-enforcement remedies; Articles 245-254 and the Seventh Schedule govern legislative competence and relevant conflicts; Article 368 concerns amendments. Articles 131, 132 and 136 provide other adjudicatory/appellate routes in their respective fields. Article 136 is discretionary and subject to its armed-forces exception, not a general right of appeal. Article 227 adds HC supervision, also with its armed-forces exception.

19.1C Basic structure: scope and examples

Kesavananda Bharati (1973) was decided by a 13-judge bench, with a 7:6 majority on the limitation of the amending power. Judicially recognised features include constitutional supremacy, democracy and republican government, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial independence/review, free and fair elections, limited amending power and harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. There is no exhaustive fixed list; the particular feature and the degree of damage must be established.

The doctrine primarily tests constitutional amendments. An ordinary statute is not ordinarily invalidated merely because a litigant asserts a free-standing basic-structure objection; it must be tested against constitutional limitations, rights and competence. Structural principles nevertheless inform that analysis, especially judicial independence and review. L. Chandra Kumar is therefore not permission to disregard structural safeguards when evaluating tribunal legislation.

Amendment correction: Article 13(4), excluding Article 368 amendments from Article 13, was inserted by the 24th Amendment, 1971, not the 42nd Amendment as one class-note correction states. This exclusion does not remove basic-structure review of amendments. The existence of judicial review does not depend on whether it appears as a named power in an Article.

19.1D Activism: development, drivers and examples

The term judicial activism is commonly traced to Arthur Schlesinger Jr.'s 1947 Fortune article about US Supreme Court justices. This dates the terminology, not the first assertive judicial decision in history. Indian activism became especially prominent in the late 1970s and 1980s through broader rights interpretation and easier access to constitutional courts. Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer and P.N. Bhagwati were important contributors, alongside the work of other judges, lawyers and public-interest organisations.

Drivers: Threats to Fundamental Rights; failure to implement statutory duties; gaps in protective legislation; expansion of Article 21; liberalised standing; and demands from disadvantaged communities. Governance instability, coalition bargaining and judicial philosophy are sometimes offered as explanatory factors, but neither coalition government nor a judge's personal preference independently authorises intervention.

19.1E Overreach debate and judicial restraint

Overreach is an evaluative criticism, not a settled legal label for every controversial order. Assess the source of judicial power, the right or duty being enforced, evidence, proportionality, affected parties' hearing, institutional competence and whether the remedy unnecessarily replaces lawful policy choices.

  • National anthem in cinemas: Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India included a November 2016 direction requiring its playing before screenings; the playing requirement was made optional in January 2018. Critics questioned judicial prescription of civic rituals; standing requirements and disability exemptions must be read with the actual orders.
  • Jolly LLB 2 (Bombay HC, 2017): Court-appointed scrutiny of an already certified film generated debate over free expression and substitution for the CBFC's statutory function. Courts can review certification legally; the question is whether the particular intervention went beyond judicial review into fresh censorship.
  • Government-school attendance direction (Allahabad HC, 2015): Directions concerning public officials' children were debated as a response to poor schools that intruded into individual choice and administration. This is a historical controversy, not a verified current compulsory service rule.
  • Firecracker litigation (2017-2018): Restrictions, licensing directions, green-cracker requirements and permitted-use windows raise competing concerns of health, environment, livelihood and implementation. Different orders had different territorial and substantive scope; describing them all as a nationwide blanket ban without legal basis is inaccurate.

Benefits of justified intervention: Rights protection, accountability, access for excluded groups and effective enforcement where ordinary administration has failed. Risks of excessive intervention: Displacement of democratic deliberation; inadequate technical expertise; unintended employment/fiscal effects; indefinite judicial administration; inconsistent directions; and diversion of court time. These are matters for evidence-based assessment, not proof that every activist judgment violates separation of powers.

Judicial restraint means respecting legitimate institutional roles while still enforcing the Constitution. Courts can require lawful action, hear experts, give proportionate and time-bound remedies and leave policy choices to the competent authority where appropriate. Article 50 specifically addresses separation in State public services; it is not a complete codification of all separation-of-powers questions. Judges lack electoral accountability but remain subject to reason-giving, appellate/review structures, institutional standards and constitutional removal mechanisms.

19.2 Public Interest Litigation

PIL relaxes traditional standing in appropriate cases so a public-spirited person or organisation may approach the SC/HC for persons unable to effectively seek justice themselves, or to address genuine public injury. Articles 32 and 226 are common routes. Judges including P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer played important roles in its development.

Courts have sometimes accepted letters, appointed commissions and monitored compliance. None of this means every letter must be registered as a PIL or that proof and hearing are unnecessary. The HC's Article 226 power extends beyond Fundamental Rights; the SC's Article 32 jurisdiction is for enforcing Fundamental Rights.

Benefits: Visibility for exclusion, environmental protection, prison reform and government accountability. Risks: Misuse, weak factual records, displacement of affected communities' voices, indefinite supervision and judicial intrusion into technical budget choices. State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) emphasised safeguards against PIL abuse.

19.2B Forms, techniques and admission safeguards

Representative standing: A petitioner acts for an identifiable disadvantaged group, such as undertrials or bonded workers. Citizen standing: A bona fide petitioner raises broader public injury, such as environmental harm. These are explanatory categories, not two exhaustive statutory forms with automatic admission rights.

Suo motu proceedings: A court initiates consideration on its own information, sometimes a report or communication. This is a mode of initiation, not the same as a citizen's standing. Epistolary jurisdiction: Treating an appropriate letter as a petition. Continuing mandamus: Retaining a matter to monitor compliance through further orders rather than disposing of it through one final direction.

Admission safeguards include verifying credentials, reliable facts, genuine public injury, absence of concealed private interests and availability of appropriate remedies. Courts may dismiss abusive petitions with costs. Ordinary private disputes and routine service disputes are generally not PIL matters; quo warranto is a distinct recognised exception in the service-context discussion. Publicity, disagreement with policy or a newspaper report alone does not prove a constitutional violation.

19.3 Reform agenda across the justice system

  • Capacity: Timely appointments, adequate judges/support staff, courtrooms, interpreters, forensic capacity and legal-aid lawyers.
  • Case management: Early identification of issues, meaningful schedules, controlled adjournments, differentiated tracks and prompt execution of decrees.
  • Government litigation: Reasoned decisions, consistent policies, lawful settlement authority and avoidance of routine appeals without merit.
  • Technology: e-filing, searchable orders, virtual participation where suitable and accessible records, with privacy, cybersecurity and offline assistance.
  • Independence with accountability: Transparent institutional processes, reasoned decisions, conflict rules, ethical standards and constitutionally sound complaints/disciplinary mechanisms.
  • Inclusive access: Disability access, translation, gender-sensitive facilities, victim/witness support, early defence counsel and rural outreach.
  • ADR and specialisation: Suitable referrals, competent neutrals, functional Family/Gram courts and independent tribunals, with ordinary adjudication available where settlement is inappropriate.
  • Measurement: Track age of cases, time to interim relief, compliance, fairness and user experience, not only raw disposal counts.

Balance: Article 50 supports separation of judiciary from executive in State public services; Articles 14, 21 and 39A frame equal and effective access. Faster disposal is valuable only when it remains lawful, independent and fair.

19.3A Class-specific reform details

Language: Article 348. Unless Parliament otherwise provides, SC/HC proceedings and specified authoritative legal texts use English. Article 348(2) allows a Governor, with the President's previous consent, to authorise Hindi or another State official language in HC proceedings, but that clause does not itself extend to judgments, decrees or orders. Section 7 of the Official Languages Act, 1963 separately provides an authorisation route for those HC outputs, with an English translation under HC authority. Subordinate-court language follows applicable law/rules. Translation, plain-language orders and interpretation can improve access without falsely saying every HC proceeding must always be only in English.

Strength and workload: The Law Commission's 120th Report (1987) is associated with the recommendation of 50 judges per million population. The 245th Report (2014) examined arrears/backlog and judicial manpower using workload/rate-of-disposal considerations; it should not be credited with originating that ratio. Population ratios are a rough indicator, not a substitute for case type, filing rates, complexity and support capacity. Current ratios and vacancy percentages require dated data.

Calendar and working hours: Proposals include improved scheduling, staggered leave and better use of available court time. Hearing hours alone exclude preparation, judgment writing and administrative work; annual sitting-day figures vary by court/year and should not be treated as a uniform national rule. Increasing hours without supporting staff or reducing avoidable procedure may have limited effect.

Drafting and accountability: Clearer laws, coordinated amendments and impact assessment can reduce avoidable disputes. A transparent complaints process for misconduct below the removal threshold addresses a different problem from appellate correction of a wrong judgment. In-house procedures are not a substitute for constitutional removal, and proposed Judicial Standards and Accountability legislation must not be described as operative merely because a Bill was introduced.

e-Courts and NJDG: The National Judicial Data Grid provides court-data dashboards useful for examining institution, disposal, pendency and case age. Record the date, court level and reporting coverage when quoting a figure. Data quality and updating intervals matter; a dashboard count is not proof that a reform caused delay or improvement. Digital transcription, document search and case management should retain human verification, privacy and access for litigants without reliable internet.

R3. Twenty common exam traps

  1. Every State must have a separate HC? No. Article 231 permits a common HC.
  2. HC judges are appointed by the Governor? No. Appointment is by the President under Article 217; the Governor is a constitutional consultee.
  3. Collegium is written verbatim in Article 217? No. It developed through judicial interpretation and established procedures.
  4. NJAC is the present appointment mechanism? No. The 99th Amendment/NJAC framework was invalidated in 2015.
  5. Article 224A request is made by the CJI? No. It is the HC Chief Justice's request, with the required prior presidential consent and retired judge's consent.
  6. HC writ jurisdiction is narrower because the HC is lower? No. Article 226 also reaches other legal rights, subject to public-law and jurisdictional requirements.
  7. Article 227 is a routine appeal on all facts? No. Superintendence is not unrestricted appellate reconsideration.
  8. All HC contempt appeals go directly to the SC? No. The statutory route depends on the order and whether it is of a single judge or bench.
  9. District Magistrate and District Judge are the same? No. Executive and judicial offices are distinct.
  10. Civil pecuniary limits are identical across India? No. Check State statutes and notifications.
  11. A Sessions death sentence can be executed immediately? No. HC confirmation and other lawful safeguards apply.
  12. Summary trial means no fair hearing? No. It is abbreviated lawful procedure, not absence of justice.
  13. Every legal-aid applicant must meet an income test? No. Separate Section 12 categories also qualify, subject to Section 13.
  14. A Family Court can only mediate? No. It can adjudicate after unsuccessful settlement efforts.
  15. Fast-track status creates greater punishment powers? No. The court's legal designation and statute determine powers.
  16. Every tribunal bypasses HC constitutional review? No. L. Chandra Kumar protects constitutional review.
  17. A mediator or conciliator can impose fair terms? No. Parties must accept a settlement.
  18. An arbitration award requires the loser to sign acceptance? No. Consent to arbitration is distinct from consent to the result.
  19. An ordinary Lok Adalat can decide merits if talks fail? No. The conditional adjudicatory role belongs to a qualifying PLA, not an ordinary Lok Adalat.
  20. Mediation is mandatory before every civil suit after 2023? No. Distinguish the general enacted framework from commercial Section 12A and verify commencement.

R4. Quick self-test

1. A citizen seeks enforcement of a statutory public duty, without alleging a Fundamental Right violation. Is Article 32 automatically available?
Answer: No. Article 32 enforces Fundamental Rights. An Article 226 remedy may be appropriate if the public-law, territorial and other requirements are satisfied.

2. A State wants to shift an HC judge to another HC. Can a State order achieve this?
Answer: No. Article 222 places transfer with the President after consultation with the CJI, interpreted through the applicable judicial consultation framework.

3. A junior civil court receives a high-value suit exceeding its notified limit. Does territorial connection alone suffice?
Answer: No. Subject-matter, territorial and pecuniary competence must all be addressed. Applicable procedural orders follow from the defect.

4. A JMFC sees a five-year maximum in the offence provision. May it impose five years using its general BNSS powers?
Answer: No. The offence maximum and the court's Section 23 sentencing competence are distinct; the lawful procedure for a higher sentence must be followed.

5. A Family Court gives an appealable final contested decree. Does the ordinary appeal go to the District Judge?
Answer: No. The Family Courts Act provides the relevant appeal to the HC, subject to Section 19 exclusions and conditions.

6. The mediator proposes Rs 80,000. One party refuses. Is there a binding award?
Answer: No. A proposed mediated settlement needs acceptance; the mediator is not an arbitrator.

7. Both parties sign a qualifying Part III conciliation settlement. Is it merely a non-binding recommendation?
Answer: No. Sections 73-74 give the statutory settlement binding status/effect, subject to the applicable commencement/amendment framework.

8. A party files a Section 34 challenge. Is award enforcement automatically stayed?
Answer: No. A separate stay must be obtained under the applicable statutory conditions.

9. An ordinary Lok Adalat announces a compromise amount despite express refusal. What is wrong?
Answer: It has exceeded its settlement role. Genuine consent is essential; lack of consent may support the legally available limited challenge.

10. A utility dispute is already before a civil court. Can the parties simply treat it as a fresh PLA case?
Answer: No. Chapter VI-A PLA jurisdiction is pre-litigation; an ordinary Lok Adalat reference is a different possibility.

11. PLA conciliation fails in a dispute relating to an offence. Does compoundability alone authorise a merits award?
Answer: No. Section 22C(8) permits post-conciliation adjudication only where the dispute does not relate to any offence.

12. A commercial plaintiff wants urgent interim protection. Must Section 12A always be exhausted first?
Answer: The statute excludes suits contemplating urgent interim relief from that requirement, but the claim of urgency is legally assessed; a formulaic assertion is insufficient.

R5. Mains-answer frameworks

"Discuss the High Court as guardian of constitutionalism at State level."
Start with Articles 214/226 → explain writs and judicial review → distinguish appeals, superintendence and Article 235 control → discuss independence safeguards → address vacancies, access and accountability → conclude with constitutional protection plus effective administration of justice.

"Can ADR solve India's judicial backlog?"
Define consensual and adjudicatory methods → explain suitable-dispute diversion and early resolution → use Section 89, Section 12A and Lok Adalats as examples → address coercion, quality, non-arbitrability and enforcement → recommend capacity, screening, legal aid and data → conclude that ADR complements, not replaces, independent courts.

"Assess specialised courts and tribunals as access-to-justice reforms."
State the specialisation rationale → compare Family Courts, FTSCs, Gram Nyayalayas and tribunals → distinguish judicial decision-making from settlement → examine staffing, tenure, independence, geography and appeals → apply Articles 14/21/39A and L. Chandra Kumar → favour functional institutions over new labels.
