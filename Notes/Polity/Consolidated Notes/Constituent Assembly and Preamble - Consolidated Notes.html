Part I / Constituent Assembly

1. Why a Constituent Assembly?

A Constituent Assembly is a body entrusted with framing a Constitution. India's Assembly was intended to replace British-imposed constitutional arrangements with a framework made by Indians for their own government.

  • End externally imposed constitutions: the Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935 were enacted by the British Parliament, not by an Indian constitution-making body.
  • Popular sovereignty: the national movement demanded that the people of India determine their political future.
  • Move beyond political independence: rights, equality, minority protection, social justice and accountable institutions required an agreed constitutional settlement.
  • Unite a diverse country: provinces and princely states needed a common framework accommodating territorial, linguistic, religious and social diversity.
Significance: Constitution-making transformed the demand for freedom from British rule into a positive programme for democratic government and social transformation.

Detailed Importance: From Self-Rule to Constitutional Government

Who makes the rules matters: independence answers who governs a country; constitution-making answers how authority is organised, limited and made accountable. An Indian ministry operating under a British-enacted framework would not, by itself, satisfy the demand that Indians determine the foundations of government.

Constituent power versus ordinary legislative power: an ordinary legislature makes laws within an existing Constitution. A constitution-making body establishes that foundational framework. India's Assembly later performed both functions, but its constitution-making role must not be confused with its separate legislative work.

Need for a shared settlement: a diverse population could disagree about religion, language, representation and economic policy while still agree on institutions through which future disagreements would be resolved. The Constitution therefore supplied both substantive commitments and peaceful decision-making procedures.

Answer-writing note: Explain the demand through three connected ideas: freedom from external control, authority derived from Indians, and institutions capable of protecting rights and accommodating diversity.

Why Each Stage Was a Distinct Advance

  • Nehru Report to Roy's proposal: an Indian-authored constitutional proposal demonstrated initiative, while the Assembly demand addressed the authority and representative procedure by which a Constitution should be made. A report and a constituent body are different things.
  • Congress's demand and Nehru's clarification: official political adoption gave the proposal organisational backing; adult franchise and freedom from outside interference specified its desired democratic character. The eventual 1946 selection method fell short of that ideal.
  • August Offer: recognition of Indian constitution-making in principle was a concession, but postponement until after the war and disagreement over political safeguards left the central conflict unresolved.
  • Cripps Mission: moving from principle towards a proposed mechanism mattered. However, delayed transfer and the provincial opt-out provision raised questions about immediate authority and national unity, while the League sought clearer acceptance of Pakistan.
  • Wavell and Simla: a dispute over executive representation revealed how difficult agreement on who could speak for communities had become. This explains the political context; it should not be presented as the legal origin of the Assembly.
  • Elections and Cabinet Mission: the provincial elections created the electoral bodies used by the Mission's scheme. The resulting Assembly was an institutional outcome, not simply another promise of future constitution-making.

Timeline method: For each event distinguish the demand, British response, proposed machinery and actual implementation. These stages were related but did not occur on the same date.

What the Composition Tells Us

Why indirect election matters: voters did not cast a separate universal ballot to choose constitution-makers. The elected provincial legislators selected representatives. Proportional representation by single transferable vote could accommodate more than a simple winner-takes-all selection, but did not remove the limitations of the underlying franchise.

Why princely-state representation differed: these territories did not share the same representative institutions as British Indian provinces. Negotiated participation brought them into a common process, but nomination raised a separate question about democratic accountability.

Why Partition changed more than numbers: it changed the territory and population represented and weakened the basis for the Cabinet Mission's limited-Union compromise. Stronger Union institutions became more prominent in the final constitutional settlement.

Representation is not the same as unanimity: participation by different communities does not prove that every view was represented equally. Conversely, a large party majority does not prove that all members held identical constitutional views.

Three different numbers: 389 and 299 describe allotted membership at different stages; 284 describes the members who signed on 24 January 1950. Do not use any of them as an automatic attendance figure for every sitting.

4. Indian Independence Act, 1947: Impact

Royal assent: 18 July 1947. Appointed day for the transfer: 15 August 1947. The Act supplied the legal framework for ending British rule and creating the independent Dominions of India and Pakistan.

ChangeImpact and significance
Two independent DominionsPartitioned British India, including Punjab and Bengal. Independence did not immediately make India a republic.

Constitution-making authorityThe Assembly could determine the independent Dominion's Constitution without British parliamentary approval; the Cabinet Mission's proposed framework no longer controlled its choices.
Legislative roleThe Assembly also exercised Dominion legislative powers. Constitution-making and ordinary legislative work were conducted in separate capacities.

Tribal agreementsBritish treaties and arrangements with tribal authorities also lapsed, requiring new arrangements.

Wider consequences: Partition brought mass displacement, communal violence, asset and military division, boundary disputes and urgent princely-state integration. These belong to the wider transfer-of-power process; not every consequence was an express statutory provision.

The Act did not create the Assembly. The Assembly already existed under the Cabinet Mission Plan. Independence transformed its authority into sovereign constitution-making power.

Detailed Constitutional Significance

Legal break with political continuity: the source of ultimate authority changed, but existing administration and laws could continue until altered. This avoided the impossible task of replacing every rule, court and public service on a single day. Continuity of an old law did not mean continuation of British sovereignty.

Dominion versus republic: between independence and 26 January 1950, India was independent while retaining the Crown within its formal constitutional structure. Becoming a republic removed hereditary Crown headship and introduced the constitutional presidency. Independence and republicanism answer different questions.

Lapse of paramountcy versus accession: ending British suzerainty removed an old legal relationship; it did not itself create the new relationship between each princely state and India. Accession and subsequent integration required separate instruments and political negotiations.

Two roles of the Assembly: constitution-making established the future legal order, while legislative work addressed immediate governing needs. The same institution could perform these functions without making ordinary legislation identical to a constitutional provision.

Cause-and-consequence distinction: The Act provided transfer-of-power machinery. Partition violence and displacement were consequences of the wider historical process, not commands in the Act. Keep legal effects separate from social consequences in an answer.

Why the Stages of Enactment Matter

General debate before clause-by-clause debate: members first considered the overall design, then examined individual provisions, and finally assessed the completed document. These stages separated disagreement about constitutional principles from disagreement about wording and implementation.

Publication and amendments: making the draft available for comment opened the process beyond the drafting group. Committee expertise was checked against Assembly debate and external suggestions. This supports an argument for deliberative legitimacy, though it did not replace direct election.

Adoption is not full commencement: adoption settled the constitutional text and commitment; Article 394 governed when provisions took legal effect. Immediate provisions supported the transition, while the principal institutions of the republic began under the fully operative Constitution on 26 January 1950.

Signing and symbols: signing formally authenticated the constitutional achievement; the Flag, Anthem statement and language compromise also helped define common public identity. These acts should not all be described as constitutional amendments or treated as happening on adoption day.

Article 395: repeal of the old governing enactments marked constitutional replacement, not wholesale deletion of every pre-existing law. The new Constitution supplied the authority under which legal continuity and future change would operate.

Overall significance: Division of work enabled specialisation, simultaneous consideration of subjects and focused attention to disadvantaged communities. Drafting brought these proposals into a coherent text, without displacing the Assembly's final authority.

Detailed Importance of Each Major Committee

  • Union Powers: deciding which level could legislate was essential to preventing jurisdictional uncertainty. Its importance lies in allocation of authority, not merely in designing ministries or parliamentary offices.
  • Union Constitution: allocation alone cannot explain how power is exercised. The executive-legislature relationship needed institutions that could govern while remaining answerable to representatives.
  • Provincial Constitution: national democracy required responsible government in the provinces as well. The committee's concerns connected regional administration with ministerial responsibility and representative legislatures.
  • Advisory Committee: majority government needed safeguards against exclusion. Bringing rights, minorities and tribal areas into focused deliberation ensured that nation-building included protections rather than only central institutions.
  • Drafting Committee: a political agreement must become precise legal language. Consistency, definitions, exceptions and remedies affect whether an agreed principle can actually operate.
  • Rules of Procedure: agreed rules determine who can move amendments and how decisions are reached. Procedure protects orderly participation and makes the resulting decisions intelligible and legitimate.
  • States Committee: participation by princely states expanded the territorial basis of constitution-making. Representation negotiations were distinct from the government's task of securing accession and political integration.
  • Steering Committee: coordination ensured that a large programme of reports and debates could progress. It organised business rather than acquiring an independent power to decide the final Constitution.

Why the Subcommittees Were Necessary

The Fundamental Rights Subcommittee considered what protections should exist and how they might be enforced. The Minorities Subcommittee confronted the distinction between protection of a community's freedoms and separate political electorates. The two tribal-area subcommittees recognised that uniform administrative rules could overlook different local institutions and circumstances. Their contribution supports differentiated safeguards, not the assumption that every recommendation was adopted unchanged.

The process was iterative: the Drafting Committee checked consistency, refined wording, considered suggestions and assisted debate. Ambedkar played a central role in explaining and defending the draft. The Constitution was a collective achievement, not the work of one person or committee alone.

Why Drafting Was More Than Writing

Translate principle into an enforceable rule: a commitment to liberty needs clarity about its beneficiaries, permitted restrictions, institutions responsible for enforcement and available remedies. Drafting determines these practical relationships rather than merely choosing elegant language.

Resolve inconsistencies: recommendations from different committees could overlap or pull in different directions. The draft needed to work as one constitutional system, including the relationship between rights, government powers and transitional provisions.

Keep responsibility correctly attributed: Rau's comparative and preparatory work, Ambedkar's legal and explanatory leadership, other members' contributions and the Assembly's amendments all mattered. Recognising Ambedkar's central role need not erase the collective process.

Exam note: Committee appointment, draft preparation, publication, introduction, adoption and commencement are six distinct milestones. A question asking when the Drafting Committee was formed is answered by 29 August 1947, not February or November 1948.

The Constitutional Problem Behind Each Choice

  • Parliamentary responsibility: electoral legitimacy had to be connected to continuing accountability. Ministers answer to the legislature rather than remaining politically unaccountable between elections.
  • Strong-Centre federalism: Partition and integration encouraged coordination, but elected states and divided powers also acknowledged regional government. National unity and state autonomy were to coexist within the Constitution.
  • Adult franchise: rejecting property and literacy tests made citizenship, not privilege, the basis of electoral participation. Political equality was adopted despite severe social and economic inequality.
  • Rights and remedies: a declaration of freedom without a route to enforcement could remain ineffective. Judicial remedies made unconstitutional state action contestable.
  • Article 17: formal independence would be incomplete if inherited social exclusion remained legally accepted. Abolition of untouchability directly challenged a system of unequal status.
  • Directive Principles: not every welfare goal could be made an immediately claimable judicial remedy. The Constitution nevertheless made these goals fundamental to governance rather than leaving social policy without constitutional direction.
  • Minority protection: cultural and educational safeguards allowed shared citizenship without demanding assimilation. Rejecting religious separate electorates did not mean rejecting all protective representation.
  • Judicial independence: rights and division of powers needed an adjudicator capable of deciding against the government. Review limited public power while leaving constitutionally lawful policy choices to elected institutions.
  • Single citizenship: national membership was common, even though federal governments operated at different levels. It did not eliminate every lawful residence-based distinction.
  • Language settlement: constitutional compromise recognised political aspirations while avoiding abrupt administrative disruption. Official-language designation must be kept distinct from a claim of linguistic superiority.

Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru: 13 December 1946. Adopted unanimously: 22 January 1947. It declared the aims of the future constitutional order, providing common direction before detailed drafting. It was not an independently enforceable law.

Legacy: Popular sovereignty became "We, the People"; justice, equality and liberty informed the Preamble, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; minority safeguards received substantive provisions; world peace finds expression in Article 51.

Resolution is not Preamble. It was the principal philosophical foundation, not a word-for-word predecessor. The final Preamble does not reproduce all the Resolution's freedoms, territorial details or institutional proposals. Declaring an intended republic in 1947 did not itself make India a republic.

Detailed Importance of the Resolution

A common direction before detailed drafting: members needed to know what government was for before deciding its exact form. Sovereignty, justice, equality and safeguards provided standards against which institutional proposals could be assessed.

A shift in the source of authority: power derived from the people, not from the British Crown or the claims of hereditary rulers. This supplied the conceptual connection to "We, the People" even though the Assembly itself had been indirectly constituted.

A social as well as political programme: the Resolution joined independence with economic and social justice. This explains why the final Constitution contains both political institutions and commitments to welfare, equality and protection from exclusion.

A framework capable of development: the shift from unit-level residuary powers to Union residuary powers demonstrates that foundational goals and institutional proposals are not identical. The Assembly could retain popular sovereignty and justice while changing the distribution of powers as circumstances changed.

Domestic and international responsibilities: minority safeguards addressed inclusion at home; the peace and human-welfare clause situated independent India within a wider world order.

Use in an answer: Call it the philosophical foundation and guiding declaration of constitution-making, then illustrate both continuity (justice and popular sovereignty) and change (residuary powers). Avoid saying that the entire Resolution became enforceable constitutional law.

How to Evaluate the Criticisms

Separate origin, process and outcome: restricted elections are a criticism of selection; excessive length concerns the resulting text; committee debate concerns the quality of deliberation. Evidence supporting one dimension does not automatically settle the others.

Do not use outcomes to deny limitations: universal adult suffrage in the final Constitution does not retrospectively make the Assembly directly elected. It does show that its constitutional design could move beyond its own electoral foundations.

Do not use limitations to dismiss every achievement: indirect election and a party majority do not prove the absence of substantive disagreement, careful drafting or rights protections. A balanced assessment tests those claims against the process and the text.

Centralisation and detail involved trade-offs: stability, integration and administrative continuity supported some choices, while regional autonomy and accessibility supported the criticisms. State the competing considerations instead of presenting either side as obviously conclusive.

Mains structure: State the criticism accurately, acknowledge its factual basis, give the relevant qualification, and end with a reasoned assessment of the Assembly's contribution.

Part II / Preamble

11. Preamble: Text, Need and Constitutional Position

The Preamble is the Constitution's introductory statement. It identifies the source of authority, nature of the State, objectives and date of adoption. A preamble is not a necessary legal component of every constitution, but India's Preamble explains the philosophy behind its detailed provisions.

Class terminology: "Preamble" means an introductory statement or preface, not "pre + amble" as movement through a Constitution. Its connection with the Objectives Resolution is philosophical and substantive, not a claim that the two texts are identical.

Present Text

WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:

JUSTICE, social, economic and political;

LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;

EQUALITY of status and of opportunity;

and to promote among them all

FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;

IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

Why Is It Important?

  • Legitimacy: identifies the people as the source of constitutional authority.
  • Philosophy: explains the ends served by legal rules and institutions.
  • Shared commitments: supplies common goals across India's diversity.
  • Social transformation: links independence with justice and inclusion.
  • Interpretation: assists courts in understanding constitutional purpose and ambiguity.
  • Public accountability: provides values against which citizens can assess governmental action.
Current legal position: Part of the Constitution; amendable subject to basic structure; not independently enforceable; not an independent source of governmental power. Its interpretive importance does not permit it to override clear constitutional provisions.

Detailed Importance: More Than an Introduction

Rules need purposes: a provision establishing an institution tells us how authority is exercised. The Preamble helps explain why that authority exists: to sustain a democratic republic committed to justice, freedom, equality and common citizenship. It connects otherwise separate chapters into a constitutional project.

A guide, not a substitute: where constitutional language permits competing interpretations, its values help identify the reading consistent with the constitutional scheme. But an institution cannot claim a new power simply because its action is said to promote justice or unity.

Interpretive example: a claim of unequal treatment must be examined through an applicable provision such as Article 14. The Preamble's equality and dignity commitments illuminate the constitutional purpose; they do not replace the legal tests under that provision.

Political and educational value: the Preamble gives citizens a common vocabulary to evaluate public action. Its goals remain relevant even when a particular demand is not independently enforceable in court. Legal enforceability and constitutional importance are different questions.

Four-part reading: "We, the People" supplies authority; the five State descriptors identify the constitutional order; justice, liberty, equality and fraternity identify goals; the final sentence records adoption and self-enactment.

12. Keywords: Authority and Nature of the State

"We, the People of India"

Popular sovereignty: ultimate constitutional authority rests with the people. The Assembly acted as the constitution-making body; no nationwide referendum adopted the text. Public institutions derive their authority from the Constitution, not an unlimited claim to represent the people.

"Solemnly Resolved"

A deliberate, serious commitment to a lasting constitutional order, rather than a temporary governmental programme.

Sovereign

India independently governs internal affairs and conducts external relations without legal subordination to another State. Voluntary UN or Commonwealth membership does not itself destroy sovereignty; the Commonwealth does not place republican India under the British Crown. Sovereignty does not mean every governmental institution is unlimited or that international obligations can be ignored.

Socialist

A commitment to social and economic justice, reducing inequality and protecting welfare through constitutional methods. Indian constitutional socialism permits private enterprise and different economic policies; it does not mandate a wholly state-owned economy. Articles 38, 39, 39A and 41-43 connect the goal with livelihoods, fair distribution and welfare. The word was added in 1976, but these commitments predated it.

Secular

No constitutionally established State religion; equal citizenship and freedom of conscience and religion. Indian secularism is neither hostility to religion nor an absolute rule of non-intervention. The State may regulate secular activities associated with religion and undertake social reform within constitutional limits.

ProvisionRelevance
Articles 14-16Equality and protection from specified discrimination, including religious discrimination. Article 16(5) permits specified religious qualifications for offices connected with religious institutions.
Article 25Freedom of conscience and to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other Fundamental Rights; clause (2) permits specified regulation and reform.

Democratic

Authority derives from people through representation, elections, public debate, accountability and peaceful changes of government. India primarily follows parliamentary representative democracy, not a general national referendum system. Article 326 provides adult suffrage; the 61st Amendment reduced the voting age from 21 to 18. Democracy includes rights, opposition and minority safeguards, not unlimited majority rule.

Republic

The head of State is elected, not hereditary. India's President is indirectly elected under Articles 54-55 for a five-year term. A republic need not have a presidential executive: India is a parliamentary republic. Republicanism and popular sovereignty are related but not synonymous.

Detailed Importance: "We, the People" and "Solemnly Resolved"

The opening rejects the idea of a Constitution bestowed as a favour by a ruler. It also explains why elected representatives remain trustees of constitutionally limited authority, not owners of the State. A temporary electoral majority is not identical to the enduring constituent authority of the people.

"Solemnly resolved" underscores the seriousness of that commitment. It does not prevent lawful amendment; it distinguishes foundational constitutional commitments from a government's changeable electoral programme.

Detailed Importance: Sovereign

Internal dimension: India's institutions govern within an independent constitutional order. External dimension: India can establish diplomatic relations, enter agreements and determine foreign policy without legal subordination to another country. Constitutional limits on domestic institutions do not contradict national sovereignty.

Example: voluntarily entering an international organisation exercises sovereign choice; it does not automatically transfer ultimate constitutional authority to that organisation. The obligations undertaken still matter. Independence is not a claim that cooperation and international law are irrelevant.

Detailed Importance: Socialist

Formal freedom may offer little practical opportunity to a person without education, livelihood or basic security. The socialist commitment supplies a constitutional reason to address these inequalities through welfare, public services, fair distribution and protection against exploitation.

Indian policy space: public provision, regulated private enterprise and market reforms must be assessed in their constitutional setting. Neither nationalisation nor privatisation is automatically required or forbidden merely by the word "socialist". The 2024 Balram Singh decision reinforces that the word does not freeze one economic model.

Exam distinction: the word expresses an objective and informs interpretation; an individual claim to a particular benefit still needs an applicable legal basis. Justice-oriented governance is broader than independently enforceable entitlements.

Detailed Importance: Secular

Secularism protects both freedom from religious coercion and freedom to practise a religion within constitutional limits. A person's standing as a citizen must not depend on following the majority's religion, or any religion at all.

Why intervention can be compatible with secularism: regulation of a religious institution's secular administration is not necessarily the same as prescribing religious belief. Reform can address exclusion, but it must be justified under the Constitution. Calling an action reform does not exempt it from rights review.

Importance of minority protections: numerical democracy can otherwise create pressure to assimilate. Cultural and educational protections help make common citizenship compatible with continuing religious and linguistic differences.

Detailed Importance: Democratic

Elections allow peaceful replacement of rulers; legislative accountability requires explanation between elections; freedom of expression allows criticism; opposition makes alternatives visible. These elements work together. An election without meaningful competition or basic freedoms cannot fulfil the entire constitutional idea of democracy.

Political and social democracy: equal voting status is essential, but entrenched social exclusion and deprivation can obstruct effective participation. The justice and equality clauses therefore support, rather than distract from, the democratic commitment.

Exam distinction: Article 326 supplies the adult-suffrage foundation. It should not be cited as automatically making voting a Fundamental Right; electoral entitlements and their exercise are also governed by election law.

Detailed Importance: Republic

Public office belongs to the constitutional community rather than a hereditary family. The presidency's selection through an electoral college shows that republicanism can operate through indirect election. It does not require the President to exercise the real executive authority found in a presidential system.

Comparison: democratic constitutional monarchies demonstrate why democracy and republic must be distinguished. Conversely, merely calling a country a republic does not prove that it has free elections or effective rights. India's Preamble deliberately combines both commitments.

13. Keywords: Goals, Dignity and National Cohesion

Justice: Social, Economic and Political

DimensionMeaning and Indian context
SocialEnd caste and other status-based exclusion; equal dignity and membership. Articles 14-18, especially Article 17, and Article 38 are important links.
EconomicFair access to livelihood and resources; reduction of deprivation and harmful concentration. Articles 38, 39, 39A and 41-43; does not require identical incomes.
PoliticalEqual political citizenship and meaningful participation; Articles 325-326 and the representative system. Electoral eligibility remains subject to lawful qualifications.

These dimensions reinforce one another. Their relationship is a constitutional and social argument, not a literal rule that failure in one automatically eliminates the others. Judicial diversity is important, but judges are not elected political representatives.

Liberty: Thought, Expression, Belief, Faith and Worship

  • Thought: freedom to think, question and form opinions, linked with autonomy.
  • Expression: communication of ideas, including government criticism; Article 19(1)(a), subject to Article 19(2).
  • Belief and faith: intellectual and religious conviction and freedom of conscience.
  • Worship: religious observance within constitutional limitations.

Liberty is not unlimited licence. Restrictions need constitutional authority and must satisfy applicable standards. The Preamble states economic justice, not "economic liberty".

Equality of Status and Opportunity

Status: equal civic worth and legal standing, not inferior citizenship based on caste, religion or sex. Opportunity: fair access to education, employment and public life, with Article 16 specifically addressing public employment. Equality permits reasonable classification and constitutionally authorised affirmative action; identical treatment can perpetuate existing disadvantage.

Fraternity

Common citizenship, solidarity and mutual respect across differences. Article 51A(e) connects harmony and common brotherhood with rejection of practices derogatory to women's dignity. Fraternity opposes caste exclusion and communal hatred without requiring cultural uniformity.

Dignity of the Individual

Every person has intrinsic worth and must not be treated as inferior or merely as an instrument. Dignity informs equality, the abolition of untouchability and Article 21 jurisprudence on autonomy, privacy and life with dignity.

Unity and Integrity of the Nation

Unity: common national belonging despite diversity, compatible with federalism and regional autonomy. Integrity: national wholeness, cohesion and territorial integrity. The latter word was added by the 42nd Amendment. Neither authorises unconstitutional suppression of dissent.

"Adopt, Enact and Give to Ourselves"

Adopt: acceptance of the Constitution. Enact: give it legal form and authority. Give to ourselves: self-government rather than an externally granted charter. The date 26 November 1949 records adoption; it does not mean every provision commenced that day.

Relationship of values: Liberty without equality can sustain privilege; equality without liberty can suppress individuality; both need fraternity to sustain democratic society. Ambedkar's 25 November 1949 speech described liberty, equality and fraternity as a "union of trinity".

Detailed Importance: The Three Dimensions of Justice

Social justice challenges the assumption that birth determines a person's worth. Abolition of untouchability is therefore more than an administrative reform: it rejects a system of unequal membership. Equal access and constitutionally authorised safeguards seek to make citizenship meaningful for historically excluded groups.

Economic justice concerns opportunities, livelihood and distribution, not simply charity after deprivation occurs. The Directive Principles ask government to address the conditions that produce inequality. Access to justice also matters: a person unable to afford legal assistance may lack a practical remedy despite a formal right, explaining Article 39A's significance.

Political justice rejects property, educational or social privilege as the basis of electoral worth. It requires meaningful participation, not a guarantee that every person will hold office. Political equality is reinforced when citizens can obtain information, express disagreement and organise lawfully.

Detailed Importance: Each Aspect of Liberty

  • Thought: independent reflection supports personal development, scientific inquiry and the ability to question established opinions.
  • Expression: ideas must be communicable for public debate and governmental accountability. Protection of criticism is especially important because popular views ordinarily face less pressure.
  • Belief: protects conviction and conscience rather than permitting public authority to prescribe every accepted idea.
  • Faith: affirms that religious commitment is a matter of conscience, consistent with equal freedom for those with different commitments.
  • Worship: connects inward conviction with outward observance. Public-order, health and other constitutional limitations govern conduct; the mere existence of a restriction does not settle whether it is valid.

Note: These five words express connected freedoms, not five separately numbered Fundamental Rights. The operative Articles determine beneficiaries, restrictions and remedies.

Detailed Importance: Equality of Status and Opportunity

Status concerns standing: a person is not a lesser citizen because of caste, religion or sex. Opportunity concerns access: apparently open institutions can remain inaccessible where historical disadvantage persists. Constitutionally authorised special provisions may address those barriers without abandoning equality.

Example: treating an inaccessible educational facility as equally available to everyone does not necessarily provide real access. The analytical point is that relevant differences may justify measures aimed at inclusion, subject to constitutional requirements. Equal opportunity does not guarantee identical outcomes.

Detailed Importance: Fraternity and Dignity

Fraternity gives equality a social foundation: legal rules work better when citizens recognise each other as members of the same political community. Caste humiliation and communal exclusion weaken this relationship even when formal institutions continue to exist.

Dignity limits instrumental treatment: an individual cannot be regarded merely as a means to an economic or political objective. It explains why liberty and equality concern personal worth, not only administrative efficiency. Dignity is not dependent on wealth, office or majority approval.

Detailed Importance: Unity and Integrity

The Preamble connects national cohesion with the dignity of individuals through fraternity. This is significant: unity is not presented only as territorial control. Inclusion and mutual respect help sustain the nation, while federal accommodation allows regional identity within a common constitutional order.

Limit: the invocation of integrity does not dispense with lawful restrictions and judicial scrutiny. Criticism of a government is not, merely because it is criticism, rejection of the nation.

Detailed Importance: Adoption and Self-Enactment

The closing sentence joins a historical event with a claim of legitimate authority. "Adopt" records acceptance; "enact" expresses legal establishment; "give to ourselves" rejects external grant. The original adoption date remains a historical record even after a valid amendment changes wording elsewhere in the Preamble.

15. Amendment of the Preamble and Related Developments

Only the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 changed the Preamble's text. Its Preamble changes took effect on 3 January 1977.

Context and criticism: Enacted during the Emergency; critics questioned the political conditions and whether ideological labels should constrain future choices. The constitutional defence is that the additions express existing commitments without destroying basic structure and do not mandate a single economic policy.

How Can It Be Amended?

  • Article 368 requires, in each House, a majority of total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting; presidential assent is required.
  • No joint sitting resolves disagreement on a Constitution Amendment Bill.
  • State ratification is necessary only when a change falls within Article 368's specified federal categories, not simply because it mentions the Preamble.
  • Compliance with voting procedure cannot save an amendment that damages or destroys the basic structure.
  • Basic structure protects constitutional principles; it is not a rule mechanically freezing each word of the Preamble.

Detailed Significance of the Three Added Terms

Socialist: made the commitment to welfare and reducing inequality explicit in the introductory statement. It strengthens the interpretive connection between the Preamble and Directive Principles without converting every welfare aspiration into an immediately enforceable right.

Secular: expressly named the order already supported by equality, conscience and minority protections. The correct before-and-after comparison is therefore implicit and substantive recognition versus explicit Preamble wording, not a religious Constitution suddenly becoming secular in 1976.

Integrity: emphasised national wholeness alongside unity. It must be read with dignity, freedom and the federal scheme rather than as an isolated authorisation for coercive action.

Procedure and Substance Are Separate Tests

An amendment must meet the applicable voting, ratification and assent requirements. Even after doing so, it remains reviewable for damage to basic structure. Procedural validity answers how the amendment was made; the substantive limit asks what the amendment does to constitutional identity.

Illustration, not a decided hypothetical: a proposal to abolish competitive elections would raise a basic-structure problem even if Parliament achieved the required voting majority. By contrast, a lawful institutional adjustment is not invalid merely because it changes constitutional text.

Assessing criticism: the Emergency context is important historical evidence for political evaluation. Legal invalidity requires a constitutional ground; disapproval of the circumstances alone does not identify which provision or structural limit was breached. The 2024 decision rejected the challenges to these additions.

Do not equate word removal with automatic destruction, or word retention with automatic compliance. The constitutional effect matters. A nominal commitment to democracy would not save an amendment that substantively destroyed it.

16. Judicial Review: Preamble, Parliament and Constitution

Judicial review allows courts to test legislation, executive action and constitutional amendments against constitutional limits. The Preamble supplies interpretive context; substantive provisions supply rights and powers, and the basic structure doctrine limits amendments.

QuestionPosition
Is Parliament sovereign in the British sense?No. It legislates within constitutional competence and rights limits and amends subject to basic structure.
Does judicial review make judges unlimited rulers?No. Courts are bound by the Constitution and do not ordinarily replace lawful policy with their preferred policy.
Can the Preamble independently create a right or power?No. It guides interpretation but does not replace the operative constitutional text.
Can a constitutional amendment abolish judicial review or democracy?Not if it damages or destroys the basic structure.
Can an ordinary law be struck down solely for violating an abstract basic feature?Generally ordinary laws are tested against constitutional provisions, including rights and legislative competence; basic structure is principally an amendment limitation.
Is basic structure an exhaustive list written in the Preamble?No. It is a judicial doctrine, with features identified through constitutional interpretation.

Arguments in tension: Democratic mandates require space for change, but cannot extinguish constitutional democracy; social reform needs effective power, but must respect rights; national unity matters, but does not eliminate federalism. The resulting principle is constitutional supremacy, not unlimited parliamentary or judicial authority.

How to Analyse a Judicial-Review Problem

  1. Identify the action: is it an ordinary law, executive decision or constitutional amendment? The relevant tests are not interchangeable.
  2. Identify constitutional authority: legislative competence, rights, procedural requirements and other operative provisions supply legal standards. Articles 32 and 226 provide important routes for review, with different scopes.
  3. Use the Preamble appropriately: connect its values to the provision being interpreted; do not present "justice" or "unity" as a freestanding governmental power.
  4. For an amendment, examine basic structure: ask whether the change damages fundamental constitutional identity, not merely whether it changes a provision that was previously important.
  5. Distinguish legality from preference: a court's role is to enforce constitutional limits, not invalidate every policy with which judges disagree.

Importance and Criticism of Review

Importance: an institution must be able to enforce rights against a popular government and protect democratic procedures from an incumbent seeking to entrench itself. Review prevents a temporary majority from eliminating the conditions under which future majorities can emerge.

Criticism: broad principles may leave judges substantial discretion, raising concerns about unelected decision-makers displacing political choices. The constitutional response is reasoned adjudication grounded in text, structure and precedent, with respect for lawful legislative and executive functions.

Balanced conclusion: a democratic mandate justifies governing within the Constitution; judicial independence enables enforcement of that Constitution. Neither establishes unlimited supremacy for its institution.

17. Judgments: Issues, Arguments, Holdings and Importance

The arguments below summarise the central constitutional positions, not verbatim pleadings. Distinguish a judgment's operative holding from its wider interpretive significance.

In re Berubari Union (1960)

Issue: Could Indian territory be ceded to Pakistan through Article 3 legislation, or was an Article 368 amendment required? Competing positions: territorial-adjustment powers versus a distinction between internal reorganisation and cession to a foreign State.

Holding: Cession required a constitutional amendment. The Court called the Preamble a key to the framers' intentions but treated it as not part of the Constitution and not an independent source of power or prohibition. Significance: Its view on Preamble status was displaced in Kesavananda; that does not mean every territorial-law conclusion was automatically reversed.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

Issue: Scope of Parliament's power to amend, including Fundamental Rights. Broad-power argument: constituent power extends to every provision and permits major reform. Limited-power argument: amendment means change within constitutional identity, not destruction of that identity.

Holding: A 13-judge bench, by 7-6 on the controlling limitation, held that Parliament may amend but cannot damage or destroy the basic structure. The Preamble is part of the Constitution and may be amended within that limit. Importance: Reconciled adaptability with continuity; its principles help identify basic features without exhausting them.

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

Issue: The 39th Amendment's special constitutional treatment of the Prime Minister's election. Defence: reliance on constituent power to determine the dispute's treatment. Challenge: removal of ordinary adjudication and unequal validation undermined constitutional democracy and rule of law.

Holding: Article 329A(4) was struck down. The opinions relied on basic features including democracy, equality, rule of law and adjudicatory safeguards. Importance: An electoral mandate cannot justify amendment-based destruction of democratic safeguards.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

Issue: The 42nd Amendment expanded Article 31C to all Directive Principles and inserted Article 368(4)-(5) to exclude amendment review and declare unlimited amending power. Defence: social transformation required legislative freedom. Challenge: limited constitutional power cannot convert itself into unlimited power, and welfare cannot erase rights.

Holding: Invalidated those changes. Limited amending power, judicial review, and harmony between Parts III and IV are basic features. Importance: Justice and liberty must be balanced. The Court did not strike down the Preamble additions.

S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

Issue: Review of President's Rule and the relevance of anti-secular state-government conduct. Competing positions: executive discretion to respond to constitutional breakdown versus federal autonomy and safeguards against arbitrary dismissal.

Holding: Secularism and federalism are basic features; Article 356 action is subject to meaningful judicial review. Anti-secular governmental conduct may be relevant, but proclamations need constitutionally relevant material. Importance: The word "secular" reflects an enforceable constitutional order when read with operative provisions, not a licence for arbitrary central intervention.

LIC of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre (1995)

Issue: Restrictive eligibility conditions imposed by a public insurer. Competing positions: commercial discretion versus public-institution obligations of fairness and equality.

Holding / significance: The Court reiterated the Preamble's integral constitutional position and used social and economic justice alongside substantive constitutional provisions to assess fairness. It did not make every aspiration independently enforceable.

Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India (25 November 2024)

Issue: Challenges to the insertion of "socialist" and "secular", including the Emergency context, ideological objections and the argument that a Preamble dated 1949 could not bear later additions.

Holding: Challenges rejected. Article 368 extends to the Preamble; its date does not prohibit later amendments. Secularism is a constitutional commitment, and socialism does not compel one economic model or prohibit private enterprise. Importance: Confirms amendability and contextual interpretation of the additions. The case is Balram Singh, not "Ransingh".

Detailed Case Lessons and Argument Notes

  • Berubari: power must come from an operative provision. The useful lesson is the distinction between a constitutional objective and legal authority to act. Even under its earlier view of status, the Court recognised interpretive relevance. Kesavananda changed the status conclusion, not the principle that the Preamble alone cannot confer a power.
  • Kesavananda: amendment is not constitutional destruction. The broad-power argument correctly stresses the need for reform; the limiting argument protects the continued existence of constitutional government. The decision accommodates both by permitting amendments to all parts, including rights, while protecting basic structure. It does not make Fundamental Rights wholly unamendable.
  • Indira Gandhi: rules must also bind the powerful. Democracy depends on fair legal conditions for electoral competition and adjudication, not only on declaring a winner popular. The case illustrates why special amendment-based insulation of an incumbent can undermine the democratic order itself.
  • Minerva Mills: means and ends must coexist. Directive Principles express social goals and Fundamental Rights protect important means of individual freedom and accountability. The constitutional balance is not served by giving every welfare claim unconditional priority over rights. Parliament also cannot remove the limits from the very power the Constitution grants it.
  • Bommai: secularism and federalism operate together. State governments must respect secular constitutional obligations, while the Union must justify Article 356 action with relevant material. The decision cannot be reduced either to unrestricted central dismissal power or to complete immunity for state governments.
  • LIC: interpretive values have practical relevance. A public institution's contractual or commercial activity does not automatically remove constitutional obligations of fairness. The Preamble informs those obligations, but an actual case still requires a proper legal basis and analysis of the challenged conditions.
  • Balram Singh: historical adoption does not freeze the text. A dated Constitution can contain valid later amendments. The 1949 date records the founding act, while Article 368 governs change. The decision also rejects treating Indian socialism as compulsory state ownership or secularism as a wholly new 1976 invention.

How the Background Cases Explain the Change

Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh supported amendment of rights by distinguishing constituent from ordinary legislative power. Golak Nath took the opposite position on Article 13 and protected rights from prospective abridgment. The 24th Amendment responded by expressly affirming amending power. Kesavananda moved beyond this all-or-nothing contest: rights may be amended, but the framework cannot be destroyed.

Coelho's importance: placing a law in the Ninth Schedule through a later constitutional amendment cannot automatically evade basic-structure scrutiny. This is a specific amendment-immunity context, not permission to challenge any ordinary policy by invoking the Preamble alone.

Judgment answer format: Background → constitutional issue → competing arguments → holding → significance → limitation. Cite the proposition the case supports rather than listing names without explanation.

19. Mains Points

  1. Legitimacy has several dimensions: acknowledge restricted selection, then assess deliberation, diversity, public feedback and the inclusive constitutional settlement.
  2. Committee government enabled collective authorship: distinguish policy recommendations, legal drafting and the full Assembly's authority.
  3. Independence and the republic are distinct: the 1947 Act enabled sovereign constitution-making; the Constitution established the republic.
  4. The Preamble links government to social purpose: rights and institutions serve justice, liberty, equality, fraternity and dignity.
  5. Indian secularism requires balance: protect conscience and equal citizenship while permitting lawful reform, without unrestricted intervention.
  6. Amendment and judicial review balance change with continuity: Parliament may adapt the constitutional text, but cannot eliminate its identity.
  7. Avoid false choices: unity need not erase diversity; socialism need not abolish private enterprise; majority rule cannot override constitutional rights.
Model conclusion: The Assembly converted political independence into a constitutional project of democratic and social transformation. The Preamble expresses its enduring commitments, while operative provisions and judicial review translate those commitments into constitutional limits and responsibilities.

22. Introductory Foundations: Law, State and Constitution

22.1 Law: rules, authority and remedies

Law comprises rules and principles recognised and applied through a legal system. It regulates conduct, authorises institutions, protects rights, resolves disputes and provides remedies. A rule need not prescribe punishment to be law: rules about valid contracts, inheritance, elections or institutional powers can be enabling or protective.

Law, morality and custom: They may overlap but are not identical. A moral criticism does not by itself establish a legal offence; a custom may have legal force where the legal system recognises it. Article 13(3)(a) expressly includes customs and usages having the force of law within its definition for that Article.

22.2 State, government, country and nation

In the introductory political-science sense, a State is a politically organised community with population, territory, government and sovereignty. Garner's account emphasises territorial political organisation and independence from external control. Treat this as a conceptual framework, not a mechanical answer to every disputed international-status question.

  • Population: A community of people; no universally prescribed minimum population or requirement of one language, religion or ethnicity. Population is not identical to citizenship.
  • Territory: An identifiable territorial base; a boundary dispute does not automatically negate statehood.
  • Government: Institutions through which public authority is organised and exercised; a democratic form is not a prerequisite for describing an entity as a State.
  • Sovereignty: Internal authority and external independence, exercised in a world of constitutional and international obligations. It does not make each public official legally unlimited.

22.3 What a Constitution does

A Constitution is the foundational body of rules organising public authority. Bryce's broad account includes laws and customs; K.C. Wheare emphasises the rules establishing and regulating government. India has a codified supreme Constitution, but not every country's constitutional order takes that form.

  1. Minimum coordination: Supplies common procedures through which people with conflicting interests can cooperate and resolve disagreements.
  2. Political identity: Gives a shared civic framework without erasing cultural identities; the Preamble states the Republic's foundational commitments.
  3. Institutional organisation: Establishes principal institutions and their relationships. Under Article 79, Parliament consists of the President and both Houses, not just Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha.
  4. Limited power: Constrains public authority through rights, procedures, review and accountability.
  5. Allocation of powers: Horizontally among institutions; vertically between levels of government. Union-State distribution and local-government devolution have different legal mechanisms.
  6. Rights and social goals: Part III supplies enforceable Fundamental Rights, some for citizens and some for all persons. Part IV supplies non-justiciable Directive Principles fundamental in governance under Article 37; they are not all individually enforceable rights.
  7. Peaceful change: Elections, ministerial accountability, amendment, lawful removal and judicial remedies channel political change without assuming revolution is the only alternative.

Social-contract analogy: It explains the legitimacy of common rules; it does not mean every Indian signed a literal contract or that each constitutional rule is optional for a dissenter. Nor is a Constitution merely descriptive: it can impose binding duties and transformative goals.

22.5 Constitution and constitutionalism

Constitution concerns the foundational rules; constitutionalism insists that public power is effectively limited, accountable and exercised consistently with rights and lawful procedures. An elaborate text does not guarantee effective implementation. Conversely, an uncodified constitutional order may contain strong limits on power.

Assessment: Examine institutional independence, access to remedies, electoral accountability, rights in practice and compliance with judgments. Avoid grading whole countries by an unqualified "has/has no constitutionalism" slogan. Constitutionalism is a continuing practice, not a certificate automatically conferred by possessing a document.

22.6 Rule of law and other safeguards

Rule of law opposes arbitrary power: officials require lawful authority, legal rules should be reasonably knowable and applied fairly, and independent remedies must be available. Rule by law describes using legal forms as instruments of control without meaningful restraints on rulers. An enactment's existence alone does not establish constitutional legitimacy.

Dicey's classical account: Supremacy of regular law, equality before the ordinary law, and the importance of judicially protected individual rights in the British constitutional order. The third proposition is historically contextual; India's written Constitution itself guarantees rights. Constitutional immunities, specialised courts and justified classifications also mean "every person is always dealt with by exactly the same procedure" is inaccurate.

Lex rex: "Law is king" is a memory aid for subjection of rulers to law, not a substitute for identifying the applicable constitutional rule. Article 14 combines equality before law and equal protection of laws; equality does not forbid every reasonable classification.

  • Independent judiciary: Impartial adjudication, effective remedies and review constrain unlawful public action. Judicial independence does not remove accountability or appellate correction.
  • Separation and checks: Divide functions while preserving constitutional cooperation; no branch acquires unlimited authority.
  • Free media and civil society: Facilitate information, scrutiny and participation. Press freedom falls within Article 19(1)(a), subject to valid Article 19(2) restrictions; "Fourth Estate" is not a formal fourth constitutional branch.
  • Civilian control of defence: Armed forces remain subject to constitutional civilian authority. Article 53(2) vests supreme command in the President, regulated by law; it is not personal military rule by the President.

Preamble connection: Democracy without accountability may become mere periodic voting; liberty without remedies may remain aspirational; equality without fair institutions may remain formal. Constitutionalism connects those goals to actual limits and practices.

23. Separation of Powers and Forms of Government

23.1 Functions, safeguards and overlap

Separation of powers distributes primary governmental functions among institutions to prevent concentration and arbitrary rule. Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) is the standard modern reference, although earlier thinkers also considered divided government. His argument connects institutional limits with liberty; it does not require every constitutional system to reproduce an idealised eighteenth-century English model.

23.3 US comparison: separate institutions, mutual checks

The US Constitution's Articles I, II and III allocate legislative, executive and judicial powers. The President and cabinet are not members of Congress; executive survival does not depend on an ordinary no-confidence vote. This stronger institutional separation is still not absolute.

  • Presidential veto: Checks congressional legislation, subject to the constitutional override mechanism.
  • Congressional control: Budget, legislation, investigations and impeachment constrain the executive. The House impeaches and the Senate tries; impeachment is not automatically removal.
  • Appointments: Presidential nomination and Senate advice and consent interact for specified offices, including federal judges.
  • Treaties: The Senate gives advice and consent by two-thirds of senators present; the President concludes/ratifies through the constitutional process. Not every international agreement is an Article II treaty, and saying simply "the Senate ratifies" obscures the distinction.
  • Judicial review: Marbury v. Madison (1803) is the classic review case; it did not originally create the separate branches.

24. Federalism, Centralising Features and Decentralisation

24.1 Constitutional division, not mere delegation

Federalism distributes authority between levels of government with constitutionally protected spheres. Separation of powers distributes functions horizontally among organs. A country can be federal and presidential, or federal and parliamentary; these describe different dimensions.

Rationale: Accommodate diversity, protect regional autonomy, bring decisions closer to affected people, prevent concentration and allow policy experimentation. The "laboratories" idea associated with Justice Brandeis captures experimentation, not freedom to violate common constitutional rights.

Typical features: Dual polity, constitutional supremacy, defined powers, protection for federal amendments, an independent constitutional arbiter and territorial representation in a second chamber. This is a comparative framework, not a claim that every federation has identical institutions or identical powers for every constituent unit.

24.2 India as a Union of States

Article 1 describes India, that is Bharat, as a Union of States. Ambedkar explained that the Union was not the result of a compact among sovereign States and that no State could secede. Absence of the word "federal" from that description does not negate federalism. S.R. Bommai recognises it as a basic feature.

Historical models: "Coming together" describes separate political units forming a federation; "holding together" describes constitutional distribution within a larger polity. These are analytical models. Indian constitution-making also involved Partition, accession and integration, so history should not be reduced to a single tidy label. Hyderabad's September 1948 integration is part of that wider history.

Descriptions: K.C. Wheare's "quasi-federal" is an academic assessment, not constitutional text. "Federal with a strong centralising tendency" captures the structure more usefully than saying States are simply Union departments. "Sui generis" means distinctive; do not attribute a particular quotation to Ambedkar without a verified source.

26. Quorum, Majorities, Bills and Amendment Procedure

26.1 Quorum is not a voting threshold

Quorum is the minimum attendance required to transact business. Under Article 100(3), until Parliament provides otherwise by law, it is one-tenth of the total number of members of the House. If absent, the presiding officer must adjourn or suspend until quorum is present under Article 100(4). Article 189 supplies the corresponding State-legislature framework, with its own formula.

Voting: Ordinary questions under Article 100 are decided by a majority of votes of members present and voting, except where the Constitution provides otherwise. Abstention is not an affirmative or negative vote. The presiding officer ordinarily does not vote in the first instance but has a casting vote on equality under Article 100; particular constitutional removal procedures must be checked separately.

Worked example: Total membership 250; vacancies 10; present 200; abstaining 20; votes cast 180. Quorum is 25. A simple majority needs 91 affirmative votes; absolute majority 126; effective majority 121; two-thirds present and voting 120; the composite Article 368 majority needs 126; two-thirds total needs 167. A full attendance figure is not itself evidence of sufficient affirmative votes.

Linked removal safeguards: Articles 148(1), 324(5) and 243K(2) refer to judicial-removal protection for the CAG, CEC and State Election Commissioner respectively. That does not give every election commissioner or every statutory officer an identical protection.

26.2 Bills: two different classifications

Introducer: A Government Bill is introduced by a minister; a Private Member's Bill by a member who is not a minister. An MP from the governing party can therefore introduce a Private Member's Bill. This is separate from the Bill's subject category.

26.3 Ordinary legislative sequence

Introduction / first reading → general discussion and detailed consideration, including committee scrutiny if referred → third reading / passage → other House → presidential assent → commencement under the applicable rule.

First reading concerns introduction rather than the main clause-by-clause debate. Second reading includes general and detailed consideration, with committee scrutiny where referred. Third reading concerns final passage. Committee referral is not automatic in every case.

Joint sitting: Article 108 has specified disagreement, rejection or delay conditions. It is not an automatic next step whenever one House dislikes a Bill and is unavailable for Money Bills and Article 368 Bills.

Article 111: The President may assent or withhold assent, and may return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration. If the Houses pass it again under that process, the President shall not withhold assent. Article 368's mandatory assent requirement is a distinct rule for constitutional amendments.

Assent, publication and commencement: A Bill becomes an Act on assent, but the date on which provisions operate can differ. A statute may specify a date, provide for notification, or fall under the applicable default commencement rule. Gazette publication does not prove every provision is operational. The BNS's general 1 July 2024 commencement and the initial exclusion of Section 106(2) illustrate this distinction; subsequent notification status needs a fresh check.

Citizenship example: Article 11 allows parliamentary citizenship legislation. Amending the Citizenship Act is not, just because its subject is constitutional, the same as amending the text of Articles 5-11. Nor is the whole Second Schedule generally amendable through one unrestricted ordinary-law power; follow the particular enabling provision.

26.5 Article 368, Part XX: complete procedure

  1. Introduce a Constitution Amendment Bill in either House of Parliament. A State legislature cannot initiate an Article 368 Bill; no national referendum is prescribed.
  2. Secure passage separately in each House by a majority of total membership and at least two-thirds present and voting. Parliamentary procedural stages need to be distinguished; not every preliminary motion requires that special majority.
  3. Where the proviso to Article 368(2) applies, obtain State ratifications before presenting the Bill to the President.
  4. The President shall give assent, as expressly provided following the 24th Amendment, 1971. There is no Article 368 joint sitting to cure a failed House vote.
  5. Read the amending Act's commencement provisions. Assent does not imply that every amendment has no separately specified commencement arrangement.
  6. Procedural compliance does not foreclose constitutional review: the amendment cannot damage or destroy basic structure.

Ratification details: State resolutions ordinarily use a simple majority under the applicable procedure; bicameral State legislatures require both Houses' participation. The President is presented with the Bill after the necessary ratifications; State assent as if to an ordinary State Bill is not an additional step. Article 368 specifies no general time limit for ratification. A State cannot rewrite the parliamentary Bill through its ratification resolution.

Federal effect matters: Examine what a change does to the protected provisions, not just the Article number that the amending clause expressly mentions. The Rajendra N. Shah decision (2021), concerning the 97th Amendment's cooperative-society framework, illustrates the importance of State ratification when federal legislative powers are affected.

Preamble link: The 42nd Amendment and basic-structure discussion already explains amendment of the Preamble. Merely mentioning the Preamble does not automatically trigger ratification, and retaining its words does not save a change that destroys the underlying constitutional commitments.

26.6 Application questions and mains frameworks

  1. Does a codified Constitution guarantee constitutionalism? No; actual limits, remedies and institutional practices matter.
  2. Can a democracy have a hereditary head of State? Yes; parliamentary constitutional monarchies show why democracy and republic differ.
  3. Does Article 122 exclude review of every legislative action? No; its procedural-irregularity protection is not a blanket shield against constitutional violations.
  4. Can State opposition alone veto an Article 3 reorganisation? No; required referral and consideration of views differ from a consent requirement.
  5. Can a Money Bill be introduced in Rajya Sabha? No; a Category II Financial Bill can originate there under its own conditions.
  6. Can a joint sitting rescue an Article 368 amendment rejected by one House? No.
  7. Does the Eleventh Schedule itself transfer all 29 subjects to every Panchayat? No; devolution depends on the constitutional/State-law framework.
  8. Must every Preamble amendment be ratified by States? No automatic rule; examine the affected Article 368 categories and the amendment's substance.

Mains: constitutionalism: Define limited government → distinguish text from practice → illustrate rights, independent courts, legislative checks and media → assess implementation gaps → connect to Preamble goals.

Mains: Indian federalism: Constitutional division → federal safeguards → centralising powers with limits → cooperation and fiscal/local-government issues → conclude with constitutional supremacy, not an absolute federal/unitary slogan.

Mains: borrowed Constitution: Identify influences → explain Indian adaptations and domestic deliberation → acknowledge colonial continuity and representational criticisms → assess the transformed framework of popular sovereignty and rights.
