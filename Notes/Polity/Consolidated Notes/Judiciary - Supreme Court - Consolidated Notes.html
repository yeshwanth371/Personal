1. Indian judiciary: constitutional role and organisation

Indian Judiciary and the Constitution

The judiciary is the branch of government that interprets laws, resolves disputes, and administers justice. In India, it also protects Fundamental Rights and ensures that the legislature and executive act within constitutional limits.

India has an independent and integrated judicial system, with the Supreme Court at the top.

1. Constitutional Role of the Judiciary

Role Explanation Relevant Provisions
Guardian of the Constitution Ensures that laws and government actions conform to the Constitution. Articles 13, 32 and 226
Protection of Fundamental Rights Issues writs to enforce Fundamental Rights. Articles 32 and 226
Judicial review Examines the constitutional validity of laws, executive actions and constitutional amendments. Amendments cannot damage the Constitution’s basic structure. Articles 13, 32, 226 and 368
Dispute resolution Decides civil, criminal and constitutional disputes. Various constitutional and statutory provisions
Federal umpire The Supreme Court decides specified disputes between the Union and States, or between States. Article 131
Authoritative interpretation The law declared by the Supreme Court binds all courts in India. Article 141

Important distinction: Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights. Article 226 empowers High Courts to enforce Fundamental Rights and other legal rights, giving them wider writ jurisdiction.

2. Organization of the Judiciary in India

This represents the general hierarchy. The exact court structure and route of appeal depend on the applicable law.

A. Supreme Court of India
  • Constitutional provisions: Articles 124–147.
  • Composition: Chief Justice of India and other Supreme Court judges.
  • Position: Highest court and final court of appeal in India.
  • Original jurisdiction: Hears certain cases directly, including specified Union–State and inter-State disputes.
  • Appellate jurisdiction: Hears appeals in constitutional, civil and criminal matters.
  • Writ jurisdiction: Protects Fundamental Rights under Article 32.
  • Advisory jurisdiction: Gives opinions on questions referred by the President under Article 143.
  • Special powers: May grant special leave to appeal under Article 136 and pass orders necessary for complete justice under Article 142.

It is a court of record under Article 129 and can punish for contempt of itself.

B. High Courts
  • Constitutional provisions: Articles 214–231.
  • Position: Highest courts within their respective territorial jurisdictions.
  • A High Court may serve one State or a group of States and Union Territories; every State does not necessarily have a separate High Court.
  • Hear appeals from subordinate courts and exercise original jurisdiction in specified matters.
  • Issue writs under Article 226.
  • Exercise superintendence over courts and tribunals within their territory under Article 227, subject to the constitutional exception for those constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces.
  • Exercise control over district and subordinate courts under Article 235.

High Courts are courts of record under Article 215.

C. District and Sessions Courts
  • Relevant constitutional provisions: Articles 233–237 deal with subordinate courts.
  • These are the principal courts at the district level.
  • The District Judge heads the district’s civil judiciary.
  • The Sessions Judge tries serious criminal cases.
  • Commonly, the same judicial officer performs both roles and is called the District and Sessions Judge.

Remember: “District” generally refers to the civil role; “Sessions” refers to the criminal role.

D. Other Subordinate Courts
Civil Courts Criminal Courts
Decide disputes involving property, contracts, compensation and other civil rights. Try offences and impose punishments according to law.
Include Civil Judges, with designations varying by State. Include Chief Judicial Magistrates and Judicial Magistrates.

Family Courts and other specialised courts handle particular categories of disputes.

3. Key Features

Integrated system: India does not have separate parallel Union and State court systems like the United States. Courts within the integrated hierarchy administer both Union and State laws.

Judicial independence: Protected through security of tenure, a difficult removal process for Supreme Court and High Court judges, constitutional safeguards for service conditions, and restrictions on legislative discussion of judges’ conduct.

Separation from the executive: Article 50, a Directive Principle, directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

Judicial review: Judicial review and judicial independence form part of the Constitution’s basic structure.

4. Tribunals and Lok Adalats

Tribunals are specialised adjudicatory bodies dealing with subjects such as administrative service disputes. Articles 323A and 323B provide for specified tribunals. They are not simply another tier in the ordinary court hierarchy, and their decisions remain subject to constitutional judicial review.

Lok Adalats provide an alternative dispute-resolution mechanism, primarily through settlement. They are not a regular appellate tier.

Exam-ready summary: India has a single, integrated and independent judiciary consisting of the Supreme Court, High Courts and subordinate courts. It administers justice, protects rights, resolves federal disputes and upholds the supremacy of the Constitution.

2. Supreme Court: formation and historical significance

Formation of the Supreme Court of India

The Supreme Court of India came into existence on 26 January 1950, when the Constitution came into force. Its inaugural sitting took place on 28 January 1950.

It was established under Article 124, in Part V, Chapter IV of the Constitution. Articles 124–147 deal with its organisation, jurisdiction and powers.

To understand its historical significance, distinguish the colonial Supreme Court at Calcutta from the present Supreme Court of India.

1. Historical Evolution

Year Development Significance
1773 Parliament of Great Britain passed the Regulating Act, 1773. Provided for establishing a Supreme Court at Fort William, Calcutta, through a royal charter.
1774 The Supreme Court of Judicature at Fort William was established. A Crown court with civil and criminal jurisdiction within defined limits. Sir Elijah Impey was its first Chief Justice.
1801 and 1823 Supreme Courts were established at Madras and Bombay, respectively. Extended the system of Crown courts in the Presidency towns.
1861–1862 The Indian High Courts Act, 1861 enabled the establishment of High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in 1862. These replaced the Presidency Supreme Courts and the corresponding Sadar courts, bringing the two judicial systems together at that level.
1935–1937 The Government of India Act, 1935 provided for a Federal Court, which began functioning on 1 October 1937. Created a central court to interpret the federal constitutional framework and decide specified federal disputes.
1949 The Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949 ended the Privy Council’s appellate jurisdiction over India. Transferred that jurisdiction to the Federal Court, ending appeals to an authority in Britain.
1950 The Constitution established the Supreme Court of India. Replaced the Federal Court and became the apex court of the sovereign republic.

Key distinction: The court established at Calcutta in 1774 was not today’s Supreme Court of India. The present Court’s immediate institutional predecessor was the Federal Court of India.

2. The Federal Court: Immediate Predecessor

The Federal Court was an important step towards establishing a national constitutional court.

  • Its first Chief Justice was Sir Maurice Gwyer.
  • It exercised original, appellate and advisory jurisdiction, within the limits of the governing law.
  • It interpreted the Government of India Act, 1935, and decided specified disputes involving the federation and its constituent units.
  • Initially, it was not the final judicial authority, because appeals to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London remained available in certain cases.

The Supreme Court inherited this institutional foundation but received broader constitutional powers, particularly for protecting Fundamental Rights.

3. Formation in 1950

Aspect Detail
Constitutional basis Article 124
Came into existence 26 January 1950
Inaugural sitting 28 January 1950
First Chief Justice of India Justice H. J. Kania, the last Chief Justice of the Federal Court
Original constitutional composition One Chief Justice and not more than seven other judges, unless Parliament prescribed a larger number
Initial location Chamber of Princes in the Parliament House building, New Delhi
Present building The Court moved into its present building in 1958

Article 374 provided transitional continuity: Federal Court judges became Supreme Court judges unless they opted otherwise, and pending proceedings transferred to the new Court.

4. Historical Significance of Its Formation

A. Judicial sovereignty
Ending Privy Council appeals in 1949 and establishing the Supreme Court in 1950 completed the transition to a final judicial authority located within independent India.

B. From colonial governance to constitutional supremacy
The new Court derived its authority from the Constitution of India, rather than a British statute. It became responsible for ensuring that public authorities acted within constitutional limits.

C. Enforceable Fundamental Rights
Under Article 32, individuals could directly approach the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights. This made the Court a constitutional protector of individuals against unlawful State action.

D. An umpire in a federal system
Under Article 131, it could decide specified legal disputes between the Union and States, or between States, helping maintain the constitutional division of powers.

E. Unity in the legal system
As the apex of an integrated judiciary, it promoted consistent interpretation of law. Under Article 141, the law it declares binds all courts within India.

F. Foundation for later constitutional developments
The Court subsequently developed major constitutional doctrines, including the basic structure doctrine in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). This was a later development, not a doctrine announced at its formation.

Exam takeaway: The Supreme Court’s formation marked the transition from a colonial judicial system to a sovereign constitutional judiciary. It combined the Federal Court’s constitutional role with final appellate authority and strong powers to protect Fundamental Rights.

3. Organisation, composition and benches

Organization of the Supreme Court of India

The Supreme Court is the highest court in India. Its organisation is primarily governed by Article 124, while Articles 124–147, in Part V, Chapter IV of the Constitution, deal with its constitution, powers and functioning.

1. Composition and Strength

Aspect Provision
Head Chief Justice of India (CJI)
Original constitutional strength One CJI and not more than seven other judges, unless Parliament prescribed a larger number
Sanctioned strength under the 2019 amendment 34 judges: one CJI + 33 other judges
Who can increase the strength? Parliament, by law
Is a constitutional amendment necessary? No. Article 124 already authorises Parliament to prescribe a larger number.

Sanctioned strength means the number of authorised posts. The actual number of serving judges may be lower because of vacancies.

2. Appointment of Judges

The President of India formally appoints the Chief Justice and other Supreme Court judges under Article 124(2).

  • Chief Justice of India: By convention, the senior-most Supreme Court judge considered fit for the office is appointed.
  • Other judges: Under the judicially established collegium system, recommendations are made by the CJI and the four senior-most Supreme Court judges.
  • The word “collegium” is not in the Constitution; the system developed through Supreme Court judgments.

3. Qualifications

Under Article 124(3), a person must be a citizen of India and satisfy any one of these conditions:

Route Required Qualification
Judicial experience At least five years as a judge of a High Court, or of two or more High Courts in succession
Advocacy experience At least ten years as an advocate of a High Court, or of two or more High Courts in succession
Distinguished jurist Be, in the President’s opinion, a distinguished jurist

Exam point: The Constitution prescribes no minimum age for appointment as a Supreme Court judge.

4. Tenure, Resignation and Removal

  • Retirement: At 65 years. There is no fixed term of a specified number of years.
  • Resignation: By writing addressed to the President.
  • Removal: By an order of the President following an address passed by each House of Parliament in the same session, on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

For removal, each House must support the address by both:

  1. A majority of its total membership.
  2. At least two-thirds of the members present and voting.

A retired Supreme Court judge cannot plead or act before any court or authority in India, under Article 124(7).

5. Special Arrangements for Judges

Arrangement Article Explanation
Acting Chief Justice 126 The President appoints a Supreme Court judge to perform the CJI’s duties when the office is vacant or the CJI is unable to perform them.
Ad hoc judges 127 When a quorum is unavailable, the CJI may request a qualified High Court judge to attend, with the President’s previous consent and after consulting the relevant High Court Chief Justice.
Attendance of retired judges 128 The CJI may request an eligible former Supreme Court or High Court judge to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge, with the President’s previous consent and the person’s consent.

6. Seat and Bench Structure

Seat: Article 130
The Supreme Court sits in Delhi. The CJI may, with the President’s approval, appoint another place or places for it to sit.

Benches: Article 145 and the Court’s rules

  • The Court does not ordinarily sit with all judges together. Cases are generally heard by benches of two or three judges.
  • A Constitution Bench of at least five judges hears cases involving a substantial question of law about interpreting the Constitution, and references under Article 143.
  • Larger benches, such as seven- or nine-judge benches, may be constituted when required.
  • The CJI is the “master of the roster”, responsible for constituting benches and allocating cases.

7. Administrative Organisation

Under Article 146, the Court has its own officers and staff, organised through its Registry, which handles filings, records, listings and other administrative work. Appointments are made by the CJI or an authorised judge or officer, subject to constitutional provisions.

4. CJI and judges: appointment, supersessions, qualifications, oath, tenure, removal and remuneration

1. CJI’s Position The Chief Justice of India (CJI) heads the Supreme Court and its administration, constitutes benches and allocates cases as the master of the roster. Judicially, the CJI is first among equals: their vote does not carry greater weight than another judge’s vote on the same bench.

2. CJI Appointment Under Article 124(2), the President appoints Supreme Court judges, including the CJI, by warrant under their hand and seal.

  • Convention: The senior-most Supreme Court judge considered fit is appointed CJI. Seniority primarily concerns service in the Supreme Court, not age.
  • Process: The Union Law Minister seeks the outgoing CJI’s recommendation → the outgoing CJI recommends the successor → the Law Minister forwards it to the Prime Minister → the Prime Minister advises the President → the President appoints.
  • If there is doubt about the senior-most judge’s fitness, consultation with other judges is contemplated.
  • Important: Seniority is a convention, not an express requirement in Article 124. CJI succession is distinct from the five-member collegium process for appointing other judges.

3. Broken Conventions The two major departures from the CJI seniority convention were:

Year Appointment Judges Superseded Historical Significance
1973 Justice A. N. Ray became CJI Justices J. M. Shelat, K. S. Hegde and A. N. Grover Followed Kesavananda Bharati. The three superseded judges had supported the basic structure limitation; Ray had dissented on that issue. All three resigned.
1977 Justice M. H. Beg became CJI Justice H. R. Khanna Followed Khanna’s famous dissent in ADM Jabalpur (1976), defending personal liberty during the Emergency. Khanna resigned.

These were supersessions, widely criticised as executive interference with judicial independence. They departed from the convention; they did not formally abolish it.

4. Other Judges’ Appointment

  1. Recommendation: The CJI initiates proposals in consultation with the four senior-most Supreme Court judges, forming the five-member collegium.
  2. Consultation: Relevant judges familiar with the candidate’s High Court are consulted. Merit, integrity, seniority and representation are considered; seniority alone does not guarantee elevation.
  3. Written assessment: Opinions and the recommendation are recorded. Under the Third Judges Case framework, a proposal should not proceed if two collegium members express adverse opinions.
  4. Executive processing: The recommendation goes to the Union Law Minister, then the Prime Minister, who advises the President.
  5. Reconsideration: The government may return a recommendation with reasons. A recommendation unanimously reiterated through the prescribed collegium process is binding.
  6. Appointment: The President issues the appointment warrant; the appointee takes the oath before entering office. There is no parliamentary confirmation hearing.

The appointment system evolved through these decisions:

Decision Main Effect
First Judges Case, 1981: S. P. Gupta Consultation did not mean concurrence; executive primacy prevailed.
Second Judges Case, 1993 Established judicial primacy and the collegium system; the CJI’s opinion had to be formed collectively.
Third Judges Case, 1998: Presidential Reference Expanded the Supreme Court appointment collegium to CJI + four senior-most judges.
NJAC judgment, 2015, often called the Fourth Judges Case Struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment and NJAC Act, restoring the collegium system.

5. Qualifications Under Article 124(3), the person must be an Indian citizen and meet any one of these conditions:

  • Have served at least five years as a High Court judge, including service in two or more High Courts in succession.
  • Have been a High Court advocate for at least ten years, including practice in two or more High Courts in succession.
  • Be, in the President’s opinion, a distinguished jurist.

There is no prescribed minimum age or separate constitutional qualification for the CJI. Previous service as a High Court judge is not compulsory.

6. Oath or Affirmation Under Article 124(6) and the Third Schedule, every judge, including the CJI, takes an oath or affirmation before the President or a person appointed by the President. The judge undertakes to:

  • Bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and uphold India’s sovereignty and integrity.
  • Perform their duties duly and faithfully, to the best of their ability, knowledge and judgment, without fear or favour, affection or ill-will.
  • Uphold the Constitution and the laws.

The person may swear in the name of God or solemnly affirm; both have equal legal effect.

7. Tenure All Supreme Court judges, including the CJI, retire at 65. There is no fixed five- or six-year term, and promotion to CJI does not restart or extend tenure. A judge may resign by writing to the President and does not hold office at the President’s pleasure. After retirement, Article 124(7) prohibits pleading or acting before any court or authority in India; this is not a blanket prohibition on all subsequent public appointments.

8. Removal Procedure “Impeachment” is common shorthand, but the Constitution calls it removal. Articles 124(4)–(5) and the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 govern the process, including for the CJI. The only grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

  1. Notice: At least 100 Lok Sabha members OR 50 Rajya Sabha members sign a notice of motion, submitted to the Speaker or Chairman respectively.
  2. Admission: The Speaker/Chairman may admit or refuse the motion. Signatures alone do not begin an automatic inquiry.
  3. Inquiry committee: If admitted, a three-member committee is constituted: one chosen from the CJI/other Supreme Court judges, one High Court Chief Justice, and one distinguished jurist.
  4. Investigation: The committee investigates the allegations and gives the judge an opportunity to defend themselves.
  5. Finding: If the committee does not establish either ground, the process stops. An adverse finding allows Parliament to consider the motion; it does not itself remove the judge.
  6. Parliamentary vote: Each House separately must approve by both a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting. There is no joint sitting.
  7. Presidential order: The addresses must be presented to the President in the same session; removal occurs through the President’s order.

Example: The 1993 motion against Justice V. Ramaswami failed in the Lok Sabha despite an adverse inquiry report because it did not secure the required majority of total membership. Abstentions therefore matter.

9. Salaries and Allowances Article 125 and the Supreme Court Judges (Salaries and Conditions of Service) Act, 1958, as amended, govern remuneration. The basic monthly salaries set by the 2018 amendment, effective from 1 January 2016, are Rs. 2,80,000 for the CJI and Rs. 2,50,000 for other Supreme Court judges; these are not take-home figures.

  • Statutory benefits include official accommodation without rent, applicable furnishing and maintenance facilities, conveyance, medical and travel facilities, leave, pension and gratuity, subject to governing rules.
  • Salaries, allowances and pensions are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, so they are not submitted to Parliament’s annual vote.
  • Article 125(2) expressly protects privileges, allowances, and leave and pension rights against disadvantageous variation after appointment. Article 360 permits directions reducing judges’ salaries and allowances during a Financial Emergency.
  • I could not retrieve the government’s detailed allowance schedule, so I have not quoted unverified allowance rates or claimed these figures reflect a fresh 2026 revision.

5. First to Fourth Judges Cases, collegium and NJAC

The easiest way to understand the Judges Cases is to follow one question:

When the government and the judiciary disagree about appointing a judge, whose opinion should prevail?

The President remained the formal appointing authority throughout. What changed was who controlled the recommendation.

1. Constitutional Starting Point Article 124(2) provides for Supreme Court appointments by the President after consultation with specified judges; consultation with the CJI is mandatory when appointing a judge other than the CJI. Article 217 governs High Court appointments.

The Constitution did not expressly create a collegium. The dispute concerned the meaning of “consultation”: does it mean seeking advice, or obtaining agreement?

2. First Judges Case S. P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981 | Seven-judge bench

  • Held that consultation does not mean concurrence.
  • The CJI’s opinion did not have overriding priority over other constitutional consultees.
  • Consequently, the executive had primacy, subject to constitutional requirements and judicial review.

In simple terms: The government had to consult the judiciary but was not bound to accept the CJI’s advice. This judgment did not create the collegium.

3. Second Judges Case Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India, 1993 | Nine-judge bench, 7:2

  • Overruled the First Judges Case on appointment primacy and gave primacy to the judiciary to safeguard judicial independence.
  • Crucially, the CJI’s opinion meant a collective institutional opinion, not the CJI’s personal preference.
  • For Supreme Court appointments, this required consultation with the two senior-most Supreme Court judges: CJI + 2 = three-member collegium.
  • Also affirmed that the senior-most Supreme Court judge considered fit should be appointed CJI.

In simple terms: The decisive opinion shifted from the government to a group of senior judges.

4. Third Judges Case In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 | Nine-judge bench This was a Presidential Reference under Article 143, seeking clarification of the 1993 judgment, rather than an ordinary lawsuit.

  • Expanded the collegium for Supreme Court appointments to CJI + four senior-most Supreme Court judges.
  • Required meaningful collective consultation and written opinions; the CJI could not act alone.
  • If two consulted collegium judges express adverse opinions, the CJI should not send that recommendation.
  • A recommendation formed without the prescribed consultation does not bind the government.

In simple terms: The Third Case expanded and clarified the collegium; it did not first create it.

5. The NJAC Attempt Criticism of the collegium included opaque selection criteria, limited accountability and risks of favouritism. Parliament sought a broader appointments body through the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014. They came into force on 13 April 2015.

The amendment introduced Articles 124A, 124B and 124C. The NJAC had six members:

Members Number
CJI, serving as Chairperson 1
Two senior-most Supreme Court judges after the CJI 2
Union Minister of Law and Justice 1
Eminent persons 2

The eminent persons were to be selected by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, CJI and Lok Sabha Leader of Opposition or, where none existed, the leader of the largest opposition party. One had to belong to the SC, ST, OBC, minority or women categories.

NJAC would recommend higher-judiciary appointments and High Court transfers. Its Act retained the senior-most-judge-considered-fit rule for CJI. An important feature was that any two members could block a judicial appointment recommendation, meaning non-judicial members could block a candidate supported by all three judicial members.

6. Fourth Judges Case Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India, 2015 | Five-judge bench, 4:1 On 16 October 2015, the Supreme Court struck down both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act, restoring the collegium.

  • Majority reasoning: Judicial independence is part of the Constitution’s basic structure; NJAC’s composition and decision-making diluted the judicial primacy considered necessary to protect it.
  • Dissent: Justice J. Chelameswar upheld NJAC and criticised the collegium’s lack of transparency and accountability.
  • Important nuance: The majority invalidated this particular NJAC design. It did not declare that the collegium was flawless or that every conceivable reform was prohibited.

7. CJI Versus Judges Do not confuse these distinct appointment routes:

Appointment Operative Recommendation Arrangement
CJI Outgoing CJI recommends the senior-most Supreme Court judge considered fit; government processes the recommendation and the President appoints. It is not an ordinary five-member collegium selection.
Other Supreme Court judges CJI + four senior-most Supreme Court judges recommend; the President formally appoints through the executive process.
High Court judges Proposals ordinarily originate with the High Court CJ + two senior-most judges; at Supreme Court level, CJI + two senior-most judges consider them, with other prescribed consultations.

The 1973 supersession by A. N. Ray and 1977 supersession of H. R. Khanna by M. H. Beg were departures from the CJI seniority convention. They predated the collegium and illustrate the concerns about executive influence underlying the later debate.

8. Current Status As of 12 September 2026, the collegium remains operative; NJAC is not in force. The Supreme Court’s official website lists collegium appointment recommendations dated 10 September 2026. The government still scrutinises recommendations and can seek reconsideration; it is not absent from the process. A unanimously reiterated Supreme Court appointment recommendation following the prescribed process is binding, although delays remain a practical concern. Proposals or political demands to revive NJAC do not themselves change the law.

Memory aid: 1981: Executive primacy → 1993: Collegium created, CJI + 2 → 1998: SC appointment collegium expanded, CJI + 4 → 2015: NJAC struck down, collegium restored.

6. Acting CJI, ad hoc judges and retired judges

Apart from the regular Chief Justice of India and other Supreme Court judges, the Constitution provides three special arrangements to keep the Court functioning:

Article 126: Acting CJI → Article 127: Ad hoc judges → Article 128: Attendance of retired judges

These are temporary arrangements, but each addresses a different situation.

1. Acting CJI Under Article 126, an Acting Chief Justice performs the duties of the CJI when:

  • The office of the CJI is vacant.
  • The CJI is absent.
  • The CJI is otherwise unable to perform the duties of the office.
Question Answer
Who makes the appointment? President of India
Who is eligible? An existing Supreme Court judge
Is the senior-most judge constitutionally mandatory? No. Article 126 does not expressly require seniority, although the senior-most available judge is ordinarily appointed.
How long does the arrangement last? Until a regular CJI takes charge or the existing CJI resumes duties, as applicable.
Does it permanently promote the judge to CJI? No. It is an arrangement to perform the CJI’s duties.

Example: If the CJI is temporarily unable to work because of illness, the President may appoint another Supreme Court judge to perform those duties. There is no need to appoint a new permanent CJI.

2. Ad Hoc Judges Under Article 127, an ad hoc judge may be brought in when there is no quorum of Supreme Court judges available to hold or continue a session.

Quorum means the minimum number of judges required for the relevant sitting. It does not mean that all sanctioned Supreme Court posts must be filled.

Who can serve? A serving High Court judge who is qualified for appointment to the Supreme Court.

Procedure:

  1. The CJI obtains the previous consent of the President.
  2. The CJI consults the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned.
  3. The CJI requests the selected High Court judge in writing to attend Supreme Court sittings for the necessary period.

Position of the ad hoc judge:

  • Attendance is a constitutional duty, taking priority over their other judicial duties.
  • While attending, the judge has the jurisdiction, powers and privileges, and performs the duties, of a Supreme Court judge.
  • This is not a permanent elevation from the High Court to the Supreme Court.

Exam distinction: Article 127 addresses lack of quorum, not merely vacancies or a large backlog.

3. Retired Judges Under Article 128, the CJI may request an eligible former judge to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge.

Question Answer
Who may be requested? A former Supreme Court or Federal Court judge, or a former High Court judge duly qualified for appointment to the Supreme Court
Who initiates the arrangement? CJI
Is presidential consent required? Yes, previous consent
Is the former judge’s own consent required? Yes. They cannot be compelled to serve.
What powers do they exercise? While sitting, the jurisdiction, powers and privileges of a Supreme Court judge
What do they receive? Allowances determined by the President
Do they become a regular Supreme Court judge again? No. Article 128 expressly says they are not otherwise deemed to be a judge of that Court.

Unlike Article 127, Article 128 does not require a lack of quorum. It allows the Court to draw on the experience of former judges.

This does not contradict Article 124(7): the prohibition on a retired Supreme Court judge pleading or acting as a legal practitioner is different from sitting as a judge under Article 128.

7. Independence of the Supreme Court

Independence of the Supreme Court The independence of the Supreme Court means that its judges must be able to decide cases according to the Constitution and law, without improper influence from the government, Parliament, powerful individuals or other sources.

Independence does not mean freedom from the Constitution or accountability. It means freedom from pressure while administering justice.

For example, when a citizen challenges a government action, the Court must be able to rule against the government without judges fearing dismissal or financial punishment.

1. Why Independence Matters

  • Protecting Fundamental Rights: The Court can provide remedies against unconstitutional State action under Article 32.
  • Upholding constitutional supremacy: Through judicial review, it can invalidate unconstitutional laws and executive actions.
  • Maintaining federal balance: It decides specified Union–State and inter-State disputes under Article 131.
  • Ensuring impartial justice: Cases must be decided on legal merits, not political influence.

Judicial independence is part of the Constitution’s basic structure, so Parliament cannot destroy it through a constitutional amendment.

2. Constitutional Safeguards

Safeguard Provision How It Protects Independence
Appointment safeguards Article 124, as judicially interpreted The collegium requires a collective judicial recommendation, limiting unilateral executive control over selection. The President formally appoints judges.
Security of tenure Article 124(2) Judges serve until 65, unless they resign or are removed through the constitutional process. They do not serve at the President’s pleasure.
Difficult removal procedure Article 124(4)–(5) Removal requires proved misbehaviour or incapacity, special majorities in both Houses and a presidential order.
Protected service conditions Article 125(2) Privileges, allowances, and leave and pension rights cannot be varied to a judge’s disadvantage after appointment.
Charged expenditure Articles 112(3)(d) and 146(3) Judges’ remuneration and the Court’s administrative expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, limiting annual financial pressure.
Restriction on parliamentary discussion Article 121 Parliament cannot discuss a judge’s conduct in the discharge of duties except upon a motion for presenting an address seeking removal.
Restriction in State legislatures Article 211 State legislatures cannot discuss the discharge of duties by Supreme Court or High Court judges.
Power to punish contempt Article 129 Helps protect judicial proceedings and the administration of justice from obstruction.
Independent establishment Article 146 The CJI or an authorised judge or officer controls appointments to the Court’s staff, subject to constitutional provisions.
Restriction on practice after retirement Article 124(7) Former Supreme Court judges cannot plead or act before any court or authority in India, reducing incentives to favour potential future clients.

3. Understanding the Safeguards

A. The government cannot dismiss a judge for an unfavourable judgment.
Removal is allowed only for proved misbehaviour or incapacity, not because a decision displeases the government. Each House must approve removal by both a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting.

B. Parliament cannot use the annual vote on expenditure as an ordinary pressure tactic.
“Charged on the Consolidated Fund” means the expenditure is not submitted to Parliament’s vote, although it may be discussed. It does not mean there are no financial rules or scrutiny.

C. Financial protection has an important qualification.
Article 125(2) expressly protects allowances and specified service rights; salary is determined by parliamentary law under Article 125(1). During a Financial Emergency, Article 360 permits directions reducing Supreme Court and High Court judges’ salaries and allowances. Do not write that judicial remuneration can “never” be reduced.

D. Administrative independence is not unlimited.
The Court controls its establishment, but rules concerning staff salaries, allowances, leave and pensions require presidential approval under Article 146(2).

4. Independence and Accountability Judicial independence does not mean that judges or judgments are beyond scrutiny.

Independence Does Not Mean… Correct Position
Judgments cannot be criticised Fair criticism of judgments is permissible; contempt is not a blanket prohibition on criticism.
Judges cannot be removed They can be removed through the constitutionally prescribed process.
Supreme Court decisions can never be reconsidered The Court can review its judgments under Article 137; curative relief is available in exceptional circumstances.
Judges may decide according to personal preference They must follow the Constitution, law, applicable precedent and principles of fair hearing.
Retired judges cannot hold any public office Article 124(7) prohibits legal practice, not every post-retirement appointment. Such appointments can nevertheless raise independence concerns.

5. Link With Separation Article 50, a Directive Principle, directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State. It is particularly relevant to the subordinate judiciary and supports the broader constitutional principle of judicial independence.

Exam-ready conclusion: The Supreme Court’s independence is secured through protected tenure, collective appointment procedures, difficult removal, financial safeguards, administrative autonomy and restrictions on interference. These protections exist for impartial justice and citizens’ rights, not for judges’ personal benefit.

8. Jurisdictions: complete overview

Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court Jurisdiction means the legal authority of a court to hear and decide a matter.

The Supreme Court’s powers can be understood through original, writ, appellate, special leave, advisory and review jurisdiction, together with its powers as a court of record and guardian of the Constitution.

1. Original Jurisdiction Article 131: Certain disputes can begin directly in the Supreme Court, without first going to a lower court.

Its exclusive original jurisdiction covers disputes between:

  • The Government of India and one or more States.
  • The Government of India and one or more States on one side, and one or more other States on the other.
  • Two or more States.

The dispute must involve a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends. A purely political disagreement is insufficient.

Example: A dispute between two States over their legal rights concerning a boundary.

Important: A private individual cannot bring an Article 131 suit. Certain disputes are excluded; for example, Parliament can exclude court jurisdiction over specified inter-State river-water disputes under Article 262.

2. Writ Jurisdiction Article 32: A person may directly approach the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The right to approach it for this purpose is itself a Fundamental Right.

Writ Purpose
Habeas corpus Secures release from unlawful detention.
Mandamus Directs performance of a public or legal duty.
Prohibition Stops a lower court or tribunal from proceeding beyond its jurisdiction.
Certiorari Quashes an order of a lower court, tribunal or authority on recognised legal grounds.
Quo warranto Questions a person’s legal entitlement to hold a public office.

Example: A person unlawfully detained may seek habeas corpus.

Distinction: Article 32 concerns Fundamental Rights; High Courts’ Article 226 writ jurisdiction extends to Fundamental Rights and other legal rights. Writ jurisdiction is also “original” in the broader sense, but it is different from the federal disputes covered by Article 131.

3. Appellate Jurisdiction An appeal asks a higher court to examine a lower court’s decision. The Supreme Court is India’s highest appellate court, but not every case automatically qualifies for an appeal.

Category Provision Main Route
Constitutional matters Article 132 A High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law concerning interpretation of the Constitution.
Civil matters Article 133 A High Court certifies that a substantial question of law of general importance is involved and that the Supreme Court should decide it.
Criminal matters Article 134 Appeals lie in specified cases, including certain death-sentence cases, or where the High Court certifies fitness for appeal.

Article 134A governs consideration of the required High Court certificate. Parliamentary laws also provide additional appellate routes.

Criminal example: A High Court reverses an acquittal on appeal and sentences the accused to death, attracting Article 134(1)(a).

4. Special Leave Article 136: The Supreme Court may, at its discretion, grant special permission to appeal against a judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order of a court or tribunal in India.

  • It is an extraordinary discretionary power, not an automatic right of appeal.
  • It is not confined to High Court decisions or one category of law.
  • Exception: Article 136 does not apply to courts or tribunals constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces.

SLP distinction: A Special Leave Petition asks for permission to appeal. Once leave is granted, the matter becomes an appeal.

5. Advisory Jurisdiction Article 143: The President may refer questions to the Supreme Court for its opinion.

Provision Subject Court’s Position
Article 143(1) A question of law or fact of public importance that has arisen or is likely to arise The Court may give an opinion and may decline to answer.
Article 143(2) Certain disputes involving pre-Constitution treaties or similar instruments excluded by the proviso to Article 131 The Court must report its opinion after a hearing.

The opinion is advisory, not binding on the President. This does not mean that the constitutional law explained in an advisory opinion has no precedential significance.

Example: The Third Judges Case, 1998, arose from a Presidential Reference under Article 143.

6. Review and Curative Review jurisdiction, Article 137: The Supreme Court can review its own judgments or orders, subject to applicable law and rules. Review is limited, for example where there is an error apparent on the face of the record; it is not a fresh appeal.

Curative petition: After dismissal of review, exceptionally narrow relief may be available to prevent a gross miscarriage of justice, such as a serious natural-justice violation. This judicially developed remedy was recognised in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002); it is not a separate jurisdiction expressly named in the Constitution.

7. Related Powers These powers overlap with the jurisdictions above rather than forming completely separate compartments.

Power Provision Meaning
Judicial review Principally Articles 13, 32 and other constitutional provisions Examines laws and State actions for constitutionality; amendments are subject to the basic structure limitation.
Court of record and contempt Article 129 Maintains authoritative judicial records and can punish contempt of itself.
Complete justice Article 142 Can make orders necessary for complete justice in a cause or matter pending before it; this is not unlimited authority to disregard substantive law.
Transfer of cases Article 139A, among other provisions Can withdraw specified cases involving common substantial legal questions and transfer cases between High Courts.
Election disputes Article 71 Decides disputes concerning elections of the President and Vice-President, and its decision is final.

10. Original jurisdiction: Article 131, exceptions and cases

1. Meaning Original jurisdiction means the power to hear a case at its beginning, rather than as an appeal against another court’s judgment.

Under Article 131, the Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction over specified federal disputes involving legal rights.

  • Original: The dispute is brought directly before the Supreme Court; no lower-court judgment is required.
  • Exclusive: No other court can entertain that dispute under this jurisdiction.
  • Federal: It concerns the Union and States in the combinations specified by the Constitution.

Think of Article 131 as the Supreme Court’s role as an independent umpire between the constituent governments of India.

2. Eligible Parties Article 131 permits these three combinations:

Combination Simple Illustration
Government of India versus one or more States The Union and a State disagree over their respective legal powers.
Government of India together with one or more States versus other State(s) The Union and State A jointly assert a legal claim against State B.
Two or more States against each other State A and State B dispute the legal location of their existing boundary.

Private individuals, companies and political parties cannot be parties to an Article 131 suit. A government-owned corporation does not become an eligible “State” merely because it falls within the wider definition of State under Article 12.

Example: A citizen’s land dispute with a State government is not an Article 131 dispute. The citizen must use the appropriate civil or constitutional remedy.

3. Legal-Right Requirement The dispute must involve a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

Expression Meaning Hypothetical Example
Existence of a right Does the claimed right or power exist? Does the Union possess legal authority to take a particular action affecting a State?
Extent of a right How far does that right or power extend? What share of a jointly owned asset legally belongs to each State?
Question of law What does the governing legal provision mean? How should a provision allocating governmental powers be interpreted?
Question of fact What actually happened or exists? Where does an established boundary lie according to the relevant records?

The right need not be a Fundamental Right. Constitutional powers, statutory rights and legally enforceable interests may qualify.

Political background does not automatically exclude a case. A dispute can be politically controversial and still raise a legal question. What is excluded is a purely political disagreement without a legal-right issue, such as dissatisfaction with another government’s political ideology.

4. Important Cases

Case Dispute and Significance
State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) West Bengal challenged Parliament’s power to authorise acquisition of State-owned property under coal-bearing areas legislation. The Court upheld the Union’s legislative power. A classic Centre–State dispute concerning legal authority.
State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) States challenged the Union’s moves towards dissolution of their Legislative Assemblies in the context of Article 356. The suits failed, but the case illustrates the distinction between political controversy and enforceable constitutional rights.
State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) Karnataka challenged the Union’s authority to appoint a commission of inquiry into allegations against its Chief Minister and ministers. The Court upheld the Union’s action; the dispute raised a legal question about governmental power.
State of Tamil Nadu v. State of Kerala (2014) In the Mullaperiyar dispute, Tamil Nadu challenged Kerala’s legislation restricting the reservoir level. The Court held the suit maintainable, invalidated the law as applied to the dam and protected Tamil Nadu’s right to raise the level to 142 feet, with supervision.

Lesson: A State can invoke Article 131 and still lose on the merits. “The Court can hear the case” and “the State’s claim is correct” are separate questions.

5. Exceptions and Limits A. Certain treaties and agreements: The proviso to Article 131 excludes disputes arising from specified treaties, covenants, agreements, engagements, sanads or similar instruments entered into before the Constitution and continuing afterwards, or instruments covered by the proviso that expressly exclude this jurisdiction. Article 363 separately bars specified disputes relating to former princely-State arrangements.

Important qualification: Do not assume every agreement signed before 1950 is excluded. In Mullaperiyar, the Court treated the 1886 lease as an ordinary, non-political agreement, so the treaty-related bar did not apply.

B. Inter-State river-water disputes: Article 262 authorises Parliament to provide a special adjudication mechanism and exclude court jurisdiction. Section 11 of the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 bars court jurisdiction over water disputes that may be referred to a tribunal under that Act.

Cauvery example: Its water-allocation dispute followed the tribunal mechanism. The Supreme Court’s major 2018 judgment arose in appeals under Article 136, not an ordinary original suit under Article 131. Thus, neither “all water disputes fall under Article 131” nor “the Supreme Court can never deal with water-related litigation” is accurate.

C. Other constitutional arrangements: Article 131 begins with “Subject to the provisions of this Constitution.” For example, Article 280 assigns recommendations on tax distribution to the Finance Commission, while Article 290 provides a particular mechanism for specified financial adjustments. Not every financial disagreement automatically becomes an Article 131 suit.

D. Challenges to legislation: Whether Article 131 can be used to challenge legislation has produced conflicting decisions and larger-bench references. Avoid an absolute “always” or “never”: the Court has examined legislation in federal suits, but maintainability must be considered in the relevant legal context.

6. How Proceedings Work The eligible government ordinarily files an original suit, stating the dispute, legal right and relief sought. The other government responds; the Court considers maintainability, identifies issues, examines evidence where necessary and delivers judgment. Relief can include a declaration of rights, injunction or other appropriate decree. No presidential reference is required.

A boundary suit determines existing legal boundaries; it is different from Parliament altering State boundaries under Article 3.

7. Article 131 Versus 32

Article 131 Article 32
Resolves specified federal legal disputes. Enforces Fundamental Rights.
Available to the specified Union/State parties. Available to eligible rights-holders, including individuals, subject to standing rules.
Exclusive to the Supreme Court for qualifying disputes. Not exclusive in protecting Fundamental Rights: High Courts also provide remedies under Article 226.

Both involve original jurisdiction because proceedings can begin directly in the Supreme Court. Presidential and Vice-Presidential election disputes are another direct jurisdiction, under Article 71, not Article 131.

Revision formula: Eligible governments + a disputed legal right + no applicable exclusion = Article 131 jurisdiction.

11. Writ jurisdiction: five writs, examples and safeguards

1. Meaning Writ jurisdiction is the power of a court to issue authoritative directions, orders or writs to protect rights and control unlawful exercise of power.

Under Article 32, the Supreme Court can issue writs to enforce Fundamental Rights guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution.

Article 131 protects the federal legal relationship between governments; Article 32 protects Fundamental Rights.

Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right. Dr B. R. Ambedkar described it as the Constitution’s “very soul” and “very heart.”

2. Essential Features

  • Original jurisdiction: A person can approach the Supreme Court directly, without first obtaining a lower-court judgment.
  • Not exclusive: High Courts also enforce Fundamental Rights through Article 226.
  • Rights-based: An Article 32 petition must concern a Fundamental Right; an ordinary contractual or statutory grievance alone is insufficient.
  • Flexible remedies: The Court can issue directions, orders and writs, not just the five traditionally named writs.
  • Access: Ordinarily, the affected person petitions; in appropriate cases, another person or a public-spirited petitioner can act for those unable to approach the Court.

Direct access does not mean every petition automatically receives a full hearing or the requested relief. The Court examines maintainability and may, where appropriate, direct the petitioner to approach the High Court.

3. Five Writs

Writ Literal Meaning Main Purpose
Habeas Corpus “You may have the body” Secure release from unlawful detention.
Mandamus “We command” Require performance of a public or legal duty.
Prohibition “To forbid” Stop a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its authority.
Certiorari “To be certified/informed” Quash an unlawful decision of a lower court, tribunal or other qualifying authority.
Quo Warranto “By what authority” Challenge a person’s legal entitlement to hold a public office.

4. Habeas Corpus The Court requires the person or authority responsible for detention to justify its legality and orders release if the detention is unlawful. Physical production is not invariably necessary for deciding the petition.

  • Available against public authorities and private persons responsible for unlawful confinement.
  • A relative, friend or another suitable person may petition on the detainee’s behalf.
  • It does not automatically invalidate a lawful arrest or detention under a competent court’s valid order.

Example: Police hold someone without lawful authority or required safeguards; a family member seeks habeas corpus. It may also address unlawful confinement by a private individual.

Noted cases: Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983) involved continued imprisonment after acquittal; the Court awarded compensation under Article 32. ADM Jabalpur (1976), which severely restricted liberty remedies during the Emergency, was expressly overruled in K. S. Puttaswamy (2017).

5. Mandamus Mandamus directs a public authority to perform a duty that the law requires it to perform.

  • The petitioner must establish a relevant legal entitlement and a corresponding duty.
  • It may reach a non-governmental body when it performs a public duty, but not a purely private obligation.
  • It can require an authority to exercise its discretion lawfully, but ordinarily cannot dictate a particular result where the law leaves that choice to the authority.
  • It does not compel a legislature to enact a particular law; constitutional immunity under Article 361 also protects the President and Governors from personal answerability to courts.

Example: A public authority unlawfully denies a constitutionally protected entitlement; the Court may direct it to fulfil its duty. If the grievance involves only a statutory right, Article 226, rather than Article 32, is ordinarily the appropriate writ route.

Noted case: Comptroller and Auditor-General v. K. S. Jagannathan (1986) explains mandamus and judicial intervention when public discretion is exercised unlawfully.

6. Prohibition Prohibition prevents a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings that exceed its jurisdiction or otherwise involve a recognised jurisdictional defect.

Example: A tribunal begins deciding a category of dispute that its governing statute does not authorise it to hear. Prohibition can stop those proceedings before the final decision.

It is preventive, not an ordinary appeal to correct every legal or factual error. A request to the Supreme Court under Article 32 must still satisfy the Fundamental Rights requirement.

7. Certiorari Certiorari quashes a decision affected by grounds such as lack or excess of jurisdiction, breach of natural justice, or an error of law apparent on the face of the record.

Example: A statutory tribunal decides against a person without giving the legally required opportunity to be heard; its decision may be quashed.

Noted case: T. C. Basappa v. T. Nagappa (1954) explained the scope of certiorari. It is a supervisory remedy, not a full rehearing on facts like an appeal.

Remember: Prohibition stops unlawful proceedings; certiorari quashes an unlawful decision. The Supreme Court is not a writ appellate court over High Court judicial decisions; challenges ordinarily follow the applicable appellate route, including Article 136.

8. Quo Warranto Quo warranto examines whether a person legally occupies a substantive public office created by the Constitution or law.

  • The appointment must violate an applicable legal requirement, such as an essential qualification.
  • A petitioner need not show personal injury.
  • It does not apply to a private office or merely because the petitioner thinks another candidate would be better.

Example: A person occupies a statutory public office despite lacking a mandatory qualification.

Noted case: University of Mysore v. C. D. Govinda Rao (1964) explains the remedy. Such challenges ordinarily arise under Article 226; Article 32 is not a general route for every unlawful public appointment.

9. Supreme Court Versus High Courts

Point Supreme Court: Article 32 High Courts: Article 226
Purpose Enforce Fundamental Rights. Enforce Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
Constitutional character The right to move the Court is itself a Fundamental Right. A constitutional remedy, but not itself a Part III Fundamental Right.
Territorial reach Can issue enforceable orders throughout India. Depends on the respondent’s location or the cause of action arising wholly or partly within the High Court’s territory.
Alternative remedy Not an absolute bar where Fundamental Rights require protection. Ordinarily relevant to discretion, subject to exceptions such as natural-justice violations or lack of jurisdiction.

10. Emergency and PIL Under Article 359, a presidential order during an Emergency may suspend the right to move courts to enforce specified Fundamental Rights. Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included in that suspension, following the 44th Amendment. Emergency therefore does not automatically abolish all writ remedies.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) relaxes standing in appropriate cases; it is not a sixth writ. In Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984), Article 32 proceedings addressed bonded labour and fundamental-rights violations.

Revision: Habeas corpus = release; mandamus = perform a duty; prohibition = stop; certiorari = quash; quo warranto = justify holding office.

12. Appellate and advisory jurisdiction

The basic distinction is:

Appellate jurisdiction: “Was the earlier court’s decision correct?”
Advisory jurisdiction: “What is the Supreme Court’s opinion on a question referred by the President?”

1. Appellate Jurisdiction Appellate jurisdiction allows the Supreme Court to hear challenges to decisions already given by courts or tribunals. It can affirm, reverse or modify a decision, or send the matter back for reconsideration.

The principal constitutional routes are Articles 132, 133, 134 and 136. Article 134A governs High Court certificates for specified appeals.

A. Constitutional Appeals Under Article 132, an appeal lies from a High Court’s judgment, decree or final order in a civil, criminal or other proceeding if the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law concerning interpretation of the Constitution.

Example: A High Court decides a dispute involving an important, unsettled question about the constitutional division of legislative powers. It may certify the case for appeal.

Key point: Merely mentioning a constitutional Article is insufficient; a substantial constitutional-interpretation question must arise.

B. Civil Appeals Under Article 133, an appeal lies from a High Court’s judgment, decree or final order in a civil proceeding if the High Court certifies both:

  1. The case involves a substantial question of law of general importance.
  2. That question needs to be decided by the Supreme Court.

Example: A civil dispute raises an unsettled interpretation of a property law affecting many similarly situated people. The High Court may grant a certificate.

Key points: There is no current monetary threshold under Article 133; the old value-based test was replaced by the 30th Amendment, 1972. Under Article 133(3), this appeal ordinarily cannot be from a single High Court judge, unless Parliament provides otherwise; Article 136 remains a distinct route.

C. Criminal Appeals Under Article 134(1), an appeal lies in these circumstances:

Route What the High Court Has Done Certificate Required?
134(1)(a) Reversed an acquittal on appeal and sentenced the accused to death. No
134(1)(b) Withdrawn a case from a subordinate court, tried it itself, convicted the accused and sentenced them to death. No
134(1)(c) Certified that the case is fit for appeal to the Supreme Court. Yes

Under Article 134(2), Parliament may enlarge criminal appellate jurisdiction. The Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970 extends the first two types of situations to life imprisonment or imprisonment of at least ten years.

Example: A trial court acquits an accused; the High Court reverses the acquittal and imposes life imprisonment. An appeal lies under the 1970 Act without a fitness certificate.

Exam trap: A High Court merely confirming a trial court’s death sentence does not, by that fact alone, satisfy Article 134(1)(a) or (b). Other appeal routes may be available.

D. Special Leave Under Article 136, the Supreme Court may, at its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from decisions of courts or tribunals in India.

  • It covers civil, criminal, labour, tax and other matters, and is not confined to High Court decisions.
  • A Special Leave Petition (SLP) asks permission to appeal; once leave is granted, the matter becomes an appeal.
  • No High Court certificate is necessary, but special leave is not an appeal available as of right.
  • Article 136 excludes courts and tribunals constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces; separate statutory appeal routes must be distinguished.

Example: A High Court refuses a certificate, but its decision allegedly causes grave injustice. The affected party may seek special leave.

Noted cases: Pritam Singh v. State (1950) stressed exceptional use of Article 136 to prevent grave injustice. Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala (2000) distinguished rejection of an SLP from disposal of an appeal: a non-speaking refusal of special leave does not mean approval of the lower court’s reasoning.

Certificate procedure: Under Article 134A, the High Court may consider certification on its own and must determine the question when an aggrieved party makes an oral application immediately after the relevant decision. It need not grant the certificate merely because it is requested.

2. Advisory Jurisdiction Under Article 143, the President may refer specified questions or disputes to the Supreme Court for its opinion. It is called a Presidential Reference.

Unlike an appeal, it need not involve correcting a lower-court judgment. It can clarify an important issue before an actual dispute fully develops.

A. Two Categories

Point Article 143(1) Article 143(2)
Subject A question of law or fact of such nature and public importance that obtaining the Court’s opinion is expedient. A dispute of the kind excluded from original jurisdiction by the proviso to Article 131, concerning specified treaties and similar instruments.
Timing The question has arisen or is likely to arise. A dispute within the specified category.
President’s choice May refer. May refer.
Court’s response May report its opinion; it can decline for appropriate reasons. Shall report its opinion after such hearing as it thinks fit.

Key points: Only the President can formally invoke Article 143, acting within the constitutional system of ministerial advice. Citizens, Governors and Chief Ministers cannot directly demand an Article 143 opinion. A reference must be heard by at least five judges, under Article 145(3).

B. Noted References

Reference Question and Significance
Kerala Education Bill (1958) Examined provisions of the proposed education law, particularly their compatibility with minority educational rights under Article 30. Shows that a proposed law can be examined through a reference.
Berubari Union (1960) Considered implementation of an India–Pakistan territorial agreement. Held that cession of Indian territory required a constitutional amendment, not merely an Article 3 law; the 9th Amendment followed.
Special Reference No. 1 of 1964 — Keshav Singh Examined the relationship between legislative privileges and judicial powers after a conflict involving the Uttar Pradesh Assembly and the Allahabad High Court.
Ayodhya Reference (1993; returned in 1994) Asked whether a Hindu temple or religious structure existed before construction of the disputed structure. The Court declined to answer, illustrating its discretion under Article 143(1).
Third Judges Case (1998) Clarified the collegium process and expanded the Supreme Court appointment collegium to CJI + four senior-most judges. Connects advisory jurisdiction with our earlier appointment discussion.

C. Effect of Opinion An advisory opinion is not an executable decree directing the President to act, and the President is not bound to accept it. However, it is not casual advice: it carries substantial judicial authority, and legal principles declared in advisory opinions can have precedential force. “Not binding on the President” should not be confused with “legally irrelevant to courts.”

13. Court of record, contempt and judicial review

These three powers protect different aspects of the justice system:

Court of Record: Preserves authoritative judicial records.
Contempt Power: Protects the administration of justice and compliance with court orders.
Judicial Review: Keeps laws and government action within constitutional limits.

1. Court of Record Under Article 129, the Supreme Court is a Court of Record, including the power to punish for contempt of itself. Article 215 gives High Courts corresponding status.

Meaning and Importance

  1. Authoritative records: Its judgments, orders and proceedings are preserved as authoritative legal records. They can establish what the Court decided without requiring the proceedings to be proved afresh.
  2. Continuity and certainty: Lawyers and courts can refer to earlier decisions, helping maintain consistency in the administration of justice.
  3. Contempt power: The Court can protect its proceedings and authority against conduct that obstructs justice.

Important distinction: The binding force of the law declared by the Supreme Court comes specifically from Article 141. Article 129 concerns court-of-record status; Article 141 concerns binding precedent. Not every sentence in a judgment is a binding legal rule.

Example: A lower court dealing with a privacy dispute must apply the relevant law declared in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017). The judgment is an authoritative record, while its binding legal principles operate through Article 141.

Noted case: M.M. Thomas v. State of Kerala (2000) emphasised that a High Court, as a court of record, has a duty to keep its records correct and the power to correct errors in them.

Exam trap: “Court of Record” does not mean its judgments can never be corrected. The Supreme Court can review its judgments under Article 137 and reconsider precedents through appropriate benches.

2. Contempt of Court Contempt involves conduct that disobeys judicial orders or interferes with the administration of justice. Its purpose is to protect justice, not shield judges from all criticism.

The principal provisions are Articles 129 and 215, together with the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971. The Act distinguishes two categories:

Type Meaning Hypothetical Example
Civil contempt Wilful disobedience of a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other court process; or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. An official deliberately refuses to reinstate an employee despite a clear court order and the ability to comply.
Criminal contempt Publication or other conduct that scandalises or tends to lower a court’s authority, prejudices judicial proceedings, or obstructs the administration of justice. Trying to intimidate a witness to prevent truthful testimony in a pending trial.

Key distinction: Civil contempt requires wilfulness. Genuine inability to comply or an accidental failure does not automatically establish contempt. Criminal contempt need not occur inside a courtroom.

Safeguards and Punishment

  • Fair criticism is permitted: Section 5 protects fair comment on the merits of a case that has been heard and finally decided. Reasoned disagreement with a judgment is not automatically contempt.
  • Fair reporting is protected: Section 4 protects fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings, subject to applicable restrictions.
  • Truth can be a defence: Under Section 13(b), introduced in 2006, the court may permit justification by truth when satisfied that it is in the public interest and the request is bona fide.
  • Substantial interference matters: Section 13 requires substantial interference, or a substantial tendency to interfere, with the due course of justice before punishment.
  • Statutory punishment: Section 12 provides for simple imprisonment up to six months, a fine up to Rs 2,000, or both. A satisfactory bona fide apology may lead to discharge or remission; it is not an automatic escape.

Noted Cases

Case What It Illustrates
P.N. Duda v. P. Shiv Shanker (1988) The Court declined contempt action over a minister’s speech, stressing that the judiciary is open to public scrutiny and criticism. Context matters.
R.K. Anand v. Registrar, Delhi High Court (2009) Attempting to influence a witness in the BMW trial led to a criminal-contempt finding. Contempt protects the integrity of trials.
In Re: Prashant Bhushan (2020) The Court found two tweets constituted criminal contempt and imposed a Rs 1 fine, with specified consequences for default. It generated debate about contempt and free speech.

Importance and impact: Contempt powers help ensure that court orders are obeyed and proceedings remain free from interference. However, excessive use can discourage legitimate criticism. Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions concerning contempt, but courts must balance that power with Article 19(1)(a) freedom of speech.

3. Judicial Review Judicial review is the power of courts to examine whether laws, constitutional amendments and executive action comply with constitutional limits, and whether administrative action complies with the law.

It reflects constitutional supremacy: neither Parliament nor the executive has unlimited power. Both the Supreme Court and High Courts exercise judicial review.

Constitutional Basis

Provision Relevance
Article 13 Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are void to the extent of the inconsistency.
Articles 32 and 226 Enable constitutional remedies through the Supreme Court and High Courts respectively; Article 226 also extends to other legal rights.
Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule Help courts determine whether the legislature had power to enact a particular law.
Article 368 and basic structure doctrine Constitutional amendments are reviewable for procedural compliance and damage to the Constitution’s basic structure.

What Can Be Reviewed?

  • Legislation: For violating Fundamental Rights or other constitutional provisions, or exceeding legislative competence.
  • Executive and administrative action: For illegality, arbitrariness, bad faith, procedural unfairness or other applicable grounds.
  • Constitutional amendments: Parliament can amend the Constitution, but cannot destroy its basic structure.

Hypothetical example: A law prohibits every form of peaceful criticism of the government. A court could examine it under Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(2), and invalidate the unconstitutional restriction.

Noted Cases

Case Decision and Impact
Kesavananda Bharati (1973) Established the basic structure doctrine, limiting Parliament’s constitutional-amending power.
Minerva Mills (1980) Invalidated provisions seeking unlimited amending power and exclusion of judicial review. Affirmed limited amending power and judicial review as basic features.
S.R. Bommai (1994) Held that proclamations imposing President’s Rule under Article 356 are judicially reviewable, strengthening protection against arbitrary dismissal of State governments.
L. Chandra Kumar (1997) Held that judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227 is part of the basic structure; tribunals cannot wholly replace constitutional-court scrutiny.
I.R. Coelho (2007) Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 remain reviewable where their impact on Fundamental Rights damages the basic structure. Ninth Schedule protection is not absolute.
Shreya Singhal (2015) Struck down Section 66A of the IT Act for violating freedom of speech, demonstrating judicial review’s direct impact on citizens.
NJAC case (2015) Struck down the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act for violating judicial independence, a basic feature. The collegium system was restored.

Importance and Limits Judicial review protects rights, preserves federalism, checks abuse of power and safeguards the Constitution’s basic structure. Courts may strike down an invalid provision, sever only the unconstitutional part, adopt a constitutionally permissible interpretation, or quash unlawful government action.

However, courts do not ordinarily invalidate a policy merely because they prefer another one. Review examines legality and constitutionality, not simply whether the judges consider a policy wise. Judicial review is also distinct from judicial activism, which describes a more interventionist judicial approach.

Final Exam Distinction Judicial review tests laws and public action against constitutional or legal standards. A review petition under Article 137 asks the Supreme Court to reconsider its own judgment on limited grounds. They are not the same power.

Memory aid: Record = authoritative proceedings; Contempt = protection of justice; Judicial review = constitutional control of public power.

Sources: Article 129 · Article 13 · Supreme Court discussion of fair criticism · Truth as a contempt defence.

14. Supreme Court as a National Court of Appeal

Supreme Court as a National Court of Appeal

The Supreme Court is called the National Court of Appeal because it is the highest and final appellate court for the whole of India. It hears eligible challenges to decisions of High Courts and, through special leave, other courts and tribunals.

Here, “national” means its authority extends throughout India, while “appellate” means it examines a decision already given by another judicial body.

1. What It Does In its appellate role, the Supreme Court can:

  • Uphold the earlier decision.
  • Reverse or modify an incorrect decision or sentence.
  • Send the case back to the appropriate court for reconsideration or a fresh trial.
  • Resolve conflicting interpretations of law and lay down a principle applicable across India.

Depending on the appeal, it can examine questions of law and, where appropriate, questions of fact. However, it does not routinely reassess evidence merely because a party disagrees with the result.

2. Constitutional Routes Its national appellate role operates through several provisions:

Article Type of Appeal
132 Constitutional appeals involving a substantial question of constitutional interpretation, with a High Court certificate.
133 Civil appeals meeting the prescribed requirements of general legal importance and need for a Supreme Court decision, with certification.
134 Criminal appeals in specified circumstances, including certain appeals available without certification.
136 Discretionary special leave to appeal from decisions of courts or tribunals, subject to the Armed Forces exception.

Certain statutes also provide appeals to the Supreme Court.

Important: Being the final appellate court does not mean every unsuccessful litigant has an automatic right to approach it. The relevant constitutional or statutory conditions must be satisfied.

3. Simple Examples Civil dispute: A High Court decides a property dispute involving an important legal question affecting people across India. If Article 133’s requirements are met and certification is granted, the Supreme Court can hear the appeal.

Criminal dispute: A High Court reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to death. An appeal lies to the Supreme Court under Article 134(1)(a) without a fitness certificate.

Grave injustice: A tribunal’s decision allegedly causes a serious miscarriage of justice. The affected party may seek special leave under Article 136, but the Court decides whether to grant permission.

4. Noted Cases

Case Connection to the National Appellate Role
Pritam Singh v. State (1950) Explained that Article 136 is an exceptional discretionary power to address serious injustice, not an unrestricted additional appeal.
M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das (2019) The Ayodhya title dispute reached the Supreme Court through civil appeals against the Allahabad High Court judgment. This was distinct from the earlier presidential advisory reference.
Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala (2000) Distinguished the request for special leave from an appeal after leave is granted. A non-speaking rejection of an SLP does not amount to endorsement of the lower court’s reasoning.

5. Why It Matters

  • Uniformity of law: Conflicting High Court interpretations can be resolved by one national authority.
  • Correction of injustice: Serious judicial errors can receive final scrutiny.
  • Integrated judiciary: State judicial systems ultimately operate within a common national judicial hierarchy.
  • Finality: Litigation ordinarily ends with the Supreme Court’s decision, subject to limited remedies such as review and curative petitions.

Under Article 141, the law declared by the Supreme Court binds all courts within India. Thus, deciding one appeal may clarify the law for thousands of later cases.

6. Dual Role

As a Constitutional Court As a National Court of Appeal
Protects constitutional supremacy, Fundamental Rights and the federal balance. Examines eligible challenges to earlier judicial decisions and corrects serious errors.
Example: Reviewing the validity of a constitutional amendment. Example: Hearing an appeal against a criminal conviction or civil decree.

These roles can overlap: an appeal may itself raise an important constitutional question.

Exam caution: This description of the Supreme Court’s existing role is different from proposals to create a separate National Court of Appeal, intended to handle ordinary appeals and leave the Supreme Court greater time for constitutional questions.

One-line answer: The Supreme Court acts as India’s National Court of Appeal by serving as the final appellate authority, correcting serious judicial errors and ensuring uniform interpretation of law throughout the country.

15. Strengthening the Constitution and the Basic Structure Doctrine

The Supreme Court strengthens the Constitution by making its limits enforceable: it protects rights, checks unconstitutional action and prevents even constitutional amendments from destroying the Constitution’s essential identity.

Its most important contribution in this area is the Basic Structure Doctrine.

1. Strengthening the Constitution

Role of the Supreme Court How It Strengthens the Constitution Example and Outcome
Protecting Fundamental Rights Makes constitutional rights enforceable, especially through Article 32. Maneka Gandhi (1978): A procedure depriving a person of liberty must be fair, just and reasonable, not arbitrary. Connected Articles 14, 19 and 21.
Exercising judicial review Invalidates laws and executive actions that exceed constitutional limits. Shreya Singhal (2015): Struck down Section 66A of the IT Act for violating freedom of speech.
Protecting federalism Prevents unconstitutional interference with State governments. S.R. Bommai (1994): Made Article 356 proclamations subject to judicial review and recognised federalism and secularism as basic features.
Interpreting evolving rights Applies constitutional guarantees to circumstances not expressly anticipated by the framers. Puttaswamy (2017): A nine-judge bench unanimously recognised privacy as a Fundamental Right.
Preserving judicial independence Protects courts’ ability to decide disputes without improper political control. NJAC case (2015): Struck down the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act; restored the collegium system.
Limiting constitutional amendments Prevents Parliament from destroying the Constitution through the amendment process. Kesavananda Bharati (1973): Established the Basic Structure Doctrine.

Key idea: The Court strengthens constitutional supremacy, not the supremacy of judges. Its own authority must also operate within the Constitution.

2. Basic Structure Doctrine The doctrine states:

Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368, but it cannot destroy or damage its basic structure.

This means the Constitution can change and develop, but it cannot be transformed into something fundamentally inconsistent with its essential principles.

Hypothetical example: Parliament may change electoral arrangements through a valid amendment. But an amendment abolishing meaningful elections and establishing permanent hereditary rule would destroy democratic government.

Important distinction: The doctrine protects essential constitutional principles, not every Article against every change. Fundamental Rights can be amended, provided the amendment does not damage the basic structure.

3. What Is Protected? The Supreme Court has recognised these among the Constitution’s basic features across different cases:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law.
  • Republican and democratic government, including free and fair elections.
  • Secularism and the federal character of the Constitution.
  • Separation of powers, judicial independence and judicial review.
  • Limited amending power of Parliament.
  • Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

There is no single exhaustive list. The Court determines whether a challenged amendment damages a basic feature by examining its nature and effect.

4. Evolution and Outcomes The doctrine developed through a disagreement over whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.

Case Main Issue Outcome and Significance
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951) Could the First Amendment restrict Fundamental Rights? First Amendment upheld. A constitutional amendment was not “law” under Article 13; Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.
Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965) Was the 17th Amendment valid? Amendment upheld. Reaffirmed Parliament’s power to amend Fundamental Rights, although separate opinions raised doubts about unlimited amendment power.
I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) Could Parliament amend Fundamental Rights? By 6:5, held that Parliament could not amend them to take away or abridge them. Applied prospectively, preserving earlier amendments.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) How extensive is Parliament’s amending power? A 13-judge bench, by 7:6, held that Parliament could amend every part, including Fundamental Rights, but not the basic structure. Upheld the 24th Amendment and displaced Golak Nath’s prohibition.
Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) Could an amendment insulate the Prime Minister’s election from normal judicial adjudication? Struck down Article 329A(4), inserted by the 39th Amendment. Applied basic-structure limits to protect constitutional principles including democracy and free and fair elections.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) Could Parliament make its amendment power unlimited and exclude judicial review? Struck down Sections 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment. Protected limited amending power, judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) Could laws placed in the Ninth Schedule receive complete protection? Established 24 April 1973, the date of Kesavananda, as the dividing line: amendments adding laws after that date are open to basic-structure scrutiny.
L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) Could tribunals replace constitutional-court judicial review? Held that review under Articles 32 and 226/227 is part of the basic structure. Tribunals supplement, rather than wholly replace, constitutional courts.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) Does Ninth Schedule inclusion automatically protect a law from challenge? No. Post-24 April 1973 insertions can be reviewed where the protected law’s impact on Fundamental Rights damages the basic structure.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015), NJAC case Was the new judicial-appointments system constitutionally valid? By 4:1, struck down the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act for violating judicial independence. The collegium system was restored.

Connection between cases: After Golak Nath, Parliament passed the 24th Amendment, 1971, expressly affirming its power to amend any constitutional provision. Kesavananda accepted that power, but imposed the basic-structure limit.

5. Why Minerva Matters The 42nd Amendment attempted two major changes examined in Minerva Mills:

  1. Section 55 inserted Article 368(4) and (5), seeking to exclude challenges to constitutional amendments and declare Parliament’s amending power unlimited.
  2. Section 4 expanded Article 31C protection from laws implementing Article 39(b)–(c) to laws implementing any Directive Principle, shielding them from Articles 14 and 19 challenges.

The Court invalidated both changes. Its central message was that a limited amending power cannot use that power to make itself unlimited, and neither Fundamental Rights nor Directive Principles should completely displace the other.

6. Importance and Significance

  • Prevents constitutional destruction: A large parliamentary majority cannot legally abolish democracy or eliminate all judicial review through an amendment.
  • Protects minorities and individuals: Constitutional essentials remain protected against temporary political majorities.
  • Balances continuity with change: Parliament retains broad reform powers while the Constitution’s essential identity survives.
  • Preserves checks and balances: No institution can use an amendment to obtain completely unchecked power.
  • Protects federalism and independent courts: These institutions remain safeguards against concentration of power.

Concern: Because the list of basic features is not exhaustive, critics argue that the doctrine gives unelected judges considerable discretion. Its justification is that constitutional amendment is a power to reform the Constitution, not to destroy it; careful, reasoned application is therefore essential.

7. Exam Distinctions

Common Mistake Correct Position
“The doctrine is expressly written in Article 368.” It is a judicially developed limitation on the power under Article 368.
“Fundamental Rights cannot be amended.” They can be amended, subject to the basic-structure limit.
“Any violation of a Fundamental Right by an amendment automatically violates basic structure.” The Court must examine whether the amendment’s impact damages a basic feature.
“Basic structure is ordinarily a standalone test for every ordinary law.” Its principal application is to constitutional amendments. Ordinary laws are ordinarily tested against constitutional provisions and legislative competence.
“Every Ninth Schedule law is immune.” Post-24 April 1973 insertions are subject to the scrutiny explained in I.R. Coelho.

Memory chain: Shankari Prasad: can amend → Golak Nath: cannot abridge Fundamental Rights → Kesavananda: can amend, but cannot destroy basic structure → Minerva Mills: cannot make amendment power unlimited.

16. Judicial activism, restraint and overreach: first discussion

These terms describe how actively courts exercise their powers and how far they go when giving remedies. They are not separate jurisdictions conferred by particular Articles.

Judicial activism: An active judicial approach to protecting rights and addressing failures of public authorities.
Judicial restraint: Respecting constitutional limits and leaving legitimate policy choices to elected institutions.
Judicial overreach: Judicial intervention that crosses those limits and takes over functions properly belonging to another institution.

1. Judicial Activism Judicial activism is an approach in which courts interpret rights and exercise their powers proactively to secure justice, particularly when existing remedies or government action are inadequate.

It may involve expanding the interpretation of rights, relaxing access to courts, issuing guidelines or monitoring compliance. Activism is not automatically unconstitutional, and a judgment does not become activist merely because it strikes down a law.

Why It Develops

  • People facing poverty or exclusion may be unable to approach courts through ordinary procedures.
  • Public authorities may fail to enforce existing laws or protect rights.
  • New problems may arise before legislation provides an adequate response.
  • A declaration of rights may be ineffective without implementation directions.

Noted Cases

Case Outcome Significance
Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) Held that a procedure depriving a person of liberty must be fair, just and reasonable; read Articles 14, 19 and 21 together. Expanded substantive protection against arbitrary State action.
Hussainara Khatoon cases (1979) Addressed prolonged detention of undertrial prisoners; recognised speedy trial as part of Article 21 and ordered relief for eligible prisoners. Brought the rights of prisoners unable to secure ordinary legal remedies into focus.
Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) Entertained a letter-based petition concerning bonded labour and issued identification, release and rehabilitation directions. Demonstrated relaxed standing and procedure for vulnerable groups.
Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) Issued binding workplace sexual-harassment guidelines in the absence of specific legislation. Filled a rights-protection gap until legislation intervened; the 2013 workplace sexual-harassment law followed.
M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, Oleum Gas Leak case (1986) Developed absolute liability for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities. Strengthened accountability for industrial harm beyond the traditional strict-liability rule and its exceptions.
Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1997) Issued directions concerning investigative agencies’ independence and functioning in the Jain Hawala matter. Used continuing judicial supervision to address institutional inaction.

Significance and Consequences Activism can make rights effective, widen access to justice and force neglected problems onto the government’s agenda. However, detailed judicial management can create implementation difficulties, budgetary pressures and dependence on courts, which may lack the expertise and information needed for ongoing administration.

2. Judicial Overreach Judicial overreach occurs when a court exceeds its legitimate constitutional role, for example by substituting its preferred policy for a lawful government choice without sufficient legal justification.

Hypothetical example: A court may require a government to stop discriminatory school admissions. But prescribing every school’s timetable, staffing pattern and budget without a demonstrated legal necessity may cross into administration.

How to Assess It Ask whether there is a legal violation, whether the remedy is connected to that violation, whether it is proportionate, and whether it leaves appropriate room for the legislature or executive to act.

Important: “Overreach” is often a contested evaluation, not a formal finding that a judgment is invalid. A controversial order is not automatically unconstitutional.

Noted Examples

Case Order or Outcome Why It Matters
Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India (2016–2018) A 2016 interim order required cinemas to play the national anthem before films. In 2018, the Court modified it, making playing the anthem optional. Critics questioned whether prescribing such a practice was a judicial function. The modification illustrates reconsideration of an expansive interim direction.
State of Tamil Nadu v. K. Balu (2016) Directed restrictions on liquor licences along national and State highways; subsequent orders clarified and modified their application. Supporters emphasised road safety; critics questioned judicial policy design and effects on livelihoods, businesses and State revenue.
Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass (2008) The Supreme Court set aside a High Court direction requiring creation of tractor-driver posts. A clearer institutional-limit example: courts should not ordinarily take over the executive’s decision to create public posts.

Issues and Consequences Overreach can weaken separation of powers, create obligations without workable funding, produce unintended economic effects and blur political accountability. Repeated intervention may also reduce public confidence if orders are inconsistent or impossible to implement.

But the opposite mistake is also important: calling every strong rights-protecting judgment “overreach” would undermine legitimate judicial review.

3. Judicial Restraint Judicial restraint means courts exercise their powers carefully, respect precedent and institutional competence, and avoid replacing lawful policy choices with their own preferences.

It does not mean that courts must obey the government or refuse to protect rights. A restrained court can still strike down an unconstitutional law.

Typical Features

  • Presuming legislation is constitutional unless a valid constitutional challenge succeeds.
  • Deciding only the questions necessary to resolve a case.
  • Giving appropriate latitude to the executive on technical, economic and administrative choices.
  • Framing remedies that correct illegality without unnecessarily taking over governance.

Noted Cases

Case Outcome Principle
BALCO Employees’ Union v. Union of India (2002) Declined to invalidate the government’s disinvestment decision. Courts do not ordinarily decide whether an economic policy is the best available policy; they examine its legality.
Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2000) The majority permitted further dam construction subject to stipulated safeguards and compliance requirements. Illustrates deference to complex development-policy decisions. The dissent and continuing debate highlight concerns about displacement and environmental protection.
Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994) Explained the limits of judicial review in government contracts. Review ordinarily examines the legality of the decision-making process, rather than functioning as an appeal on the merits of the government’s choice.
Aravali Golf Club (2008) Rejected judicial direction to create posts. Reinforced the need to respect the functions assigned to other branches.

Significance and Risks Restraint protects democratic accountability, predictability and institutional competence. But excessive deference can leave unlawful action unchecked, prolong rights violations and deny effective remedies to vulnerable people. Restraint must not become judicial abdication.

4. Related Concepts

Concept Meaning and Connection
Judicial review The legal power to test laws and public action against constitutional or legal standards. Activism and restraint describe approaches to exercising power, not alternatives to judicial review.
Public Interest Litigation (PIL) A procedural avenue permitting suitable public-interest claims, often with relaxed standing for disadvantaged groups. It is a major vehicle for activism, but not every PIL is justified.
Suo motu action A court initiates proceedings on its own notice, within its jurisdiction. It is a procedural starting point, not proof of activism or overreach.
Epistolary jurisdiction Treating letters or similar communications as petitions in appropriate cases, helping overcome barriers to access.
Continuing mandamus Keeping a case pending and issuing periodic directions or seeking reports to secure compliance, as in Vineet Narain.
Judicial legislation A critical description of courts effectively making detailed rules. Vishaka illustrates constitutionally grounded gap-filling, but not an unlimited judicial power to legislate.

5. Constitutional Balance Articles 32 and 226 provide important remedial powers, while Article 142 enables the Supreme Court to do complete justice in a pending cause or matter. None is an unlimited authority to govern.

In Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998), the Court held that contempt jurisdiction could not be used to suspend an advocate’s licence to practise as a substitute for the statutory disciplinary process. It illustrates that Article 142 cannot simply replace substantive law and institutional procedures.

Final Comparison

Approach Central Question Main Benefit Main Danger
Activism “What effective intervention is needed to protect rights?” Makes constitutional promises practical. Can drift into policy-making or administration.
Restraint “What should remain for other institutions to decide?” Preserves legitimate institutional roles. Can become excessive deference.
Overreach “Has the court crossed its legitimate limits?” Not an ideal to pursue; claimed benefits do not settle legality. Weakens institutional balance and accountability.

Exam conclusion: India needs effective judicial protection of rights combined with institutional restraint. The proper boundary depends on the legal violation, the court’s jurisdiction and the necessity and proportionality of its remedy, not simply on how bold the judgment appears.

17. Judicial activism, overreach, restraint and underreach: Articles and outcomes

These terms describe how courts exercise their powers, especially when reviewing government action, protecting rights or addressing failures by public authorities.

Activism: Courts intervene actively to enforce constitutional duties.
Overreach: Courts are criticised for going beyond their proper constitutional role.
Restraint: Courts deliberately respect the decision-making space of other institutions.
Underreach: Courts are criticised for failing to intervene when constitutional protection is necessary.

1. Core Distinctions

Concept Meaning Hypothetical Example
Judicial activism An active approach to interpreting rights and providing effective remedies, particularly where existing institutions fail. A court issues safeguards against workplace harassment when no adequate legislation exists.
Judicial overreach Judicial intervention that improperly assumes legislative or executive functions, or exceeds lawful remedial limits. A court selects an economic policy merely because it considers that policy better, without identifying a legal violation.
Judicial restraint Respecting constitutional boundaries, precedent and the expertise or discretion of elected institutions. A court declines to substitute its preferred disinvestment policy for a lawful government decision.
Judicial underreach Excessive deference or insufficient remedies despite a serious constitutional violation. A court refuses effective scrutiny of arbitrary detention merely because the executive invokes an emergency.

Important: Activism, overreach and underreach are evaluative descriptions, not separate constitutional jurisdictions. Whether a decision amounts to overreach or justified activism is often disputed. Restraint is not automatically underreach.

2. Relevant Articles No Article expressly grants a general power called “judicial activism.” Courts act through established constitutional powers:

Article Connection
13 Invalidates ordinary laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights, to the extent of the inconsistency.
14, 19 and 21 Equality, freedoms, life and personal liberty provide substantive standards for rights-based intervention.
32 and 226 Authorise constitutional remedies and writs. Article 226 additionally covers other legal rights.
136 Allows discretionary special leave to appeal, including intervention against serious injustice.
141 Makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India.
142 Allows the Supreme Court to do complete justice in a cause or matter pending before it; it is not unlimited authority to govern.
144 Requires all civil and judicial authorities in India to act in aid of the Supreme Court.
37 and 39A Directive Principles inform governance and interpretation; Article 39A supports equal justice and legal aid. DPSPs are not independently enforceable by courts.
50 Directs separation of the judiciary from the executive in the State’s public services; the broader separation-of-powers principle also follows from the constitutional scheme.
122 and 212 Protect parliamentary and State legislative proceedings from challenge merely for procedural irregularity, but not blanket immunity for substantive unconstitutionality.

3. Judicial Activism Activism commonly involves purposive interpretation of rights, accessible procedures and effective remedies where a narrow approach would leave constitutional guarantees unenforced.

Its methods include Public Interest Litigation (PIL), relaxed standing, acceptance of appropriate letters as petitions, guidelines addressing legal gaps, and continuing mandamus, where a court retains a matter to monitor compliance.

Noted Case Outcome and Significance
Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) Highlighted prolonged detention of undertrial prisoners and recognised speedy trial as part of Article 21; led to release orders and reforms in attention to legal aid.
Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) Used flexible PIL procedures to address bonded labour and directed identification, release and rehabilitation measures. Expanded practical access to justice.
M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, Oleum Gas Leak case (1986–87) Developed absolute liability for enterprises engaged in hazardous activities. Strengthened accountability beyond the traditional rule of strict liability.
Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) Issued binding workplace sexual-harassment guidelines under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21, drawing on international obligations. They operated until legislation occupied the field, notably the 2013 Act.
Prakash Singh v. Union of India (2006) Issued police-reform directions, including institutional safeguards and tenure protections, to reduce improper interference and improve accountability. Implementation has remained uneven.

Significance and consequences: Activism can make rights meaningful for disadvantaged people, expose administrative failure and prompt reform. However, detailed supervision can strain judicial capacity, create implementation difficulties and leave public agencies dependent on court directions.

PIL is a procedural vehicle, not a synonym for activism: A PIL may produce active intervention, a restrained decision or dismissal for misuse.

4. Judicial Overreach The central concern is not that a judgment displeases the government. It is whether the court crosses constitutional boundaries, especially by replacing lawful policy choices or issuing directions without an adequate legal basis.

Noted Case Outcome and Lesson
Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998) Held that the Supreme Court could not use contempt jurisdiction and Article 142 to suspend an advocate’s licence as a substitute for the statutory disciplinary process. Complete justice does not erase jurisdictional limits.
Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass (2007) Disapproved judicial directions requiring creation of posts. Emphasised that courts should not assume executive or legislative functions.
Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India (2016 order; modified 2018) Initially required cinema halls to play the national anthem before films. In 2018, playing it was made optional pending government consideration. Often debated as intervention in an area better left to policy.

Issues and consequences: Overreach can weaken separation of powers, bypass democratic accountability, impose costs without sufficient technical assessment and produce orders that are difficult to implement.

Qualification: Filling a temporary legal gap to protect established rights, as in Vishaka, is not necessarily equivalent to permanently taking over policy-making.

5. Judicial Restraint Restraint means courts intervene when legal grounds justify intervention, while avoiding substitution of their own preferences for those of competent authorities. It can include deciding cases narrowly and avoiding unnecessary constitutional rulings.

Noted Case Outcome and Significance
BALCO Employees’ Union v. Union of India (2002) Rejected the challenge to BALCO’s disinvestment. Emphasised that courts do not ordinarily assess the wisdom of economic policy merely because another policy is possible.
Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994) Explained limits of review in government contracting: courts scrutinise legality and the decision-making process, while allowing administrative discretion.
Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2000) The majority permitted further dam construction subject to conditions, including rehabilitation requirements. Reflected deference to policy and technical decisions; critics disputed whether rights received sufficient protection.

Significance: Restraint protects institutional competence, democratic choice, predictability and the judiciary’s legitimacy. Risk: If taken too far, deference may leave unlawful action or rights violations unchecked.

6. Judicial Underreach Underreach describes a court’s failure to provide protection that the Constitution requires, whether through excessive deference, unduly narrow interpretation, delay or ineffective remedies. It is a critical assessment, not a separately defined legal doctrine.

Noted Case Outcome and Subsequent Development
ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976) During the Emergency, the majority denied access to habeas corpus challenges of the kind before it under the presidential suspension order. Justice H.R. Khanna dissented. Widely regarded as a failure to protect liberty; expressly overruled in Puttaswamy (2017).
Suresh Kumar Koushal v. Naz Foundation (2013) Reversed the Delhi High Court’s reading down of Section 377 and left reform to Parliament. Criticised for inadequate protection of constitutional rights; overruled by Navtej Singh Johar (2018), which decriminalised consensual same-sex sexual conduct between adults.

Consequences: Underreach can allow arbitrary power, prolong injustice, disproportionately harm vulnerable groups and reduce confidence in constitutional remedies.

Important safeguard: Following the 44th Amendment, Article 359 cannot be used to suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of Articles 20 and 21.

7. How to Judge Ask: Is there a legal violation? Is the remedy connected to it? Does the court have authority? Is intervention proportionate and workable? Does it preserve legitimate policy discretion?

A court may properly be firm on rights and restrained on policy at the same time. Striking down an unconstitutional law is not overreach simply because Parliament passed it.

Exam conclusion: Judicial activism is valuable when it makes constitutional promises effective; restraint is valuable when it respects lawful institutional choices. Overreach crosses constitutional limits, while underreach fails to enforce them.

18. Review, Curative Petitions and Miscellaneous SC Powers

Class-review supplement: This chapter expands the 26 August class topics that appeared only briefly in the original discussions. It supplements those discussions; the embedded archive continues to preserve the original 17 explanations, not the new review material.

18.1 Article 137: reviewing the Court's own decisions

The SC may review any judgment or order made by it, subject to parliamentary law or rules under Article 145. This differs from judicial review of legislation/executive action and from an appeal to a superior court. There is no superior domestic court to which an ordinary appeal from the SC lies.

Civil review: Order XLVII of the Supreme Court Rules refers to grounds under Order XLVII Rule 1 CPC: discovery of new and important material unavailable despite due diligence; mistake/error apparent on the record; or other sufficient reason within the recognised legal limits. Criminal review: The SC Rules specify error apparent on the face of the record. Do not automatically transplant every civil-review ground into every criminal case.

An error apparent is not simply an argument requiring lengthy debate that another view is preferable. Review is not a second full hearing to improve arguments already rejected. Petitions are ordinarily filed within 30 days, subject to the applicable rules and condonation of delay, and ordinarily considered by circulation without oral argument, by the same judges as far as practicable.

Death-penalty exception: Mohd. Arif alias Ashfaq v. Registrar, Supreme Court of India (2014) recognised open-court hearing by a bench of at least three judges for review of death-sentence cases. "All reviews are decided only in chambers" is therefore inaccurate.

HC comparison: Civil review rests on Section 114 and Order XLVII CPC where applicable; constitutional writ jurisdiction also has recognised review/recall powers. There is no HC counterpart identically worded to Article 137. Court-of-record status does not by itself authorise an unrestricted criminal rehearing contrary to statutory finality rules.

18.2 Curative petition: an exceptional final safeguard

Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002) recognised narrowly confined relief after dismissal of review to prevent abuse of process and gross miscarriage of justice. It is judicially developed, drawing on the SC's inherent powers and constitutional role, not an ordinary third appeal expressly created by an Article.

Examples include a serious natural-justice failure such as an adversely affected person not being heard, or undisclosed judicial connections creating a reasonable apprehension of bias. Audi alteram partem means hear the other side; nemo judex in causa sua expresses impartial adjudication. Mere disagreement with the judgment is insufficient.

  1. State the exceptional grounds and comply with the prescribed requirement concerning their assertion in review and dismissal of review by circulation.
  2. Provide the prescribed senior-advocate certification and Advocate-on-Record certification under the SC Rules.
  3. Initial circulation is to the three senior-most judges and the judges who delivered the challenged judgment, if available, under the governing framework.
  4. If the required majority concludes that a hearing is needed, the matter is listed as directed; it is not automatically an oral hearing.
  5. File within a reasonable time under the curative rules; do not substitute the review petition's 30-day rule as a universal curative deadline. Frivolous petitions may attract costs.
Judgment → limited review → exceptionally, curative relief.
This describes possible safeguards, not three guaranteed rounds of litigation.

18.4 Article 141: precedent and bench discipline

The law declared by the SC binds all courts within India. Ratio decidendi is the legal principle necessary to the decision; a casual observation is not automatically a binding rule. A judgment must be read with its facts, issues, reasons and majority, not reduced to an isolated sentence. Reasoned legal declarations in advisory opinions require attention too; "advisory" does not mean legally irrelevant.

  • Larger bench: Its governing legal position binds smaller benches.
  • Equal-strength bench: Must follow a coordinate bench's governing precedent or seek appropriate reference; it cannot simply overrule it. The class statement permitting an "equal or greater" bench to overrule is incorrect.
  • Dissent: May influence later development but is not the controlling majority holding.
  • Distinguishing: A precedent may not govern materially different facts or legal issues; distinguishing is not overruling.
  • Sub silentio: A point that passed without conscious determination is not a binding resolution of that point merely because it arose in the background.
  • Per incuriam: A narrowly used concept concerning decisions made in ignorance of binding law; it is not a licence for a lower court to disregard an SC judgment merely because it disagrees.
  • Prospective overruling: I.C. Golak Nath (1967) introduced its use in Indian constitutional adjudication. The Court may limit the temporal effect of a new rule; the judgment determines what past transactions are protected.

Authorities and tribunals: Article 141's express wording concerns courts, but tribunals and public authorities cannot lawfully treat binding SC interpretations as optional. Article 144 separately requires all civil and judicial authorities to act in aid of the SC. Obiter observations deserve appropriate judicial respect; their weight depends on whether they actually declare a considered legal principle, not solely the label attached by a reader.

18.5 Article 142: clauses, enforcement and limits

Clause (1): In a cause or matter pending before it, the SC may make orders necessary for complete justice. They are enforceable throughout India in the manner prescribed by parliamentary law or, until provision is made, presidential order. Clause (2): Subject to parliamentary law, the Court has powers concerning attendance of persons, discovery/production of documents and investigation/punishment of contempt of itself.

Complete justice is not a freestanding power to decide any issue without jurisdiction, dispense with every statute, or violate Fundamental Rights. Nor does every case-specific Article 142 direction automatically become a general rule under Article 141. Identify the remedy, reasons and intended scope.

18.6 Articles 144-147: aid, rules, staff and interpretation

Article 144: All civil and judicial authorities in India shall act in aid of the SC. It is not the article authorising rules of procedure.

Article 145: SC rules regulate practice/procedure, subject to parliamentary law and with presidential approval. Subjects include admission of practitioners, appeals, Fundamental Rights proceedings, bail/stays, review, costs, fees and other specified procedural matters. The CJI's roster function operates within the Court's institutional framework; it is not presidential allocation of individual cases.

  • 145(2)-(3): Rules may fix bench sizes, subject to the minimum of five for substantial constitutional-interpretation questions and Article 143 references. The proviso permits an appeal bench below five to refer a necessary substantial constitutional question to the required bench.
  • 145(4): Judgments are delivered in open court; Article 143 reports follow an opinion also delivered in open court. A review considered by circulation does not erase this constitutional distinction about delivery.
  • 145(5): Judgment/report requires the concurrence of a majority of judges present at the hearing; dissenting judgments/opinions may be delivered. Every judge need not write separately.

Advocate-on-Record (AOR): Under the SC Rules, AORs ordinarily act and file for represented parties and take responsibility for the case on record. Other advocates may plead when duly instructed as the rules require. A person may appear in person subject to applicable rules, so "only an AOR may ever approach the SC" is too broad. Qualification involves prescribed practice/training, examination and other requirements, not merely passing one exam. Senior Advocate designation under the Advocates Act is different from AOR status and does not itself permit filing as an AOR.

Bench terminology: Most regular matters use two or more judges; single-judge business is confined to categories authorised by the rules, not all routine bail matters. "Full Bench always means three SC judges" is not a universal constitutional classification. Bench strength determines precedent discipline, not whether a judgment is important in public debate.

Article 146: CJI or an authorised judge/officer appoints staff. The President may by rule require UPSC consultation for specified appointments of persons not already attached to the Court. Service conditions are subject to parliamentary law and rules made by the CJI/authorised person; salary, allowance, leave and pension rules need presidential approval. Administrative expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, while fees and other court receipts form part of that Fund. Charged expenditure is not voted, but remains part of the constitutional financial process.

Article 147: An interpretive provision for this chapter: specified references to substantial constitutional-interpretation questions include interpretation of the Government of India Act, 1935 and related instruments and the Indian Independence Act, 1947. It is not a separate modern appellate jurisdiction over all historical disputes.

19. Legal-System Foundations, Comparative Models and Judicial Service Details

19.1 Legal system and common-law heritage

A legal system comprises laws, institutions and procedures: legislatures, administration, police, courts, tribunals, the legal profession, legal aid and correctional institutions. The judiciary is central but is not the whole legal system.

India inherited English common-law methods, including precedent, adversarial proceedings and reasoned adjudication, but these now operate under the Indian Constitution and extensive legislation. Common-law heritage does not mean only uncodified English law applies. Stare decisis supports consistency through precedent, subject to court hierarchy, bench strength and the legal issue decided.

Two meanings of civil law: In "civil versus criminal", civil law concerns non-criminal rights and remedies. In "common-law versus civil-law legal traditions", civil law describes a different historical/systemic tradition associated with codification. India's use of a Civil Procedure Code does not by itself classify India as a civil-law-tradition jurisdiction.

19.4 Adversarial and inquisitorial approaches

India is primarily adversarial: opposing parties present and test their cases before an impartial judge, commonly using cross-examination. In an inquisitorial model, a judge or judicial authority takes a more investigative role in developing the record. Actual systems combine features; neither model alone guarantees justice.

The judge is an impartial adjudicator, not necessarily a passive spectator. Courts may seek clarification, question witnesses under lawful powers, regulate evidence and prevent abuse while maintaining a fair opportunity for both sides. The prosecution's duty is to assist a fair trial, not secure conviction at any cost.

Strengths: Party participation, testing evidence, transparent reasons and a neutral decision-maker. Risks: Unequal resources, weak representation, intimidation, delay and tactical withholding. Article 39A legal aid, interpreters, witness protection and effective case management help make the opportunity to be heard real. No single constitutional article labels the whole system "adversarial".

Age determination: Article 124(2A) leaves determination of an SC judge's age to the authority and manner provided by parliamentary law. It differs from Article 217(3)'s HC age-dispute mechanism. 20.1 Certificate appeals and special leave

Article 134A: The HC may consider the relevant certificate on its own motion and shall determine the question when an aggrieved party makes the prescribed oral application immediately after the judgment/decree/final order/sentence. The certificate must satisfy the conditions of Article 132, 133 or 134 as relevant; Article 134A does not make every appeal automatic.

Single-judge civil decisions: The restriction on an Article 133 appeal from one HC judge is in Article 133(3), unless Parliament provides otherwise. It is not a proviso to Article 133(1), as the 25 August class note labels it. Different statutory or Article 136 routes must be assessed separately.

Criminal enlargement: The constitutional categories in Article 134(1)(a)-(b) involve death sentences after reversal of acquittal or HC withdrawal and trial. 20.3 Contempt: who initiates and which remedy?

In the Court's presence: Section 14 provides a special procedure with notice of the allegation, opportunity to defend and prescribed safeguards. Other criminal contempt: Section 15 allows the Court's own motion, the law officer's motion, or a qualifying private motion with written law-officer consent. For the SC, the relevant law officer is the Attorney General or Solicitor General; for an HC, the Advocate General under the statutory framework.

Consent is not universally required for civil contempt or a court's own motion. A communication asking the Court to act suo motu is not automatically a maintainable private contempt petition; the Court controls whether to act. HC proceedings concerning subordinate-court contempt also have the specified reference/law-officer routes.

20.4 Quick application questions

An interstate matrimonial case is pending before a Family Court. Is Article 139A(2) automatically the correct transfer provision? No. That clause concerns transfer from one HC to another; Section 25 CPC is the usual relevant SC civil-transfer route, subject to its conditions.

An equal-strength SC bench dislikes an earlier legal rule. May it overrule it? No. It follows the precedent or seeks an appropriate larger-bench reference.

A private litigant files criminal contempt without the required law-officer consent. Does calling it public interest cure the defect? No. The prescribed route still matters; the Court's independent decision to act suo motu is a different question.

The SC dismisses an SLP without reasons. Has it adopted every sentence of the HC judgment? No. Refusal of leave is distinct from appellate adjudication and declaration of law.

21. PIL, Judicial Approaches and Reform: Related Class Topics

The original Chapters 13 and 15-17 already explain judicial review, basic structure, activism, restraint, overreach and underreach. This supplement adds the missing 30 August class framework and links to the fuller related material in the HC companion.

21.1 Origins and reasons for activism

The expression judicial activism is commonly traced to Arthur Schlesinger Jr.'s 1947 Fortune article on the US Supreme Court. This dates the term, not the first assertive judicial ruling. In India, activism became especially prominent in the late 1970s and 1980s with expanded rights interpretation and liberalised standing. Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer and P.N. Bhagwati were influential contributors.

Drivers discussed in class: Threatened Fundamental Rights, executive failure to implement duties, gaps in protective legislation, expansion of Article 21 and public-interest access for disadvantaged groups. Political instability, coalition bargaining or judicial philosophy may help explain patterns but are not themselves legal authority to intervene. The remedy still needs a constitutional/statutory basis.

Benefits: Effective rights, accountability, access and urgent protection. Risks: Inadequate technical expertise, displacement of democratic deliberation, uncertain standards, unintended economic effects, prolonged judicial administration and court time diverted from ordinary cases. None proves that every assertive judgment is illegitimate. Equally, executive inaction does not entitle a court to assume unlimited policy-making power.

21.2 Public Interest Litigation: meaning and forms

PIL permits a bona fide person or organisation with sufficient interest to raise appropriate public injury or act for people unable effectively to approach the court. It relaxes ordinary locus standi, not the requirement of a legal grievance. Article 32 remains directed to Fundamental Rights; Article 226 has its wider lawful scope. PIL is not a sixth writ or a new constitutional Article.

Vishaka (1997)Workplace sexual-harassment protection through guidelines pending legislation; now read with the 2013 Act.
State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010)Checks on credentials, bona fides, factual reliability and genuine public interest; discourages abusive PIL.

Admission safeguards: Verify the petitioner's credentials and facts; identify a genuine public injury; disclose conflicts/private interests; hear affected parties; and consider appropriate alternative remedies. Courts may impose costs for frivolous or vexatious petitions. Ordinary private/service grievances generally cannot be relabelled PIL; quo warranto is a distinct exception relevant to unlawful occupation of public office.

21.4 Additional class examples: remedy and institutional boundary

  • Right to food: PUCL orders from 2001 included cooked mid-day meals in government and government-assisted primary schools. Distinguish enforcement of entitlements from a general power to administer every welfare programme.
  • Delhi public transport: M.C. Mehta vehicular-pollution litigation included CNG conversion and compliance directions. This is a different proceeding from the Oleum Gas Leak case.
  • Forests: T.N. Godavarman proceedings from 1996 illustrate continuing environmental supervision and the debate about its long-term institutional limits.
  • Advance medical directives: Common Cause (2018) recognised a framework for withholding/withdrawing life-sustaining treatment and advance directives; procedural directions were modified in 2023. Not blanket authorisation of active euthanasia.
  • Film scrutiny: The Bombay HC's intervention in Jolly LLB 2 (2017) prompted debate over judicial review of certification versus fresh court-led censorship. The existence of CBFC jurisdiction does not immunise certification from lawful review.
  • School-attendance direction: A 2015 Allahabad HC order concerning public officials' children raised concerns over personal choice and policy substitution. Treat it as a historical controversy, not a verified current nationwide obligation.
  • Firecrackers: The 2017-2018 litigation involved different restrictions, permitted-use windows and green-cracker requirements. Do not conflate all orders into an unqualified nationwide ban or declare all environmental intervention devoid of constitutional authority.

Chapter 16 already discusses the national-anthem cinema orders and highway liquor restrictions. For all examples, distinguish the initial order, later modifications, legal basis and competing arguments. "Activism" and "overreach" are evaluative labels rather than two separate statutory lists of cases.

21.5 Judicial review: class clarifications

Article 13(4) was introduced by the 24th Amendment, 1971, not the 42nd. It does not eliminate basic-structure review of amendments. Ordinary statutes are ordinarily tested against constitutional provisions and competence, while structural principles inform that assessment; the original basic-structure chapter explains the distinction.

Legislative competence: Pith and substance concerns the law's true nature; incidental overlap is not necessarily invalid. Colourable legislation concerns evasion of constitutional limits, not merely alleged political motives. Parliament also has recognised constitutional routes to legislate on State subjects, so a State List connection alone does not settle validity.

Rights review: Maneka Gandhi requires fair, just and reasonable procedure, reading Articles 14, 19 and 21 together; the words "procedure established by law" were not formally replaced by "due process". A presumption of constitutionality is a starting principle, not immunity or an unchanging burden on the challenger in every case.

Comparative caution: The US is not uniformly "broader" and the UK does not have "no judicial review". Compare particular powers, rights, standing, executive action, primary legislation and amendments using Chapter 19's table. Federal US courts generally do not advise on pending bills; Article 143 in India has different constitutional conditions.

Quote current vacancies and pendency only with date and court coverage.

Language: Article 348 supplies the English-language framework for SC/HC proceedings and authoritative texts, subject to constitutional/statutory qualifications. This is not a universal Hindi authorisation for SC proceedings.

Calendar: Evaluate scheduling, staggered leave and actual workload, including preparation and judgment writing. Annual working days differ by court and year; an unverified figure such as 190 sitting days is not a permanent constitutional rule.

NJDG: The National Judicial Data Grid supports analysis of case institution, disposal, age and pendency. Check reporting coverage and update date before quoting a dashboard number. It is distinct from the class's unverified proposed tribunal-data-grid arrangement.

ADR and related institutions: The HC/ADR companion gives the fuller treatment of tribunals, legal aid, specialised courts and mediation. Section 12A commercial pre-institution mediation must not be confused with a mandatory process for all civil suits under the Mediation Act; the latter's commencement needs provision-specific checking.

21.7 Mains frameworks

Judicial independence and appointments: Constitutional safeguards → Judges Cases → collegium benefits and concerns → comparative alternatives → constitutionally sound transparency/diversity reforms. Conclude that independence protects litigants and lawful adjudication, not institutional immunity.

PIL and constitutional governance: Standing and access → representative cases → enforceable rights and remedies → abuse, expertise and separation concerns → admission safeguards, proportionality and compliance limits.

SC constitutional versus appellate role: Articles 32/131/136 and constitutional references → access and error correction → docket pressures → judicial capacity, filtering and regional-access proposals. Distinguish proposed structural changes from sitting elsewhere under Article 130.

Rapid Revision

  • Formation: established 26 January 1950; inaugural sitting 28 January 1950. First CJI: H. J. Kania.
  • Organisation: Articles 124-147. Sanctioned strength under the 2019 amendment: CJI plus 33 judges; retirement at 65.
  • Appointments: President formally appoints. CJI succession convention is senior-most judge considered fit; other Supreme Court appointments use CJI plus four senior-most judges.
  • Judges Cases: 1981 executive primacy; 1993 judicial primacy and CJI plus two; 1998 CJI plus four; 2015 NJAC struck down.
  • Removal: proved misbehaviour or incapacity; each House needs a majority of total membership AND at least two-thirds present and voting; addresses in the same session; President orders removal.
  • Special judges: 126 acting CJI; 127 ad hoc serving High Court judge; 128 attendance of retired judges.
  • Jurisdictions: 131 federal disputes; 32 Fundamental Rights; 132 constitutional appeals; 133 civil; 134 criminal; 136 special leave; 137 review; 143 presidential advice.
  • Advisory distinction: President may refer under either clause; Court may report under 143(1), shall report under 143(2).
  • Different powers: 129 authoritative records and contempt; 141 binding law; 142 complete justice. Judicial review is not the same as Article 137 review.
  • Basic structure: Kesavananda Bharati, 24 April 1973. Parliament may amend, but cannot destroy the Constitution's essential identity.
  • Judicial approaches: activism enforces rights proactively; restraint respects lawful institutional choices; overreach exceeds limits; underreach fails to enforce them.
  • Review: Article 137; generally 30-day filing under the rules. Curative is exceptional, not another appeal; death-review oral-hearing safeguards differ from ordinary circulation.
  • Transfer: 139A(1) common substantial legal questions; 139A(2) HC-to-HC. Interstate district-level civil transfers commonly use Section 25 CPC.
  • Precedent: equal bench follows or refers; larger bench may overrule. Non-speaking SLP refusal does not adopt the lower court's reasoning.
  • Procedure and staff: 144 aid; 145 rules/open-court delivery/majority; 146 establishment; 147 interpretation.
  • Contempt: Section 20 one-year initiation limit; Section 19 distinguishes HC single-judge and bench appeals. Consent is not required for every kind of contempt.
  • Financial security: charged is not voted, but appropriation is still required.
  • PIL: representative/citizen standing; suo motu initiation; epistolary access; continuing mandamus. None removes the need for a genuine legal grievance.
