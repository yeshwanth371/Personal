Labels used: [Correction] fixes an error or imprecise statement in the rough notes. [Additional] adds a relevant exam fact. [Not mentioned in the Constitution] identifies an academic, legal or political classification rather than constitutional wording.

1. Removal of Supreme Court and High Court Judges

Constitutional Basis

  • Article 124(4): A Supreme Court judge may be removed by an order of the President after each House of Parliament passes an address in the same session on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Article 124(5): Parliament may regulate by law the procedure for presenting the address and investigating and proving the alleged misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Article 217(1)(b) read with Article 218: The same removal mechanism applies to High Court judges.

Required Majority

In each House separately, the address must be supported by:

  1. A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  2. A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.
Special Majority Type 2 [Not mentioned in the Constitution]: This coaching/textbook label is commonly used for the combined majority above. The Constitution states the two numerical requirements but does not name them "Type 2".

Procedure under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

[Correction] The inquiry committee is not necessarily chaired by the CJI or a Supreme Court judge. Section 3 of the Judges (Inquiry) Act specifies its membership: one member chosen from among Supreme Court judges, one from among Chief Justices of High Courts, and one distinguished jurist. Where notices are admitted in both Houses on the same day, the committee is constituted jointly under the statutory procedure.
Exam Point: Parliament passes an address; the President removes the judge by order. "Impeachment of a judge" is common shorthand, but that expression is not used in Articles 124 and 217.

Restriction on Discussion of Judicial Conduct

ProvisionRule
Article 121Parliament cannot discuss the conduct of a Supreme Court or High Court judge in discharge of judicial duties except upon a motion for presenting an address to the President praying for removal.
Article 211A State Legislature cannot discuss the conduct of a Supreme Court or High Court judge in discharge of judicial duties. Unlike Article 121, it contains no removal-motion exception because State Legislatures have no role in removal.
[Correction] Article 211 is not an MLA-level equivalent permitting discussion during a removal motion. Removal is exclusively a parliamentary process; Article 211 imposes an unqualified bar in State Legislatures.

[Correction] "Justice Swamy Trisen, 2019" is a mistaken name and date. The relevant Calcutta High Court judge was Justice Soumitra Sen, and the Rajya Sabha vote and resignation occurred in 2011. He resigned after the first House passed the motion, not after both Houses failed it.
[Additional] In Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability v. Union of India (1991), arising from the Justice V. Ramaswami proceedings, the Supreme Court held that an admitted removal motion and the statutory inquiry did not lapse merely because the Lok Sabha was dissolved. The Judges (Inquiry) Act creates a special constitutional process distinct from ordinary pending business.

Position to date: No Supreme Court or High Court judge has been removed through completion of the Article 124(4) process.

2. Resignation of Judges

  • A Supreme Court judge may resign by writing addressed to the President under Article 124(2)(a).
  • A High Court judge may resign by writing addressed to the President under Article 217(1)(a).
[Correction] In Union of India v. Gopal Chandra Misra (1978), the Supreme Court held that a resignation may be made effective from a future date and requires no acceptance by the President. Crucially, a prospective resignation may be withdrawn before it becomes effective. The rough note omitted this central holding.
[Additional] Resignation can prevent completion of removal proceedings because the person ceases to hold judicial office, but it does not automatically erase possible civil or criminal liability arising from the underlying conduct.

3. Oath and Post-Retirement Practice

Oath or Affirmation

  • Under Article 124(6), a Supreme Court judge makes and subscribes an oath or affirmation before the President or a person appointed by the President.
  • The form appears in the Third Schedule.
  • The judge swears true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India, to perform duties without fear or favour, affection or ill-will, and to uphold the Constitution and the laws.

Practice after Leaving Office

Former officeConstitutional restriction
Supreme Court judgeArticle 124(7): cannot plead or act in any court or before any authority within India.
Permanent High Court judgeArticle 220: cannot plead or act before any authority in India except the Supreme Court and the other High Courts.
[Correction] Article 220 permits a former permanent High Court judge to practise in the Supreme Court and High Courts other than the High Court in which that person held permanent judicial office. The earlier version incorrectly omitted the word "other." A former Supreme Court judge faces the broader Article 124(7) prohibition.

Post-Retirement Public Employment

The Constitution contains no general cooling-off period that prohibits retired constitutional-court judges from accepting commissions, tribunals, governorships or other public appointments. Particular offices may have their own eligibility rules.

[Correction] The rough note's attribution of a universal two-year cooling-off recommendation to "the Law Commission" could not be verified reliably from an identified Law Commission report. [Verification Required] Quote a specific report number and paragraph before using that attribution. The broader reform proposal is frequently debated because immediate appointments can create a perception affecting judicial independence.

4. Salaries, Allowances and Financial Security

Article 125 and the Second Schedule

  • Supreme Court judges receive salaries determined by Parliament by law.
  • Until Parliament so provides, the Second Schedule applies. Current salaries and service conditions are principally governed by parliamentary legislation, not fixed operationally by the Schedule alone.
  • Allowances, leave and pension rights may be determined by law; after appointment, these cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage.
[Correction] Article 125 does not say that the Second Schedule permanently fixes current salaries. It supplies the constitutional fallback "until Parliament by law otherwise provides." Parliament has enacted the Supreme Court Judges (Salaries and Conditions of Service) Act, 1958.
[Additional] Financial Emergency exception: Under Article 360(4)(b), a presidential direction may require reduction of salaries and allowances of persons serving the Union or a State, including Supreme Court and High Court judges. This is the principal constitutional exception to the protection against disadvantageous variation.

Charged Expenditure and the Annual Financial Statement

  • Article 112: The President causes the Annual Financial Statement to be laid before both Houses. In practice, the Finance Minister presents the Union Budget.
  • It separately shows expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India and other expenditure proposed to be made from the Fund.
  • Under Article 112(3)(d), salaries, allowances and pensions payable to or in respect of Supreme Court judges are charged expenditure.
  • Charged expenditure is discussed but is not submitted to vote in Parliament; voted expenditure is submitted as demands for grants.
  • An Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund for both voted grants and charged expenditure.
  • A Finance Bill [Not mentioned in the Constitution as a defined category] commonly gives effect to the Government's taxation and revenue proposals. Constitutional terminology instead expressly defines a Money Bill in Article 110 and refers to financial Bills in Article 117.
[Correction] Charged expenditure does not bypass appropriation. It is not voted upon, but it is included in the Appropriation Bill before withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund.

[Correction] Article 128 is not limited to disposing of pending cases, and it uses allowances determined by presidential order rather than saying that the President fixes every "payment." Article 127 similarly speaks of jurisdiction, powers, privileges and duties, not a general constitutional entitlement to the regular salary of an SC judge.

6. Seat of the Supreme Court: Article 130

The Supreme Court sits in Delhi or in such other place or places as the CJI may, with the approval of the President, appoint from time to time.

[Additional] Article 130 permits other sitting places but does not itself create permanent regional benches. Any use of another place requires the CJI's decision and presidential approval.

7. Original Jurisdiction: Article 131

Original jurisdiction means that proceedings can begin directly in that court. Article 131 gives the Supreme Court exclusive original jurisdiction over a dispute, insofar as a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends, between:

  1. Government of India and one or more States;
  2. Government of India and one or more States on one side, and one or more other States on the other; or
  3. Two or more States.
Exam Point: The parties must fit Article 131 and the dispute must concern a legal right. A private party cannot invoke Article 131. A Union Territory is not a "State" for this provision merely because it has a legislature.

Exclusions and Qualifications

ProvisionEffect
Proviso to Article 131Excludes disputes arising from specified pre-Constitution treaties, agreements, covenants and similar instruments that continue in operation or provide that the Court lacks jurisdiction.
Article 262Allows Parliament to provide for adjudication of inter-State river-water disputes and to exclude the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other courts. Section 11 of the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 does so for covered disputes.
Article 363Separately bars court interference in certain disputes arising from specified pre-Constitution treaties, agreements, covenants, engagements, sanads and similar instruments.
[Correction] There is no general Article 131 exception for every adjustment of salaries or pensions or every Finance Commission award. Article 290 specifically concerns adjustment in respect of certain expenses and pensions, while Finance Commission recommendations operate through Articles 280 and 281. These should not be memorised as blanket Article 131 exclusions.
[Correction] Exclusion of river-water disputes under Article 262 is not an "exception against Article 136." When Parliament validly excludes the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other courts for a covered dispute, a party cannot restore that barred jurisdiction merely by styling the proceeding as a special-leave petition.

8. Appellate and Special-Leave Jurisdiction

Constitutional, Civil and Criminal Appeals

ArticleScopeKey condition
132Constitutional mattersHigh Court certifies a substantial question of law as to interpretation of the Constitution.
133Civil mattersHigh Court certifies both that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance and that the question needs decision by the Supreme Court.
134Criminal mattersSpecified death-sentence cases, a fit-case certificate, and any further jurisdiction conferred by Parliament.
134ACertificate for Articles 132, 133 and 134High Court considers the certificate on its own motion or on an oral application by the aggrieved party immediately after judgment.
[Additional] Under the proviso to Article 133(1), no civil appeal ordinarily lies to the Supreme Court from the judgment, decree or final order of one judge of a High Court, unless Parliament provides otherwise by law.

Criminal Appeals

Under Article 134(1), an appeal lies where a High Court:

  1. On appeal, reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to death;
  2. Withdraws a case from a subordinate court, tries it itself, convicts the accused and sentences the accused to death; or
  3. Certifies under Article 134A that the case is fit for appeal to the Supreme Court.
[Correction] The constitutional text in Article 134(1)(a) and (b) expressly refers to a death sentence. Parliament expanded these categories to include life imprisonment or imprisonment for at least ten years through the Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970. The earlier BNSS attribution was incorrect.

Special Leave to Appeal: Article 136

  • The Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave from a judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order of any court or tribunal in India.
  • It is an extraordinary residual power, not an automatic right of appeal.
  • Article 136(2) excludes courts or tribunals constituted by or under laws relating to the Armed Forces.
[Correction] The broad statement that the Supreme Court has declared Article 136 itself to be part of the basic structure requires a specific judgment and proposition and was not reliably verified. Judicial review and judicial independence are recognised basic-structure features, but that does not justify treating every formulation about Article 136 as settled law.
[Additional] SLP, merger and precedent: A non-speaking order merely dismissing a special-leave petition ordinarily does not cause the lower judgment to merge into the Supreme Court's order and does not declare law under Article 141. Once leave is granted, the matter becomes an appeal and the appellate decision attracts the doctrine of merger. This distinction was systematised in Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala (2000).

[Additional] A curative petition [Not mentioned in the Constitution] is a narrowly available judicial remedy developed in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002), ordinarily invoked after dismissal of a review petition to prevent a gross miscarriage of justice.

9. Writ Jurisdiction

Writs are extraordinary public-law remedies used to protect liberty, control unlawful public action and enforce rights. The Constitution empowers the Supreme Court under Article 32 and High Courts under Article 226.

BasisSupreme Court: Article 32High Court: Article 226
PurposeEnforcement of Fundamental Rights.Enforcement of Fundamental Rights and "for any other purpose," including legal rights.
NatureThe right to move the Supreme Court is itself guaranteed by Article 32(1).Constitutional power; relief remains discretionary and is ordinarily subject to principles such as availability of an efficacious alternative remedy.
Territorial reachOperates throughout India within the Court's constitutional jurisdiction.Linked to the authority's location or to where the cause of action arises wholly or partly.
[Correction] It is too absolute to say the Supreme Court is always "bound to issue a writ" whenever a violation is alleged. Article 32 guarantees access for enforcement of Fundamental Rights, but the petitioner must establish the right, violation, maintainability and entitlement to the appropriate remedy. High Courts also retain discretion; the alternative-remedy rule is ordinarily a rule of self-restraint, not a total absence of jurisdiction.

Emergency and Article 359

During an Emergency, a presidential order under Article 359 may suspend the right to move any court for enforcement of specified Part III rights and suspend proceedings for those rights for the period stated. Rights under Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included.

[Correction] Article 359 does not literally suspend Article 32 or Article 226 as institutional jurisdictions. It suspends the right to move courts for enforcement of the Fundamental Rights specified in the presidential order. High Court jurisdiction for independent legal rights remains available, subject to the facts and other lawful restrictions.

Article 139: Parliament may by law confer on the Supreme Court power to issue directions, orders or writs for purposes other than those mentioned in Article 32(2), provided those powers are not inconsistent with the Constitution.

Five Writs

WritLiteral ideaCore use
Habeas corpus"Produce/have the body"Tests the legality of detention and secures release where detention is unlawful.
Mandamus"We command"Commands a public authority to perform a public or statutory duty.
Prohibition"To prohibit"Prevents an inferior court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond jurisdiction or contrary to law.
Certiorari"To be informed/certified"Quashes an order of an inferior court, tribunal or authority for jurisdictional or serious legal error.
Quo warranto"By what authority"Challenges a person's legal authority to hold a substantive public office.
[Additional] Prohibition is principally preventive and operates while proceedings are pending; certiorari is principally corrective and commonly quashes an order already made. Their practical boundaries can overlap.
[Additional] Special writ rules: Habeas corpus may issue against unlawful private detention, not only State detention. Mandamus generally requires a public or statutory duty and does not ordinarily issue against a purely private person or against the President or a Governor for exercise of constitutional powers. For quo warranto, the applicant need not prove personal injury because the court examines the holder's legal authority to occupy a public office.

10. Courts of Record and Contempt

CourtProvisionExpress consequence
Supreme CourtArticle 129Court of record with all powers of such a court, including power to punish for contempt of itself.
High CourtArticle 215Court of record with all powers of such a court, including power to punish for contempt of itself.
  • Their judicial records have authoritative evidentiary value and are preserved according to law and court rules.
  • Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions imposed by law on freedom of speech and expression in relation to contempt of court.
  • The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 defines civil and criminal contempt, regulates jurisdiction and procedure, prescribes punishment and provides safeguards and appeals.
  • Article 142(2), subject to parliamentary law, supports the Supreme Court's powers concerning attendance, production of documents and investigation or punishment of contempt of itself.

Civil and Criminal Contempt

TypeStatutory meaning
Civil contemptWilful disobedience of a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other court process, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court.
Criminal contemptPublication or an act that scandalises or lowers the authority of a court, prejudices or interferes with a judicial proceeding, or obstructs the administration of justice in another manner.
[Additional] Statutory safeguards: The 1971 Act protects innocent publication, fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings, and fair criticism of a finally decided case. Truth may be permitted as a defence when it is in the public interest and invoked bona fide. Section 20 ordinarily bars initiation after one year from the alleged contempt, while section 19 provides specified appeals from High Court contempt orders.
[Correction] The Supreme Court's express status and inherent power to punish for contempt of itself come from Article 129, not Article 142 alone. Article 142(2) supplements the Court's operational powers and is expressly subject to law made by Parliament.
[Correction] "Court of record" does not mean every record can literally never be destroyed or that every observation in every judgment is binding. Record retention follows applicable rules; nationwide binding force for the law declared by the Supreme Court comes from Article 141.

12. Key Facts for Prelims

  • Grounds for removal: proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Removal notice: at least 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members.
  • Each House must pass the address in the same session by both components of the Article 124(4) majority.
  • Articles 121 and 211 restrict legislative discussion of judicial conduct, but only Parliament has the removal role.
  • No SC or HC judge has yet been removed through the completed constitutional process.
  • An admitted removal motion and statutory inquiry do not lapse merely on dissolution of the Lok Sabha.
  • Oath: Article 124(6) and Third Schedule; former SC judge's practice bar: Article 124(7); former permanent HC judge: Supreme Court and other High Courts under Article 220.
  • Article 360 permits reduction of judicial salaries and allowances during a Financial Emergency.
  • Acting CJI: 126; ad hoc judge: 127; retired judge: 128; seat of SC: 130.
  • Article 131 requires specified governmental parties plus a dispute involving a legal right.
  • Constitutional, civil, criminal and special-leave appeals: Articles 132, 133, 134 and 136.
  • Review: 137; transfer: 139A; complete justice: 142; advisory jurisdiction: 143.
  • SC writs enforce Fundamental Rights; HC writs also enforce other legal rights.
  • SC and HCs are courts of record under Articles 129 and 215.

13. Mains Points

  • The high removal threshold protects decisional independence but leaves a gap for misconduct too serious for informal correction yet unlikely to secure a parliamentary supermajority.
  • Resignation during proceedings can frustrate institutional fact-finding; reform may preserve completion of an inquiry without treating it as removal from an office already vacated.
  • Post-retirement appointments should balance use of judicial expertise against perceived incentives; transparent selection and a carefully designed cooling-off rule are possible safeguards.
  • Financial security, protected tenure and charged expenditure insulate judges from routine political pressure.
  • Articles 32 and 226 make constitutional remedies central to rule of law; High Courts' broader reach also supports decentralised rights protection.
  • Article 136 should remain exceptional so that the Supreme Court does not become a routine final court for every factual or procedural dispute.

TrapCorrect position
Parliament removes a judge.Parliament passes the address; the President issues the removal order.

The inquiry committee must be chaired by the CJI.No. The Act specifies three categories of members, not that chairmanship rule.
Justice Soumitra Sen's case occurred in 2019.Rajya Sabha passed the motion and he resigned in 2011.
A prospective judicial resignation cannot be withdrawn.Gopal Chandra Misra permits withdrawal before the future effective date.
A former permanent HC judge may practise in the same High Court.No. Article 220 permits practice in the Supreme Court and other High Courts.
Judicial salaries can never be reduced.Article 360 permits reduction of salaries and allowances during a Financial Emergency.
Charged expenditure needs no appropriation.It is not voted, but withdrawal is still covered by appropriation.

Q1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014, General Studies Paper I

The power of the Supreme Court of India to decide disputes between the Centre and the States falls under its

(a) advisory jurisdiction
(b) appellate jurisdiction
(c) original jurisdiction
(d) writ jurisdiction

Answer: (c) original jurisdiction
Explanation: Article 131 confers exclusive original jurisdiction over specified Union-State and inter-State disputes when the existence or extent of a legal right is involved.

Q2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021, General Studies Paper I

With reference to the Indian judiciary, consider the following statements:

  1. Any retired judge of the Supreme Court of India can be called back to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge by the Chief Justice of India with the prior permission of the President of India.
  2. A High Court in India has the power to review its own judgement as the Supreme Court does.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only
(b) 2 only
(c) Both 1 and 2
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (c) Both 1 and 2
Explanation: Article 128 permits the CJI, with the previous consent of the President, to request a qualified former judge to sit and act; the former judge must consent. A High Court, as a court of record under Article 215, possesses an inherent review power subject to law.
