1. Chief Minister

1.1 Constitutional Position and Appointment

Part VI, Chapter II, Articles 153-167 concerns the State executive. Under Article 163(1), the Council of Ministers has the Chief Minister at its head and aids and advises the Governor, except in constitutionally authorised discretionary matters. Under Article 164(1), the Governor appoints the CM; other ministers are appointed by the Governor on the CM's advice.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] The CM as the real executive head and the Governor as the nominal/constitutional head are descriptions of the parliamentary arrangement. They do not mean the Governor is legally powerless or that the CM can disregard constitutional limits.

SituationAppointment principle
Clear Assembly majorityOrdinarily appoint the leader of the party or alliance able to command the Legislative Assembly's confidence.
Hung AssemblyIdentify a person likely to command confidence and require its timely demonstration on the floor of the Assembly. Being leader of the single largest party does not by itself establish a majority.
Change of leader during an Assembly termA successor may form the government without a fresh general election if the government commands Assembly confidence.
Exam distinction: The people do not directly elect a person to the constitutional office of CM. Article 164 provides appointment by the Governor within a system of legislative responsibility, not a separate popular election to the executive office.

1.2 Membership, Eligibility and the Six-Month Rule

  • The CM may be a member of the Legislative Assembly or, where one exists, the Legislative Council. Collective responsibility nevertheless remains to the Assembly.
  • Article 164(4): a minister, including the CM, who is not a member of the State legislature for six consecutive months ceases to be a minister when that period expires. An otherwise eligible non-member can initially be appointed.
  • Article 173: the constitutional minimum age for membership is 25 for the Assembly and 30 for the Council; citizenship, the prescribed oath/affirmation and applicable statutory qualifications also matter.
  • The six-month provision does not remove disqualifications under the Constitution or election law, and is not permission to govern indefinitely through repeated non-member appointments.
Do not confuse the two six-month rules: Article 164(4) concerns a minister's non-membership; Article 174(1) concerns the maximum interval between legislative sessions. Neither grants the CM a fixed six-month tenure irrespective of confidence or eligibility.

Case-law support: S. R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab (2001) rejects repeated appointment without obtaining legislative membership during the same Assembly's term. B. R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu (2001) establishes that the non-member route cannot bypass membership qualifications and disqualifications. See the case revision table.

1.3 Oath, Tenure, Resignation and Continuity

  • The Governor administers the ministerial oaths of office and secrecy under Article 164(3), using the Third Schedule. These differ from a legislator's oath under Article 188.
  • The CM has no separately guaranteed five-year term. Article 164(1)'s pleasure provision operates with Assembly confidence and parliamentary government, not as a routine personal power to dismiss a majority-backed ministry.
  • The CM tenders resignation to the Governor. A CM's resignation or death ends that ministry as constituted; a successor-led Council must be formed. Individual ministers' departures do not necessarily end the whole government.
  • An outgoing ministry may be asked to continue temporarily pending its successor. Following a CM's death, a constitutionally appropriate interim arrangement may be needed; a Deputy CM does not automatically succeed.
  • Loss of majority should ordinarily be established through the Assembly. If a ministry loses confidence and refuses to resign, dismissal may follow; dissolution is not automatic if an alternative government is possible.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] Deputy Chief Minister is not a separate constitutional office with independent succession powers. A person given that designation is a minister under Article 164. Caretaker government describes a transitional arrangement; it is not a separate category of executive created by the Constitution.

2. State Council of Ministers: Article 164

Article 163 establishes aid and advice; Article 164 supplies the principal rules governing the ministry. The Union's Article 75 discussion on 18 September is a useful comparison, but is not a substitute for these State provisions.

Amendment links: the 91st Amendment Act, 2003 inserted clauses 164(1A)-(1B). The 94th Amendment Act, 2006 updated the tribal-welfare proviso by replacing Bihar with Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand; Madhya Pradesh and Odisha remain included. Do not confuse these two amendments.

Small-Assembly exception: In a 60-member Assembly, 15% is 9, but the constitutional minimum of 12 governs. Count the CM within 12. This State minimum does not extend to the Union or Delhi.

2.2 Responsibility and Legislative Participation

  • Collective responsibility: the whole Council stands or falls together before the Assembly. Ministers publicly support settled Cabinet decisions; one who cannot do so is expected to resign.
  • No-confidence: an Assembly no-confidence defeat affects the entire ministry, including ministers who belong to the Legislative Council. The Council cannot remove the ministry through a binding no-confidence vote.
  • Individual responsibility: the pleasure rule permits removal of an individual minister on the CM's advice without necessarily bringing down the whole Council.
  • Legal accountability: ministers have no general Article 361 immunity. Article 163(3)'s protection against inquiry into ministerial advice does not make all governmental actions immune from judicial review.
  • Article 177: every minister may speak and participate in the Assembly, the Council where present, and a legislative committee of which the minister is named a member. The Article itself confers no vote; a minister votes by virtue of membership of the relevant House.
Participation is not membership: A non-member minister can participate under Article 177 during a lawful non-member period, but cannot vote in either House merely as a minister. A Council-member minister can speak in the Assembly but cannot vote there merely by holding ministerial office.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] Article 164 does not prescribe a mandatory tier-by-tier ministerial hierarchy. Article 352(3)'s express definition of the Union Cabinet should not be described as a constitutional definition of every State Cabinet.

3. Advocate General: Articles 165, 177 and 194

3.1 Appointment, Qualifications and Conditions

PointConstitutional position
Office and appointmentArticle 165(1): the Governor appoints a person qualified for appointment as a High Court judge as Advocate General for the State.
QualificationsArticle 217(2): Indian citizen with at least ten years in judicial office in India, or at least ten years as an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession, subject to the constitutional counting provisions.
Tenure and removalArticle 165(3): holds office during the Governor's pleasure. The Constitution prescribes no fixed term or judge-style removal procedure for this law officer.
RemunerationDetermined by the Governor under Article 165(3), within the constitutional government framework.
Service conditionsCheck applicable State rules and appointment terms for professional practice, conflicts, fees and other conditions. The Union law officers' 1987 Rules are not automatically the State's rules.
Qualification trap: High Court eligibility has no distinguished-jurist alternative. That alternative belongs to Supreme Court judge eligibility and therefore the Attorney General of India's qualification framework. Nor does appointment as Advocate General make the person a High Court judge.

3.2 Duties, Participation and Privileges

  • Article 165(2): advise the State government on legal matters and perform other legal duties referred or assigned by the Governor; discharge functions conferred by the Constitution or other law.
  • Represents the State in litigation as required under the applicable legal and service framework. Article 165 does not reproduce Article 76(3)'s express all-courts right-of-audience clause for the Attorney General.
  • Article 177: may speak and participate in the Assembly, the Council if any, and a legislative committee of which the Advocate General is named a member; no vote by virtue of that Article.
  • Article 194(4): applies the relevant legislative freedom-of-speech, privilege and immunity provisions to constitutionally entitled participants such as the Advocate General. This is not immunity for every professional or private act.
  • Holding the office alone does not make the Advocate General a minister, Cabinet member or legislator.

Union-State comparison: see the Attorney General versus Advocate General table in section 12.5 for appointment, qualifications, duties, tenure, remuneration and participation rights.

4. Conduct of State Government Business: Article 166

ClauseRequirementMeaning
166(1)All executive action of the State government is expressed to be taken in the Governor's name.The constitutional form does not mean the Governor personally decides every administrative matter.
166(2)Orders and instruments made and executed in the Governor's name are authenticated as prescribed by the Governor's rules.A duly authenticated instrument cannot be challenged merely on the ground that it was not made or executed by the Governor.
166(3)Governor makes rules for the convenient transaction of State government business and its allocation among ministers, except business involving constitutionally required discretion.Provides the framework for departmental allocation, decision-making and matters requiring Cabinet consideration.
Authentication is not universal immunity: Article 166(2) excludes the specified objection about the Governor making/executing the instrument. It does not validate unconstitutional substance, lack of legal authority or every procedural defect. Nor is Article 166 a general licence for the Governor personally to run departmental administration.

Union-State comparison: Article 77 concerns Union business; Article 166 concerns State business. Do not confuse authentication under these Articles with a general requirement that every executive act have a minister's countersignature.

The Governor's formal appointment power must be read with ministerial advice, prescribed consultations and institutional independence. It does not automatically carry an equivalent power of removal.

7. Governor: Nominations and Legislator Disqualification

7.1 Legislative Council Nominations: Article 171

  • In a State having a Legislative Council, Article 171(3)(e) provides for the Governor to nominate the remainder after the elected categories, commonly summarised as one-sixth of the Council's total membership.
  • Article 171(5): nominees must possess special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, co-operative movement or social service.
  • The elected categories in Article 171(3) use "as nearly as may be"; the exact constitutional word for the nominated component is remainder, not a universal fixed number of seats.
  • Nominations operate within the constitutional aid-and-advice framework; this is not an unrestricted personal patronage power.

Different destinations: These are legislative-accountability reports. A Governor's report concerning President's Rule goes to the President under Article 356; the Manipur Hill Areas reporting duty under 371C(2) also goes to the President. Do not merge all reports into one route.

8.2 State Finance Commission: Articles 243I and 243Y

  • The Governor constitutes a State Finance Commission every fifth year under Article 243I, following the initial constitution required after the 73rd Amendment.
  • It reviews the financial position of Panchayats; the same Commission also reviews Municipalities under Article 243Y.
  • It recommends principles for sharing specified State tax revenues, assigning or appropriating taxes to local bodies, and grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • It also recommends measures to improve local bodies' finances and considers other matters referred by the Governor in the interests of their sound finance.
  • The State legislature may determine the Commission's composition, member qualifications and selection method by law. Do not automatically import the Union Finance Commission's composition.
  • Its recommendations are not themselves an appropriation of money. The constitutional accountability requirement includes the action-taken memorandum.

Union-State distinction: Article 280 = Finance Commission constituted by the President for the Union-State fiscal framework; Articles 243I/243Y = State Finance Commission constituted by the Governor for State-local fiscal arrangements.

10. Floor Tests, President's Rule and Important Cases

10.1 Majority and Government Formation

  1. Confidence is tested in the Assembly: letters and competing claims may raise a question, but the Governor should not replace a feasible floor test with a private assessment of who has the majority.
  2. Use relevant material: constitutional action should address a genuine confidence question, not mere policy disagreement with the ministry. The Governor must remain impartial.
  3. Alternative government: if the incumbent loses confidence or resigns, explore whether another ministry can command Assembly confidence before treating the situation as requiring President's Rule.
  4. Minority ministry is not automatically unconstitutional: a party without an absolute majority of its own can form a ministry sustained by coalition partners or outside support.
  5. No automatic dissolution: the fall of one ministry does not necessarily end the Assembly's term. Formation of a successor government and dissolution are separate questions.

10.2 Article 356: What the Governor Can and Cannot Do

  • The Governor may send a report; the President issues the proclamation, acting within the Union aid-and-advice framework. The President can act on the Governor's report or otherwise.
  • The condition is that the State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. Ordinary maladministration or a political disagreement with the Centre is not by itself that condition.
  • A proclamation generally needs approval of both Houses of Parliament within two months, subject to Article 356(3)'s special arrangement when Lok Sabha is dissolved.
  • Under S. R. Bommai, the President should not dissolve the Assembly through the Article 356 mechanism before parliamentary approval of the proclamation; it can meanwhile be kept in suspended animation.
  • The proclamation and its supporting material are subject to judicial review on the recognised grounds. Parliamentary approval does not eliminate that review.
  • If the proclamation is unconstitutional, courts can restore the dismissed ministry and Assembly, while moulding relief to the circumstances. Restoration is a judicial power, not an unavoidable remedy in every historical case.
  • High Court protection: Article 356(1)'s proviso does not permit the President to assume High Court powers or suspend the Constitution's provisions relating to High Courts.
Three separate propositions: Governor reports; President proclaims; Parliament approves and courts may review. A Governor does not personally impose President's Rule, and a change of ruling party at the Centre is not a constitutional ground to remove State ministries.

11. Sarkaria and Punchhi: Reform Recommendations

Recommendations are not constitutional amendments: Distinguish what a commission proposed, what the Constitution currently provides, and what courts have held. In particular, there is no existing Article 61-style impeachment procedure for a Governor.

11.1 Sarkaria Baseline

The Sarkaria Commission, constituted in 1983, reported in 1988. Its selection recommendations already appear in the 18 September note: an eminent, detached person from outside the State, not deeply involved in recent politics, with meaningful CM consultation. These are recommendations, not additional Article 157 eligibility clauses.

For constitutional breakdown, emphasise Article 356 as a last resort, exploration of alternatives, a clear factual Governor's report and, where the situation permits, an opportunity to correct the failure. Relevant recommendations are discussed in S. R. Bommai; a warning is not an exceptionless prerequisite in an extreme emergency.

11.2 Punchhi Commission: Governor-Related Recommendations

The Commission on Centre-State Relations chaired by former CJI M. M. Punchhi was constituted on 27 April 2007 and submitted its report on 30 March 2010. The following are from Volume II; paragraph references below use its concluding summary of recommendations.

11.3 Punchhi's Suggested Invitation Order

When no party or pre-poll coalition has a clear majority, the report suggested the following order, as guidance for constitutional conventions:

  1. A group of parties with a pre-poll alliance commanding the largest number.
  2. The largest single party claiming support from others.
  3. A post-election coalition with all partners joining the government.
  4. A post-election alliance with some parties in government and others, including independents, supporting from outside.
Do not memorise this as an enacted constitutional ranking: It is the Commission's proposed convention, subordinate to the central need for Assembly confidence and applicable constitutional law. A bare claim to be the largest party is not the same as demonstrated majority support.

Historical-source caution: The report predates the 2025 Article 200/201 decisions. Its older discussion of assent options and proposed timelines should not displace the later legal analysis in the 19 September class note.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • 164: Governor appoints CM; other ministers on CM's advice; collective responsibility to Assembly.
  • 15% of Assembly; minimum 12; CM included: State Council size, 91st Amendment.
  • Tribal Welfare Minister: Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha; updated by 94th Amendment.
  • Six consecutive months: a minister's non-membership limit, not a licence to bypass disqualification or repeatedly restart the period.
  • 165: Advocate General, High Court judge qualifications, Governor's pleasure and remuneration determination.
  • 166: Governor's name, authentication, transaction/allocation rules. 167: communicate decisions, furnish information, submit an individual minister's matter to Council when required.
  • 177: ministers and Advocate General can participate; no vote merely by virtue of that Article.
  • State PSC: Governor appoints; President removes; six years / 62 years, whichever is earlier.
  • 243K / 243ZA: State Election Commission for Panchayats/Municipalities, not Assembly elections.
  • 171: nominated Council remainder, commonly one-sixth; fields include co-operative movement.
  • 192: Governor acts according to ECI opinion; Tenth Schedule: Speaker/Chairman route.
  • 151(2), 323(2): CAG and State PSC reports. 243I / 243Y: State Finance Commission, every fifth year, Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • 371A / 371H: specified law-and-order responsibilities; 371C: Manipur Hill Areas; 371F(g): Sikkim peace and equitable advancement; 371(2) / 371J: regional development arrangements.
  • Punchhi: 2007 constitution of Commission; 2010 report; secure tenure and impeachment-style removal are recommendations.

Mains Points

  • Democratic legitimacy and constitutional supervision: Articles 163-164 locate ordinary governance in a confidence-backed Council; the Governor's exceptions require a constitutional basis.
  • Information without parallel government: Articles 166-167 facilitate orderly administration, collective deliberation and communication; they do not authorise unrestricted gubernatorial control of departments.
  • Independent constitutional bodies: separate appointment from removal. The State PSC and Election Commission safeguards reduce the risk of arbitrary executive control.
  • Federal balance in a crisis: use relevant facts, a feasible floor test, alternatives to constitutional breakdown and judicial review. Bommai turns federalism into an enforceable safeguard, not merely a political assurance.
  • Asymmetric federalism: targeted Article 371 provisions protect regional and community interests; carefully distinguish the different mechanisms and conditions.
  • Reform argument: impartial selection, tenure safeguards, transparent conventions and restraint in unrelated statutory roles can improve trust. Identify Punchhi proposals as reform options rather than settled constitutional text.

UPSC CSE Prelims 2014, General Studies Paper I

Which of the following are the discretionary powers given to the Governor of a State?

  1. Sending a report to the President of India for imposing the President's rule
  2. Appointing the Ministers
  3. Reserving certain bills passed by the State Legislature for consideration of the President of India
  4. Making the rules to conduct the business of the State Government

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 1 and 3 only
  3. 2, 3 and 4 only
  4. 1, 2, 3 and 4

Answer: (b) 1 and 3 only. Other ministers are ordinarily appointed on the CM's advice under Article 164(1); Article 166 business rules are not a general personal-discretion power. Distinguish exceptional selection of a CM in an unclear-majority situation from routine ministerial appointments.

Practice Questions: Not PYQs

  1. Can a Council-member CM remain in office after losing the Assembly's confidence?
    Answer: Council membership satisfies the membership question, not the confidence requirement. The Council remains collectively responsible to the Assembly.
  2. Can the Governor remove a State PSC member merely because the Governor appointed the member?
    Answer: No. Removal follows Article 317 through the President; the Governor has the separate, conditional suspension power described above.
  3. A minister takes an administrative decision without Council consideration. What can the Governor require under Article 167(c)?
    Answer: Require the CM to submit that matter to the Council for consideration. This is not a general Governor veto.
  4. Who decides an Article 191(1) disqualification question, and whose opinion governs?
    Answer: The Governor under Article 192, acting according to the ECI's opinion. Defection is a different route.
  5. Does an Advocate General have a constitutional distinguished-jurist qualification route?
    Answer: No. Article 165 imports High Court judge qualifications under Article 217(2), not the Supreme Court eligibility alternatives.
  6. Why is "Punchhi gives Governors a guaranteed five-year term" incorrect?
    Answer: A commission recommendation does not amend Article 156. Secure tenure was a proposal; presidential pleasure remains subject to constitutional judicial limits.
