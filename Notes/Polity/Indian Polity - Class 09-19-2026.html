Topics: Governor's executive, legislative, financial and judicial powers; Articles 200-201 and the 2025 Supreme Court rulings; ordinances and discretion; Part I, Union and its territory.

Labels used: [Correction] explains a substantive correction. [Additional] marks relevant supplementary material. [Verification Required] flags an unresolved claim. [Not mentioned in the Constitution] distinguishes textbook classifications from constitutional wording.

1. Governor: Executive Powers and Functions

1.1 Article 154: Executive Head of the State

Article 154(1) vests the executive power of the State in the Governor, to be exercised directly or through subordinate officers in accordance with the Constitution. This broadly parallels the President's position under Article 53 at the Union level.

Read Article 154 with Article 163: the Governor ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister, except in constitutionally authorised discretionary matters.

[Correction] Executive power being vested in the Governor does not mean that the Governor personally runs the State government or enjoys every power available to the President. The two offices are analogous, not identical.

[Additional] Article 162: State executive power generally extends to matters on which the State legislature can make laws, subject to the Constitution and the proviso concerning Union executive power in matters of shared legislative competence.

1.2 Governor as Chancellor of State Universities

The Governor serves as Chancellor of many State universities where the applicable university legislation provides for that office and its powers.

[Correction] This is a statutory role, not a uniform power conferred by Article 154. Do not assume that every State university must have the Governor as Chancellor or that the arrangements cannot be changed by law.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] Governor as university Chancellor is not a constitutional designation. The relevant university Act, including valid amendments, determines the position.

Article 168: the State legislature consists of the Governor and one House or two Houses, as applicable. The Governor is therefore an integral part of the legislature, without being a member of its House or Houses.

[Correction] The rough "High Court and PSC respectively" formula omits the High Court from Article 234. Also distinguish consultation under Article 233(1) from the express recommendation requirement under Article 233(2).

4.2 Article 161: Scope of the Mercy Power

The Governor may grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or suspend, remit or commute a sentence, for a person convicted of an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the State's executive power extends.

[Correction] "Violation of State law" is too narrow if it means only legislation enacted by the State legislature. The constitutional test is State executive competence over the subject, not simply which legislature enacted the law.

[Additional] Meanings for revision:

TermBasic meaning
PardonForgiveness of the offence and relief from its punishment; distinguish it from merely reducing a sentence.
CommutationSubstitute a lighter kind of punishment, for example death with imprisonment for life.
RemissionReduce the amount or duration of punishment without changing its character.
RespiteA lesser punishment because of special circumstances, such as a serious physical condition or pregnancy.
ReprieveA temporary postponement of execution of a sentence, especially to allow consideration of further relief.

[Additional] These clemency terms are expressly used in Articles 72 and 161; they are not merely coaching classifications. Suspension temporarily holds the operation of a sentence in abeyance.

4.3 Death Sentences, Advice and Limits

  • President: Article 72(1)(c) expressly extends the President's clemency power to all cases where the sentence is a sentence of death.
  • Governor: Article 72(3) expressly preserves the Governor's power under applicable law to suspend, remit or commute a death sentence. Do not conclude that the Governor has no role in death-sentence mercy matters.
  • [Additional] Court martial: Article 72(1)(a) expressly covers court-martial punishment for the President. Article 161 does not give the Governor a corresponding military-clemency jurisdiction.
  • [Additional] Ministerial advice: Article 161 clemency is exercised on the State Council of Ministers' advice, not the Governor's personal choice. Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980), also applied in Nalini v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (1999), explains this distinction.
[Verification Required] The rough statement "the Governor cannot pardon a death sentence" is a familiar exam-text formulation, but a categorical exclusion is not expressly written in Article 161 and was not independently established by the primary sources checked for this note. Retain it as a claim requiring an authoritative case-law reference, not as a verified quotation from the Constitution. The verified distinctions are the President's all-death-cases jurisdiction, the Governor's State-executive subject limit, and the preserved power to suspend, remit or commute.

[Additional] Clemency is an executive constitutional function, not a fresh judicial appeal. It is subject to limited judicial scrutiny for defects such as arbitrariness or mala fides; the 2025 rulings on Bill assent should not be applied indiscriminately to mercy petitions.

5. Article 200: Bills Presented to the Governor

2025 Exam Alert: Read Article 200 with the 20 November 2025 five-judge opinion. The April deadlines and its general insistence on assent to a repassed Bill must not be presented as the later position. The historical comparison is in section 7.

5.1 Three Constitutional Options, Not Four Independent Choices

OptionMeaningLimit
AssentThe Governor grants assent to the Bill.[Additional] Assent and the date on which all provisions commence are not necessarily the same; the Act's commencement provisions also matter.
Reserve for the PresidentThe Bill enters the Article 201 process.Reservation is not itself assent or rejection.
Withhold assent and return with commentsReturn the Bill to the legislature for reconsideration under the first proviso.Available only for a non-Money Bill; return must be as soon as possible.
[Correction] Replace "without assent" with "withhold assent". The rough four-item list incorrectly separates withholding from return as if both were independent options. The November opinion, paragraph 165.1, treats them as a linked third option for non-Money Bills. It does not permit simply withholding a Bill without returning it.

Money Bill distinction: under this interpretation, the Governor can assent or reserve; the withholding-and-return option is unavailable. A Money Bill is not automatically exempt from reservation.

5.2 Mandatory Reservation: High Court Safeguard

Under the second proviso to Article 200, the Governor shall not assent and shall reserve a Bill which, in the Governor's opinion, would so derogate from the High Court's powers as to endanger the position the Constitution designs that Court to fill.

[Correction] This is not a rule requiring reservation of every Bill concerning a High Court. The constitutional threshold is the specified threat to its powers and position.

5.3 A Bill Passed Again after Reconsideration

The first proviso says that, after reconsideration and repassage with or without amendment, the Governor "shall not withhold assent therefrom".

8. Article 213: Ordinance-Making Power

8.1 Conditions, Effect and Duration

The Governor's ordinance power broadly corresponds to the President's power under Article 123, but includes the specific presidential-instruction requirements below.

  • [Additional] Legislative recess: in a unicameral State, the Assembly must not be in session. In a bicameral State, an ordinance cannot be promulgated when both Houses are in session; one House being out of session is sufficient for this particular condition.
  • [Additional] Immediate necessity: circumstances must require immediate action. The Governor acts on ministerial advice; this is not ordinary personal discretion.
  • [Additional] Legal effect: a valid ordinance has the same force and effect as a State Act assented to by the Governor, within the State's legislative competence and other constitutional limits.
  • [Additional] Legislative control: it must be laid before the Assembly, or both Houses where applicable. It ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly, unless it ceases earlier through the prescribed disapproval process or withdrawal.
  • [Additional] Different reassembly dates: count the six weeks from the later date. In a bicameral State, Article 213(2)(a) requires Assembly disapproval agreed to by the Council.

8.2 Three Cases Requiring Instructions from the President

Article 213(1) provisoTest using a Bill / Act with the same provisionsIllustration
(a) Previous sanction for introductionWould such a Bill require the President's previous sanction before introduction?Article 304(b): reasonable restrictions in the public interest on trade, commerce or intercourse with or within the State.
(b) Reservation for considerationWould the Governor have considered it necessary to reserve such a Bill for the President?A Bill meeting the High Court safeguard in Article 200's second proviso.
(c) Presidential assent needed for validityWould such a State Act be invalid without reservation and presidential assent?[Additional] Article 254(2): a State Concurrent List provision requiring presidential assent to prevail in that State over a repugnant parliamentary/existing law.
[Correction] The rough notes supply only two categories and blur reservation with assent. There are three in Article 213(1). Its precise term is "instructions from the President", not merely a general permission to legislate.
[Correction] The trade example is Article 304(b)'s specified restrictions, not every State measure touching inter-State trade. The High Court example belongs to the reservation requirement; presidential instructions cannot make an otherwise unconstitutional provision valid.

[Additional] Article 213(3) makes invalid provisions void. Its proviso treats an ordinance promulgated on presidential instructions as reserved and assented to for the specified Concurrent List repugnancy purpose; it is not a cure for every constitutional defect.

8.3 Re-Promulgation Is Not a Substitute for Legislation

[Additional] D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar, decided 20 December 1986 and reported in (1987) 1 SCC 378, condemned repeated re-promulgation to bypass the legislature. In Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar, 2 January 2017, the seven-judge Bench's majority reaffirmed that principle, treated laying before the legislature as mandatory, and recognised review of the satisfaction underlying an ordinance for constitutional abuse.

[Additional] An ordinance's provisions are continued as ordinary legislation by enacting a law, not merely by passing a resolution of approval. Do not confuse the six-week ordinance rule with Article 201's six-month reconsideration rule.

9. Constitutional Position and Discretion of the Governor

9.1 Article 163: Rule and Exception

ClausePosition
163(1)A Council of Ministers headed by the CM aids and advises the Governor, except where the Constitution requires the Governor to exercise a function in discretion.
163(2)Addresses a question whether a matter falls within constitutionally required discretion, and gives finality in the terms stated there.
[Additional] 163(3)Courts may not inquire into whether, and what, ministerial advice was tendered.
[Correction] Article 163(2) does not authorise the Governor to invent discretionary powers at will. Discretion must have a constitutional basis, including recognised constitutional interpretation. The finality language is not a universal exclusion of judicial scrutiny of constitutional limits.

[Additional] In Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016), discussed in the November 2025 opinion, the Court rejected treating Article 163 as a general reservoir of arbitrary discretion. Read the specific 2025 Article 200 ruling alongside the general aid-and-advice framework, not as its wholesale replacement.

9.3 Situational Discretion

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] "Constitutional discretion", "situational discretion" and "hung Assembly" are textbook classifications/descriptions, not constitutional headings. The word "discretion" itself is expressly present in Article 163 and other provisions.

[Additional] Common examples, subject to constitutional limits:

  • Choosing a CM when no party/alliance has a clear majority, with the object of identifying a government able to command Assembly confidence.
  • Addressing loss of majority, ordinarily through a floor test, and possible dismissal where a ministry has lost confidence but refuses to resign.
  • Considering dissolution when a ministry has lost its majority and whether an alternative government can be formed.
[Correction] These situations are analogous to some presidential situations, but the constitutional provisions differ. They do not permit routine personal selection of all ministers: under Article 164(1), other ministers are appointed on the CM's advice. The November Article 200 discretion ruling does not make Article 161 clemency or Article 213 ordinances ordinary discretionary powers.

10. Part I: Union and Its Territory

10.1 Articles 1-4 and the First Schedule

Article 1(1): "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."
ProvisionMeaning
Article 1(1)Constitutional names India and Bharat; description Union of States.
Article 1(2)The States and their territories are specified in the First Schedule.
Article 1(3)The territory of India comprises the territories of the States, Union territories specified in the First Schedule, and such other territories as may be acquired.
[Correction] Article 1 does not literally say "India is federal". It says "Union of States". India's federal character is understood from the constitutional scheme, including the distribution of powers, not from substituting "federal" into Article 1.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] "Federal State" and "quasi-federal" are analytical descriptions of India's system, not Article 1's chosen description. This does not deny federalism's constitutional significance.

10.2 Union of India versus Territory of India

Expression in the textbook comparisonScope
Union of India, in the narrow federal-units senseThe constituent States forming the federal Union.
Territory of India, Article 1(3)States + Union territories + other acquired territories; the wider territorial expression.
[Correction] This comparison does not mean that UTs are outside India, or that "Union of India" must mean only a list of States in every legal context. [Additional] In litigation, the expression also identifies the Union government. Article 1(3)'s words are "such other territories as may be acquired", not a statement that only States can form Indian territory.

10.3 Why "Union of States"? Ambedkar's Explanation

In the Constituent Assembly on [Additional] 4 November 1948, Dr. B. R. Ambedkar explained the deliberate use of Union:

  1. The Indian federation was not the result of an agreement among States to join a federation.
  2. No State has a right to secede; the Union is indestructible.

He also explained that, although the country and people could be divided into States for administrative convenience, they formed one integral whole.

[Correction] The rough "Feder means agreement" line is an etymological/textbook shorthand, not a constitutional rule that every federation must originate through a compact. For India, the important proposition is no compact conferring a right of secession, not a word-origin argument.
[Correction] "States exist only for administrative convenience" overstates the point. In the same speech, Ambedkar said States were not merely administrative units or agencies of the Union. They possess constitutionally assigned powers. Administrative reorganisation and genuine federal autonomy can coexist.

10.4 "Indestructible Union of Destructible States"

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] "Indestructible Union of destructible States" is a textbook description of India. The comparison "indestructible Union of indestructible States" describes the United States; neither is the wording of India's Article 1.
PointIndiaUnited States comparison
UnionNo constitutional right of a State to secede.Ambedkar referred to the American Civil War as settling the indestructibility of that Union.
Territorial identity of StatesArticle 3 empowers Parliament to reorganise States and alter their areas, boundaries or names, following the prescribed process.[Additional] Article IV, Section 3 requires the consent of the concerned State legislatures and Congress for the specified formation of States within, or by joining, existing States.
[Correction] "Destructible" concerns the ability to reorganise a State's territorial identity, not permission to destroy federalism or abolish all constitutional limits. Conversely, American States are not literally incapable of any boundary/reorganisation change: consent safeguards distinguish the arrangements.

10.5 Single Citizenship

India has one Indian citizenship, not a separate constitutional citizenship of each State. Residence in a State does not make a person a separate citizen of that State.

[Additional] Citizenship provisions are in Part II, Articles 5-11, not Part I. Residence-based rules for particular purposes should not be confused with a second State citizenship.

[Not mentioned in the Constitution] "Single citizenship" is the explanatory label for this arrangement; the constitutional provisions speak of citizenship of India.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • 154: State executive power; 163: aid and advice with constitutionally recognised discretion; 168: Governor is part of the State legislature.
  • Chancellor: statutory university role, not a universal constitutional office.
  • Fifth Schedule paragraph 5: application of laws and regulations in Scheduled Areas; regulations need presidential assent.
  • Sixth Schedule: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram; paragraph 9(2) concerns a dispute about a District Council's royalty share.
  • 202: annual financial statement; 203(3): grants on recommendation; 207: financial Bills; 267(2): State Contingency Fund.
  • 233: district judges, Governor + High Court consultation; 234: other judicial service, rules after consultation with PSC and High Court; 235: High Court control.
  • 161: clemency linked to State executive competence. 72(1)(c): President's all-death-cases power; 72(3): preserves gubernatorial suspension, remission and commutation.
  • 200: three options after the November opinion; no return of a Money Bill. A repassed non-Money Bill may be assented to or reserved.
  • 201: six months for legislative reconsideration, not a presidential assent deadline. Repassage does not compel presidential assent.
  • 2025: 8 April = two-judge Tamil Nadu judgment; 20 November = five-judge advisory opinion. No general court-fixed assent deadlines or deemed assent; limited remedy for prolonged gubernatorial inaction remains.
  • 213: three presidential-instruction cases; ordinance ceases six weeks after reassembly, reckoned from the later House date in a bicameral State.
  • Part I, 1-4: Union and territory; First Schedule: States/UTs; Article 3: views, not consent; Part II, 5-11: citizenship.

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical applications:

  • Responsible government: the Governor is the constitutional head, while the elected ministry normally governs through legislative confidence. Exceptions must be tied to the Constitution, not personal political preference.
  • Federalism and assent: Bill consideration must reconcile constitutional safeguards with the elected legislature's ability to legislate. Reservation is a constitutional mechanism, not a routine device for obstruction.
  • 2025 balance: distinguish judicial substitution of an assent decision from judicial insistence that a constitutional office act. Discuss both separation of powers and remedies against paralysis.
  • Ordinance accountability: immediate necessity, legislative laying and the six-week limit safeguard legislative scrutiny. Re-promulgation used to evade scrutiny undermines representative government.
  • Tribal autonomy: the Fifth and Sixth Schedules use different mechanisms for protection and self-government. Precise safeguards matter more than treating the Governor's role as unlimited control.
  • Union and diversity: a non-secessionist Union and flexible State boundaries coexist with constitutionally protected State powers. Single citizenship promotes common political membership without eliminating all residence-based legal distinctions.

PYQ 1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014, General Studies Paper I

Which of the following are the discretionary powers given to the Governor of a State?

  1. Sending a report to the President of India for imposing the President's rule
  2. Appointing the Ministers
  3. Reserving certain bills passed by the State Legislature for consideration of the President of India
  4. Making the rules to conduct the business of the State Government

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 1 and 3 only
  3. 2, 3 and 4 only
  4. 1, 2, 3 and 4

Correct answer: (b) 1 and 3 only. Reporting under 356 and reservation under 200 are the relevant discretionary functions. Routine appointment of other ministers follows the CM's advice under 164(1); Article 166 business rules are not a general personal-discretion exception. Do not confuse exceptional selection of a CM in an unclear-majority situation with statement 2.

Question source: Rau's IAS: 2014 Governor-discretion PYQ.

PYQ 2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2015, General Studies Paper I

The provisions in Fifth Schedule and Sixth Schedule in the Constitution of India are made in order to

  1. protect the interests of Scheduled Tribes
  2. determine the boundaries between States
  3. determine the powers, authority and responsibilities of Panchayats
  4. protect the interests of all the border States

Correct answer: (a) protect the interests of Scheduled Tribes. Read Article 244 with the two Schedules. State boundaries are a separate Article 3 subject; the Sixth Schedule is not a general border-State arrangement.

Question source: Rau's IAS: 2015 Fifth and Sixth Schedules PYQ.

PYQ 3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017, General Studies Paper I

Which one of the following is not a feature of Indian federalism?

  1. There is an independent judiciary in India.
  2. Powers have been clearly divided between the Centre and the States.
  3. The federating units have been given unequal representation in the Rajya Sabha.
  4. It is the result of an agreement among the federating units.

Correct answer: (d) It is the result of an agreement among the federating units. This directly tests Ambedkar's explanation of "Union of States". [Additional] Federalism does not require equal State representation in India's Rajya Sabha; the Fourth Schedule allocates seats.

Question source: Rau's IAS: 2017 Indian federalism PYQ.

PYQ 4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2022, General Studies Paper I

If a particular area is brought under the Fifth Schedule of the Constitution of India, which one of the following statements best reflects the consequence of it?

  1. This would prevent the transfer of land of tribal people to non-tribal people.
  2. This would create a local self-governing body in that area.
  3. This would convert that area into a Union Territory.
  4. The State having such areas would be declared a Special Category State.

Correct answer: (a). Paragraph 5(2)(a) permits regulations prohibiting or restricting tribal land transfers. The answer identifies the protective purpose; it should not be stretched into a claim that every transfer is automatically void without considering the applicable law or regulation.

Question source: Rau's IAS: 2022 Fifth Schedule land-protection PYQ.

PYQ 5. UPSC CSE Mains 2022, General Studies Paper II

Discuss the essential conditions for exercise of the legislative powers by the Governor. Discuss the legality of re-promulgation of ordinances by the Governor without placing them before the Legislature.

Suggested answer outline: Begin with Article 213: the required legislative recess, circumstances necessitating immediate action, ministerial advice, legislative competence and presidential instructions in the three specified situations. A valid ordinance has the force of an Act but must be laid before the legislature and ceases six weeks after reassembly, or earlier through the prescribed disapproval/withdrawal process. Use D. C. Wadhwa and the majority in Krishna Kumar Singh to explain why repeated re-promulgation to avoid the legislature is a constitutional abuse. The latter treats laying as mandatory and recognises limited judicial review of the underlying satisfaction. Conclude that emergency law-making is a temporary constitutional mechanism, not an alternative to representative legislation.

Question source: Rau's IAS: 2022 GS II questions; full Governor-and-ordinances question.

PYQ 6. UPSC CSE Mains 2014, General Studies Paper II

Instances of President's delay in commuting death sentences has come under public debate as denial of justice. Should there be a time limit specified for the President to accept/reject such petitions? Analyse.

Suggested answer outline: Identify the President's clemency jurisdiction under Article 72, especially clause (1)(c), and distinguish it from the Governor's Article 161 jurisdiction. Explain the case for prompt consideration: human dignity, fairness, accountability and avoidance of prolonged uncertainty, linked to Article 21. Balance these concerns against careful examination of each mercy petition and access to relevant records. Consider transparent processing procedures and administrative targets without inventing a constitutional deadline or automatic grant of mercy. Distinguish this question from the 2025 Bill-assent litigation under Articles 200-201; the two constitutional functions must not be conflated.

Question source: Rau's IAS: 2014 mercy-petition delay PYQ.
