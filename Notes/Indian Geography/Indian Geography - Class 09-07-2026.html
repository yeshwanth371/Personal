1. Eastern Ghats: Character and Physiography

The Eastern Ghats are an old, discontinuous and deeply denuded series of hill masses running broadly north-east to south-west along eastern Peninsular India. They are lower and less continuous than the Western Ghats because major east-flowing rivers cut across them and because long erosion has reduced and separated their relief.

[Correction] The range was not made discontinuous by river erosion alone. The Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri have cut broad valleys through an already ancient, structurally complex and heavily denuded mountain belt. Tectonic history, differential erosion and river incision act together.

1.2 Physical Geography, Drainage and Importance

DimensionGeographical detailWhy it matters
Geology and reliefAn ancient mobile belt of charnockite, khondalite, granite-gneiss, quartzite and metamorphic rocks, with locally different geological histories. Long denudation produced rounded hills, residual massifs, plateaus and structural valleys rather than one continuous escarpment.Explains mineral occurrences, thin stony soils, varied landforms and the fragmented distribution of forests and settlements.
DrainageThe Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri are antecedent or structurally guided systems that cross the hills through gaps and gorges. Smaller east-flowing rivers such as the Vamsadhara, Nagavali, Pennar and Palar drain separate sectors.River gaps connect interior plateaus with coastal plains, carry sediment to deltas and provide sites for reservoirs, irrigation and transport corridors.
ClimateRainfall is lower and more variable than on the windward Western Ghats. Northern sectors receive mainly south-west monsoon rain; south-eastern Tamil Nadu sectors gain a larger share from the north-east monsoon.Seasonal water stress, rain-shadow conditions and drought shape tank irrigation, dry farming, forest type and rural livelihoods.
Soils and vegetationRed and yellow soils, lateritic patches and thin upland soils support dry deciduous to moist deciduous forests; higher or wetter pockets retain semi-evergreen vegetation.The belt is a refuge for red sanders and other endemic plants and provides elephant, tiger and gaur habitats and links between central and southern Indian landscapes.
ResourcesBauxite occurs on some plateau caps; iron ore, manganese, limestone, graphite and decorative/building stone occur regionally.Minerals support industry and employment but open-cast mining can fragment forests, alter drainage and displace Adivasi communities.
Human geographyMany upland districts have dispersed settlements, significant Scheduled Tribe populations, podu/shifting cultivation traditions and limited transport access.Development requires forest rights, watershed restoration, locally suitable roads and health/education access rather than uniform lowland models.
[Additional] National importance: the Eastern Ghats form an ecological and hydrological bridge between central India and the southern Western Ghats. Protecting corridors around Similipal-southern Odisha, northern Andhra, Nallamala-Seshachalam and B.R. Hills is important for genetic movement of large mammals and climate adaptation.
Cause-effect chain: old fractured rocks + seasonal monsoon + vegetation loss → rapid runoff and soil erosion → reduced infiltration and summer streamflow → tank siltation and drought stress. Watershed treatment, native forest recovery and regulated mining address the process rather than only water scarcity symptoms.

2. Shillong or Meghalaya Plateau

The Shillong Plateau is a detached north-eastern outlier of the Peninsular block, composed mainly of ancient crystalline rocks. It is commonly linked physiographically to the Chota Nagpur-Rajmahal system but is separated from it by the low, alluvium-covered Garo-Rajmahal or Malda Gap.

Ancient Peninsular basement→faulting and relative subsidence→low Garo-Rajmahal / Malda Gap→thick Ganga-Brahmaputra alluvium→Shillong Plateau appears detached
[Correction] The gap was not formed simply by “subsidence due to heat”. It reflects faulting, crustal movements and basin development and was subsequently buried by thick fluvial sediments. “Malda Gap” and “Garo-Rajmahal Gap” are the standard expressions.
UnitLocation and importance
Garo HillsWestern Meghalaya. Nokrek, about 1,412 m, is the highest Garo peak and lies within the UNESCO-recognised Nokrek Biosphere Reserve (MAB nomination: 2009).
Khasi HillsCentral Meghalaya. Shillong Peak (about 1,961 m) is the plateau high point. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji/Sohra lie on the rain-facing southern Khasi escarpment.
Jaintia HillsEastern Meghalaya; continuation of the high-rainfall limestone-rich plateau.
Karbi Anglong / Mikir HillsIn Assam, east of the Meghalaya Plateau; an old Peninsular outlier separated by faulted valleys. “Mikir Hills” is the older geographical name.
[Correction] Mawsynram and Cherrapunji are in the Khasi Hills, not the Garo Hills. Mawsynram is generally cited as the world's wettest inhabited place by long-period average rainfall, while individual-year records can vary. Always attach the averaging period when comparing rainfall records.
[Additional] Extreme rainfall results from moisture-laden Bay of Bengal winds being funnelled toward and forced rapidly up the steep southern escarpment overlooking Bangladesh.

2.1 Physiographic Controls and Regional Significance

AspectDetail and importance
Tectonic settingThe plateau is bounded by major fault systems, including the Brahmaputra valley margin to the north and the Dauki Fault along the steep southern edge. Continued intraplate deformation makes the region seismically active.
ReliefA rolling plateau surface rises sharply above the Bangladesh plain. The abrupt southern scarp intensifies orographic uplift, while deeply incised valleys and waterfalls reflect river dissection.
KarstThick limestone, especially in Khasi-Jaintia sectors, supports caves, sink features and underground drainage. It also supplies cement and lime industries, creating a conservation-versus-extraction issue.
Drainage and powerShort, high-gradient streams descend toward Bangladesh; north-flowing systems drain toward the Brahmaputra. The Umiam-Barapani system supports water supply and hydropower.
Soils and land useHeavy rainfall causes leaching, laterisation and severe erosion where forest cover is removed. Jhum, horticulture, broom grass, betel leaf, arecanut and terrace cultivation vary by slope and community.
Biocultural valueSacred groves, community forests, living-root bridges and matrilineal Khasi, Jaintia and Garo social traditions show how cultural institutions can conserve biodiversity and manage difficult terrain.
[Additional] Strategic importance: the plateau overlooks the narrow land and river corridors linking the Brahmaputra valley, Barak valley and Bangladesh. Roads, rail links and border trade must account for landslides, extreme rainfall, earthquakes and fragile karst aquifers.
Rainfall paradox: exceptionally high annual rain does not guarantee year-round household water security. Rapid runoff, steep slopes, thin soils, limited storage, spring degradation and seasonal distribution can produce dry-season shortages even around Cherrapunji.

3. India's Coastline: Old and Revised Measurement

India has a long mainland-and-island coastline facing the Arabian Sea, Indian Ocean and Bay of Bengal. Coastline length is scale-dependent: a finer measuring scale follows more bays, creeks, estuaries and island edges, producing a longer total. This is the coastline paradox, not a physical expansion of national territory.

DatasetLengthHow to use it
Long-used textbook figure7,516.6 km including islands; mainland about 6,100 km.Use only when a question explicitly follows the older NCERT/government dataset.
Government revision announced in 2024-2511,098.81 kmUse as the revised official figure, with reference year/method. It reflects higher-resolution GIS/cartographic measurement and fuller accounting of coastal features.
[Correction] The older figure is 7,516.6 km, not 7,517 km as an exact value. The revised length does not mean the coast grew by about 3,582 km; the measurement standard and detail changed.
[Additional] Under the revised dataset, Andaman and Nicobar Islands have the longest coastline among states and Union Territories, followed by Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. Gujarat remains the mainland state with the longest coastline. Quote exact state totals only with the revised government table because old and new figures should not be mixed.

3.1 “Correct The Map” and Equal-Area World Maps

In 2025, the African Union (AU), a 55-member intergovernmental body, backed the “Correct The Map” campaign promoting the Equal Earth projection in place of routine Mercator use. The campaign argues that Mercator greatly enlarges high-latitude regions and visually understates Africa and other tropical regions.

QuestionBalanced analysis
What was approved?An AU political endorsement of wider use of an equal-area projection. It was not a binding UN resolution creating one compulsory “new world map”.
Why Equal Earth?It preserves relative area while presenting a visually familiar world outline, improving comparison of country and continent size.
Why not Mercator everywhere?Mercator preserves local angles and is useful for navigation, but area distortion rises sharply toward the poles; Greenland and Europe appear disproportionately large relative to Africa.
Who backed it?The AU endorsed the campaign across its 55-member institutional bloc. Treat claims that every member country separately legislated adoption as [Verification Required].
What issues remain?No flat map can preserve area, shape, distance and direction simultaneously. Equal Earth distorts some shapes and directions, so projection choice must match purpose. Existing atlases, software and teaching materials also create transition costs.

3.2 Geographic Importance of India's Coastal Position

DimensionImportance
LocationThe central position at the head of the Indian Ocean places India between the energy-producing Persian Gulf, eastern Africa and the manufacturing/trade centres of South-East and East Asia.
TradePorts connect the large hinterland to global container, energy and bulk-cargo routes. Coastal economic clusters support refining, petrochemicals, steel, automobiles, shipbuilding, fisheries and services.
SecurityThe coast requires surveillance of sea lanes, offshore installations, ports and fishing zones and supports naval reach, humanitarian assistance and disaster relief across the Indian Ocean Region.
Exclusive Economic ZoneMainland and island territories give India access to a large maritime zone with living resources, seabed minerals, renewable-energy potential and marine scientific value, subject to UNCLOS rights and duties.
Monsoon and climateSurrounding seas supply monsoon moisture and moderate coastal temperatures, but also expose settlements to cyclones, storm surges, marine heatwaves and sea-level rise.
Civilisational linksMonsoon navigation connected Indian ports with Arabia, eastern Africa and South-East Asia, enabling long-term exchange of goods, religions, languages, technologies and communities.
Exam distinction: coastline length describes the land-water boundary; territorial sea, contiguous zone, Exclusive Economic Zone and continental shelf are different legal-maritime concepts. A longer measured coastline does not automatically change maritime boundaries.

5. Andaman and Nicobar Islands

The Andaman-Nicobar chain is the emergent crest of an arcuate submarine ridge within the Sunda-Andaman island-arc system. It continues southward from the Rakhine/Arakan Yoma region toward Indonesia and reflects subduction of the oceanic Indian Plate beneath the Burma microplate, part of the broader Sunda plate system.

[Correction] Use Burma microplate, not “Burmese plate” as a full major plate. The oceanic Indian Plate is subducting beneath the Burma/Sunda overriding plate; it is not a collision between two equal oceanic plates.

5.1 Earthquakes and the 2004 Tsunami

Locked subduction interface→stress accumulates→megathrust rupture→seafloor displaced vertically→tsunami radiates across Indian Ocean
[Correction] The 26 December 2004 Sumatra-Andaman earthquake had moment magnitude Mw 9.1-9.3, not “9.3 on the Richter scale”. Modern great earthquakes are reported on the moment magnitude scale. The tsunami caused more than 220,000 deaths around the Indian Ocean; exact totals vary by source.

5.3 Strategic, Ecological and Cultural Significance

  • Sea-lane position: overlooks western approaches to the Malacca Strait, a major maritime trade and energy chokepoint connecting the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
  • Security: supports maritime-domain awareness, search and rescue, anti-piracy work and India's Indo-Pacific presence through the Andaman and Nicobar Command.
  • Blue economy: fisheries, marine research, carefully regulated tourism and potential logistics services.
  • Biodiversity: tropical forests, mangroves, coral reefs, seagrass, dugongs, marine turtles, endemic birds and the UNESCO-recognised Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve (MAB, 2013).
  • Indigenous communities: Great Andamanese, Jarawa, Onge and Sentinelese in the Andamans; Nicobarese and Shompen in the Nicobars.
[Correction] Avoid “primitive tribes” and racial labels such as “Negrito” in current answer writing. Use Indigenous communities or, where legally applicable, Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs). These communities are contemporary rights-holders, not evolutionary relics.

Strategic geography: the islands do not sit inside the Malacca Strait; they overlook its western approaches. Their value comes from position, surveillance and logistics, but geography also imposes high costs through distance, seismicity, ecological sensitivity and limited land and water.

6. Great Nicobar Development Project: Significance and Issues

The proposed integrated development at Great Nicobar includes an international container transshipment terminal at Galathea Bay, a greenfield international airport, a power plant and a township. Its policy case is based on strategic location, transshipment capacity, connectivity, employment and a stronger Indian presence near the Malacca approaches.

Potential gainsMajor concerns
Capture cargo now transshipped through foreign hubs; improve naval and coast-guard logistics; create disaster-response capacity; improve connectivity and services; support India's Indo-Pacific strategy.Large forest diversion and habitat fragmentation; leatherback turtle nesting around Galathea Bay; mangrove/coral and sediment impacts; seismic, tsunami and cyclone exposure; freshwater and waste limits; induced population pressure; Shompen and Nicobarese rights, health and cultural survival.
[Correction] The proposal is not simply “energy, airport and seaport”. Its four linked components include the transshipment port, airport, power system and township. Galathea Bay is near the south-eastern part of Great Nicobar; Indira Point is the island's southern tip.
[Additional] Great Nicobar covers about 1,044 sq km and its biosphere reserve was added to UNESCO's World Network in 2013. The Shompen move through forest and riverine landscapes, so safeguards must assess access routes and cumulative disturbance, not only village footprints.

6.1 Required Safeguards and Policy Approach

  1. Avoid-minimise-restore-offset: first redesign to avoid old-growth forest, turtle nesting beaches, mangroves, coral areas and critical wildlife corridors; offsets cannot substitute for irreplaceable habitat.
  2. Rights before construction: complete lawful recognition of forest and habitat rights, independent health/cultural impact assessment and meaningful participation under the Forest Rights Act and applicable Andaman and Nicobar tribal-protection rules.
  3. Cumulative assessment: assess port, airport, township, roads, dredging, shipping, in-migration and waste together rather than as isolated projects.
  4. Disaster-resilient design: use multi-hazard microzonation, tsunami evacuation routes and vertical shelters, cyclone standards, redundant energy/water/communications, early warning and protected natural buffers.
  5. Carrying capacity: phase population and construction to freshwater, sewage, solid-waste and health-service limits; apply independent stop/go review points.
  6. Transparent monitoring: publish baseline and periodic data on turtles, coral, mangroves, forest loss, sediment, water and community health, with independent scientific and tribal representation.
Sendai Framework link: apply all four priorities: understand multi-hazard risk; strengthen risk governance; invest in resilience; and prepare to respond and “Build Back Better”. For Great Nicobar, ecological buffers and Indigenous knowledge are risk-reduction infrastructure, not obstacles external to development.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Eastern Ghats are old, discontinuous and crossed by Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri systems.
  • Jindhagada / Arma Konda is generally cited as the Eastern Ghats high point; exact official name/elevation needs source qualification.
  • B.R. Hills are in Karnataka; Seshachalam is in Andhra Pradesh and is associated with red sanders.
  • Shillong Plateau is a detached Peninsular outlier separated by the Garo-Rajmahal/Malda Gap.
  • Nokrek is in Garo Hills; Mawsynram and Cherrapunji are in Khasi Hills; Karbi Anglong/Mikir Hills are in Assam.
  • Old coastline figure: 7,516.6 km including islands. Revised official figure: 11,098.81 km (2024-25 revision).
  • Longest revised coastline: Andaman and Nicobar among all units; Gujarat among mainland states; Tamil Nadu follows Gujarat in the all-unit ranking cited in the revision.
  • Equal Earth preserves relative area; Mercator preserves local angles, making it valuable for navigation but poor for area comparison.
  • Western coast: generally narrow and estuarine. Eastern coast: generally broad and deltaic. Both have important ports and fisheries.
  • Ten Degree Channel separates Andaman and Nicobar groups; Great Channel separates Great Nicobar from Sumatra approaches.
  • Barren Island is India's only confirmed active volcano; the island arc is related to Indian Plate subduction beneath the Burma microplate.
  • 2004 Sumatra-Andaman earthquake: Mw 9.1-9.3; seafloor displacement generated the Indian Ocean tsunami.
  • Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve joined UNESCO's World Network in 2013; Galathea Bay is ecologically and strategically important.

Mains Points

  • Relief controls economy: narrow west-coast plains favour estuaries, harbours and fisheries; broad eastern deltas favour intensive agriculture but face cyclone and sediment risks.
  • Measurement is political and technical: the revised coastline reflects GIS scale, while Equal Earth debates show how projection choices influence perceived geopolitical importance.
  • Island security is ecological security: damaged coral, mangroves and forests weaken storm, erosion and tsunami resilience.
  • Great Nicobar is a development dilemma: strategic logistics must be tested against irreversible biodiversity loss, extreme hazard exposure and Indigenous rights.
  • Resilience requires systems thinking: port, airport, settlement, water, waste and evacuation systems must be assessed cumulatively under Sendai principles.

30-Second Revision

The Eastern Ghats are old, discontinuous hills cut by major east-flowing rivers; remember Mahendragiri, Jindhagada/Arma Konda, Nallamala-Seshachalam, Javadi-Shevaroy and Karnataka's B.R. Hills. The detached Shillong Plateau lies beyond the alluvium-filled Malda Gap: Nokrek is Garo, while Mawsynram-Cherrapunji are Khasi. India's coastline was remeasured from 7,516.6 km to 11,098.81 km using finer methods. The narrow west coast is mainly estuarine; the wider east coast is mainly deltaic. Equal Earth corrects Mercator's area bias but is not distortion-free. The Andaman-Nicobar arc forms above subduction of the Indian Plate beneath the Burma microplate; Ten Degree Channel separates the groups and Barren Island is active. Great Nicobar's port-airport-power-township proposal offers strategic value but requires cumulative ecological assessment, tribal-rights protection and Sendai-based multi-hazard design.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

UPSC CSE Prelims 2015 - General Studies Paper I

Which one of the following pairs of islands is separated from each other by the “Ten Degree Channel”?

  1. Andaman and Nicobar
  2. Nicobar and Sumatra
  3. Maldives and Lakshadweep
  4. Sumatra and Java

Correct answer: (a) Andaman and Nicobar. The channel near 10 degrees N separates Little Andaman in the north from Car Nicobar in the south and conventionally divides the two island groups.

UPSC CSE Prelims 2014 - General Studies Paper I

Which of the following have coral reefs?

  1. Andaman and Nicobar Islands
  2. Gulf of Kachchh
  3. Gulf of Mannar
  4. Sunderbans

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1, 2 and 3 only
  2. 2 and 4 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2, 3 and 4

Correct answer: (a) 1, 2 and 3 only. India's major reef regions include Andaman-Nicobar, Gulf of Kachchh, Gulf of Mannar and Lakshadweep. The Sundarbans are a mangrove-delta ecosystem, not a coral-reef region.

UPSC CSE Mains 2018 - General Studies Paper I

What are the consequences of spreading of “Dead Zones” on marine ecosystem?

Answer direction: Nutrient enrichment drives algal blooms; decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, producing hypoxia/anoxia, fish kills, benthic loss, food-web change and livelihood damage. Connect the process to untreated sewage, fertiliser runoff, aquaculture and weak flushing in estuaries and coastal waters.

UPSC CSE Mains 2014 - General Studies Paper I

Explain the formation of thousands of islands in Indonesian and Philippines archipelagos.

Answer direction: Explain convergence around the Pacific and Sunda margins: oceanic-plate subduction generates trenches, earthquake zones and volcanic island arcs; accretion, uplift, faulting, coral growth and sea-level change further multiply islands. Link the Sunda arc north-westward to the Andaman tectonic setting while noting that many Andaman-Nicobar islands are uplifted accretionary ridges rather than volcanoes.
