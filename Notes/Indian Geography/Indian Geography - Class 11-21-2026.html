1. Rainfall Extremes and the Annual Rainfall Distribution

Annual rainfall in India ranges from under 25 cm in the deserts to over 1,000 cm on the Meghalaya plateau - one of the steepest rainfall gradients in the world. The gradient follows three controls already studied: relief (windward or leeward of a mountain), distance from the sea and the direction of the monsoon branches (see the 16 November note, 6.2).

[Correction] The notes list the Thar as a whole under "below 25 cm". 1.2 Very High Rainfall

  • Where: the north-east (Meghalaya plateau, sub-Himalayan Assam and Arunachal, hills of Mizoram-Manipur-Tripura), the windward Western Ghats and west coast, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
  • Why: both monsoon branches are forced up mountains (orographic lifting); the islands sit in the sea and get rain from both monsoons.
  • Mawsynram (Meghalaya) - 11,872 mm (about 1,187 cm) average annual rainfall, the WMO-listed record for greatest average annual rainfall (period 1941-1979), on the south-facing, funnel-shaped Khasi Hills. Neighbouring Cherrapunji (Sohra) holds the record for the most rain in 12 months (1860-61)†.
  • [Additional] Among IMD sub-divisions, Konkan and Goa has the highest annual normal (over 300 cm), followed by north-eastern sub-divisions (200-280 cm) (IMD, 2022).
Threshold note. The notes put "very high" at above 250 cm. NCERT's rainfall-zone map uses over 200 cm for the very-high zone; 250 cm is the threshold usually given for tropical wet evergreen forest (section 4). Both appear in books; for maps of rainfall zones use 200 cm, for forest types use 250 cm.

1.3 The 100 cm Isohyet: India's Wet-Dry Divide

An isohyet is a line joining places with equal rainfall. The 100 cm annual isohyet runs roughly down the leeward (eastern) side of the Western Ghats, across western Madhya Pradesh to the Ganga plain near Prayagraj, and north-west along the Himalayan foothills to Jammu (see Map 1). It splits India into two contrasting halves.

2. Retreat of the South-west Monsoon and the North-east Monsoon

2.1 October-November: the Transition (Retreating Monsoon Season)

October-November is the transitional period of seasonal reversal of winds: the south-west (summer) monsoon withdraws and the north-east (winter) monsoon winds set in.

Sun moves south after the September equinox → land of north-west India cools; the heat low weakens → monsoon trough (ITCZ) shifts south → an anticyclone and dry, sinking air set in over NW India; the subtropical westerly jet returns south of the Himalaya → SW winds withdraw, first from west Rajasthan → NE winds take over
  • Dates (IMD normals): withdrawal begins from west Rajasthan around 17 September and is complete from the whole country around 15 October. NCERT's older text says the retreat from west Rajasthan begins in the first week of September; IMD revised its normal dates in 2020†, so use 17 September.
  • Weather: clear skies, high day temperature and high humidity over the plains - the oppressive "October heat"†.
  • Retreat order: north-west India first, then central India, then the east and finally the south peninsula.
[Correction] The notes say the south-west monsoon departs from north-west India "due to westerly winds". The westerlies are a result as much as a cause. The withdrawal starts because the sun moves south, the north-west Indian heat low weakens and the monsoon trough shifts south; an anticyclone with dry, sinking air then forms over north-west India and the subtropical westerly jet re-establishes south of the Himalaya. IMD declares withdrawal using the end of rain for several days, an anticyclone at low levels and a fall in moisture†.

2.2 The North-east Monsoon (October-December)

  • Winds: the north-east winds are dry over land, so most of India has a dry winter. Where they cross the Bay of Bengal they pick up moisture and give rain to the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu, Puducherry, south coastal Andhra Pradesh) and to the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
  • IMD's NE monsoon region (five sub-divisions): Tamil Nadu-Puducherry-Karaikal, Coastal Andhra Pradesh-Yanam, Rayalaseema, South Interior Karnataka, and Kerala-Mahe.
  • Importance: Tamil Nadu gets about 48% of its annual rain in October-December and only 36% in June-September (IMD, 2022) - it lies in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats in summer and the Bay branch runs parallel to its coast.
  • Rain-bringers (NCERT): the NE winds, which "pick up moisture" over the Bay and give torrential rain to the Tamil Nadu coast, south Andhra Pradesh, south-east Karnataka and south-east Kerala in October-November; NCERT adds that the bulk of the Coromandel coast's rain comes from cyclonic depressions crossing from the Bay. In short: the NE winds themselves (lifted by the coast and the Eastern Ghats) plus depressions and cyclones from the Bay of Bengal - the post-monsoon cyclone season (see the 16 November note, 4.6).
[Correction] The notes say the north-east monsoon is "also called the retreating SW monsoon". They are linked but not the same. The retreating monsoon is the October-November season during which the south-west monsoon withdraws; the north-east (winter) monsoon is the reversed wind system of October-December that follows. The Coromandel coast's rain comes from the north-east winds and Bay depressions, not from the retreating south-west winds. Some books loosely use "retreating monsoon" for both; in answers, keep them distinct.

3. Module 4: Natural Vegetation - Introduction

3.1 What Decides the Natural Vegetation

Natural vegetation is the plant community that has grown without human help and has adjusted to its environment over a long time. It is a manifestation of climate, physiography (relief) and hydrology, as the notes say, together with soil and living factors.

ControlEffect on Indian vegetation
Rainfall (amount and season)The master control on the plains and plateaus: it decides evergreen versus deciduous versus thorn. A long dry season makes trees shed leaves.
TemperatureTropical forests need warmth all year (mean annual temperature above 22°C for evergreen forest, NCERT); in the mountains falling temperature with height gives montane belts (a later class).
Relief and slopeWindward slopes are wetter (evergreen Western Ghats) than leeward ones (deciduous Deccan); aspect matters in the Himalaya.
Soil and hydrology [Additional]Tidal, saline, waterlogged mud gives mangroves; freshwater swamps give swamp forests. These are edaphic (soil-controlled) types, not rainfall types.
Living and human factors [Additional]Grazing, fire, shifting cultivation, felling and plantations have turned much dry deciduous forest into open scrub and parkland.

Indian vegetation is largely monsoon vegetation. Because most rain falls in June-September, followed by a long dry season, most trees shed their leaves seasonally to save water. Hence tropical deciduous ("monsoon") forests are the dominant forest type of India.

4. Tropical Wet Evergreen Forests

4.1 How and Where They Form

Rainfall above 250 cm + warmth all year (about 25-27°C) + very short dry season → no need to shed leaves together → trees shed, flower and fruit at different times → forest stays green all year (evergreen)
  • Distribution: windward (western) slopes of the Western Ghats (Kerala, Karnataka, Goa, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu), the north-east (Upper Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur, Tripura) and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Champion and Seth put the area at about 4.1 million ha† (NBSAP).
  • Example: Silent Valley National Park (Kerala) preserves one of the last undisturbed tracts†.

4.2 Features

  • Tall, dense, multi-layered (stratified) - emergent trees reach 45-60 m; the canopy is so thick that little light reaches the floor (see Figure 2).
  • Hardwood, broad-leaved trees; mixed stands - many species grow together, rarely a pure stand of one species.
  • Climbers (lianas), epiphytes (orchids, ferns), mosses; bamboos and canes in places; the flora of the north-east has Malayan affinities.
  • Species: rosewood, ebony, mahogany, jackfruit (notes); NCERT also lists aini, rubber and cinchona; others include mesua, gurjan, dhup and toon†.
  • Rich biodiversity zone - core of two global biodiversity hotspots (Western Ghats-Sri Lanka and Indo-Burma).
[Additional] Mahogany is introduced. True mahogany (Swietenia) is native to tropical America and was planted in India; NCERT lists it among evergreen species because it grows well in these forests†. Rosewood (Dalbergia latifolia), ebony (Diospyros ebenum) and jackfruit are native.

4.3 Importance and Problems

  • Ecological: highest species richness; water towers for peninsular rivers (Western Ghats); large carbon store; climate regulation.
  • Economic: fine, hard, durable timber; spices, cane, medicinal plants; eco-tourism.
  • Why little commercial use: hard wood difficult to work, mixed stands (no large patches of one tree), dense growth and steep, wet terrain - poor access.
  • Threats: plantations (rubber, tea, coffee, cardamom), dams and roads, shifting cultivation (jhum) in the north-east, encroachment. ISFR 2023 records forest-cover loss in the Western Ghats eco-sensitive area (58.22 km² over the decade) and in the north-eastern region (327.30 km² since 2021).

5. Tropical Semi-evergreen Forests

  • Formation: in the slightly less rainy parts of the same regions, about 200-250 cm, with a somewhat longer dry season; a transition between wet evergreen and moist deciduous forest - a mix of evergreen and deciduous trees.
  • Distribution: Western Ghats (lower and leeward slopes), Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, parts of Odisha and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (NBSAP). Area about 2.4 million ha†.
  • Features: moderately dense; canopy not continuous; fewer species than the wet evergreen; climbers and undergrowth stay green, so the forest still looks green when deciduous trees are bare.
  • Species (NCERT): white cedar, hollock, kail; also laurel, rosewood, mesua and champa†.
[Correction] The notes list teak and sandalwood among semi-evergreen species. Both are deciduous trees: teak is the signature tree of moist (and dry) deciduous forests, and sandalwood is a tree of deciduous and dry forests of the southern peninsula. Teak may enter the deciduous edge of a semi-evergreen belt, but the textbook species of semi-evergreen forests are white cedar, hollock and kail. NCERT's own exercise asks "Sandalwood is an example of ..." with the answer deciduous forest.
Ebony and mahogany (also in the notes' semi-evergreen list) belong mainly to the wet evergreen forest; they can occur in the evergreen component of semi-evergreen stands.

6. Tropical Moist Deciduous Forests

6.1 Formation and Distribution

Rain 100-200 cm, mostly June-September → dry season of 4-6 months → trees shed leaves in spring and early summer to cut water loss (6-8 weeks) → new leaves with the monsoon
  • The most common forest of moderately high rainfall; deciduous forests together are the dominant forest of India.
  • Distribution: a strip along the Himalayan foothills (Shiwalik, Bhabar-Terai); the eastern (leeward) slopes of the Western Ghats; the Chhota Nagpur plateau, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, east Madhya Pradesh; and the north-eastern hills (NBSAP). Area about 22.4 million ha†.

6.2 Features and Species

  • Trees about 2-3 layers, fewer species than evergreen forest, more pure stands of commercially useful trees.
  • Sal (Shorea robusta) - strong, durable timber; the major tree of the northern and eastern moist deciduous forests (Terai, Chhota Nagpur, Odisha). Teak (Tectona grandis) dominates the southern and western forests (Western Ghats, central India). This sal-east / teak-west split is a classic exam point.
  • Other species: shisham, mahua, amla, peepal, semul, kusum, arjun, hurra, sandalwood and bamboo; NCERT Class 11 places sandalwood in this list.
  • [Additional] In ISFR 2023, sal is the leading species by growing stock inside forests (11.43%), followed by teak and chir pine.
[Correction] Tendu (Diospyros melanoxylon), listed in the notes under moist deciduous, is placed by NCERT in the dry deciduous forest (with palas, amaltas, bel, khair and axlewood). It is the bidi-leaf tree of central India. Mahua occurs in both moist and dry deciduous forests.
[Correction] The notes call sandalwood "endangered". On the IUCN Red List, Indian sandalwood (Santalum album) is Vulnerable (assessed 2018, published 2019), threatened by over-harvesting for heartwood and oil and by spike disease. It is red sanders (Pterocarpus santalinus) that is Endangered (IUCN 2021) - see section 8.2.

6.3 Importance and Problems

  • Most commercially exploited forests - timber (sal, teak), bamboo for paper, minor forest produce (mahua flowers, lac, tendu leaves, honey) supporting tribal livelihoods.
  • Problems: large areas cleared for farming, mining (Chhota Nagpur coal and iron ore belt) and settlements; forest fires in the dry season; grazing; invasive Lantana camara.

7. Tropical Mangrove (Littoral and Swamp) Forests

7.1 What Mangroves Are and the Conditions They Need

Mangroves are salt-tolerant trees and shrubs (halophytes) of the intertidal zone - land flooded at high tide and exposed at low tide - on tropical and subtropical coasts.

  • Warm water and air (tropical and subtropical latitudes).
  • Sheltered, low-energy coasts: deltas, estuaries, creeks, lagoons and gulfs where waves are weak.
  • Fine, sediment-rich mud brought by rivers and tides.
  • Brackish water - mangroves tolerate sea water but grow best where river fresh water mixes with sea water (lower salinity), as the notes say.
  • Waterlogged, anaerobic (oxygen-poor) soil - the main stress, together with salt; hence their special roots.
Swamp versus marsh. Both are wetlands. A swamp is a wetland dominated by woody plants (trees, shrubs) - mangroves form tidal swamp forests. A marsh is a wetland dominated by grasses, reeds and herbs (salt marsh, freshwater marsh).

[Correction] The notes say mangrove "leaves have large pores for salt excretion". Salt is excreted through specialised salt glands on the leaf surface (for example in Avicennia), not through large pores. [Correction] The notes say "one mangrove tree absorbs 3 times more CO2 than others". The comparison is per unit area of ecosystem, not per tree, and most of the carbon is in the waterlogged soil, not the tree. The commonly quoted range is that mangrove ecosystems store or sequester about 3-5 times (often "up to four times") more carbon per hectare than tropical land forests; India's environment minister cited "four to five times" at COP27. Use "up to about four times per hectare".

7.7 Threats and Challenges

  • Climate change: sea-level rise drowns mangroves that cannot move inland ("coastal squeeze" against embankments and towns); more frequent and intense cyclones (Amphan 2020, Yaas 2021 in the Sundarbans†); rising salinity.
  • Reduced fresh water and silt from upstream dams and barrages - raises salinity; Sundarbans sundari suffers "top-dying"†.
  • Conversion for shrimp and prawn aquaculture, paddy, salt pans, ports and real estate (Mumbai, Chennai, Gujarat coasts).
  • Pollution: marine pollution, oil spills, river-borne sewage, industrial effluents and plastics.
  • Over-harvesting of fuelwood, fodder and timber; grazing.
  • Data concern: net national gains hide local losses - ISFR 2023 shows a 7.43 km² fall since 2021, with declines in Gujarat and A&N.

7.8 Conservation Measures

  • CRZ Notification 2019 (Environment (Protection) Act, 1986): mangroves are ecologically sensitive areas (CRZ-IA); a 50 m buffer where a mangrove patch exceeds 1,000 m²; if mangroves are lost to essential projects, three times the number must be replanted. Also protected under the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 1980, the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, the Indian Forest Act, 1927 and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002.
  • MISHTI - Mangrove Initiative for Shoreline Habitats and Tangible Incomes: announced in the Union Budget 2023-24, launched on 5 June 2023 (World Environment Day); aims to restore/afforest about 540 km² of mangroves in 9 coastal states and 4 UTs; implemented through convergence with MGNREGS and CAMPA (gap funding) and state plans. By August 2025, 22,560.34 ha had been taken up in 13 states/UTs, 19,220 ha of it in Gujarat (PIB, 21 August 2025).
  • Mangrove Alliance for Climate (MAC): launched on 8 November 2022 at COP27, Sharm el-Sheikh (Egypt), led by the UAE and Indonesia; India joined as a founding partner with Sri Lanka, Australia, Japan and Spain. Voluntary - members set their own targets; no binding commitments.
  • National Coastal Mission - "Conservation and Management of Mangroves and Coral Reefs" supports 38 identified mangrove sites (60:40 centre-state funding); the GCF-funded ECRICC project in Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra and Odisha (PIB, 27 March 2025).
  • Ramsar sites and protected areas (Sundarbans, Bhitarkanika); community-based management (Pichavaram)†.

7.9 Mains Frame: Significance and Challenges of Mangroves

Introduction: Mangroves are salt-tolerant intertidal forests; India has 4,991.68 km² (0.15% of its area, ISFR 2023), 42% of it in the Sundarbans.

Significance: (1) bio-shield against cyclones, surges and tsunamis; (2) erosion control, land building and salinity control; (3) biodiversity and fish nurseries - tiger, crocodile, mud crab; (4) livelihoods - fisheries, honey, eco-tourism; (5) blue carbon - up to about four times the carbon per hectare of land forests; (6) water filtration.

Challenges: sea-level rise and coastal squeeze; stronger cyclones; reduced freshwater and silt from dams; aquaculture, ports and urban sprawl; pollution and oil spills; over-harvesting; local losses hidden by national net gains.

Way forward: enforce CRZ 2019; scale MISHTI with community ownership and native species (not monoculture); ensure environmental flows to deltas; include mangroves in carbon markets and India's NDC sink; use MAC for knowledge sharing.

Conclusion: Mangroves are cheap, self-repairing coastal infrastructure - protecting them is both climate adaptation and mitigation.

8. Tropical Dry Vegetation (below 100 cm)

8.1 Overview

  • Found west of the 100 cm isohyet and in rain-shadow areas: an open forest where grass is more prominent and trees are smaller and widely spaced.
  • Two types (notes): tropical dry deciduous (70-100 cm; semi-arid to semi-humid) and tropical thorn vegetation (below 70 cm; semi-arid to arid).
  • Common adaptations: small leaves, thick bark, deep roots, long leafless dry season, fire tolerance.
Threshold note. NCERT Class 9 puts thorn forests below 70 cm†; NCERT Class 11 puts them below 50 cm; Champion and Seth give roughly 25-75 cm (NBSAP). The notes follow the 70 cm rule.

8.2 Tropical Dry Deciduous Forests (70-100 cm)

  • Formation: rainfall of about 70-100 cm with a long dry season of 6-8 months; trees stay leafless for long periods; frequent dry-season fires keep the forest open.
  • Distribution: irregular wide strips from the Himalayan foothills to Kanyakumari - the rainier parts of the peninsular plateau and the rain-shadow Deccan, the plains of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, east Rajasthan, Gujarat, west Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu (NBSAP). Champion and Seth put it at about 29.7 million ha, the largest of their type groups†.
  • Landscape - "parkland": short trees (generally below 25 m, often 10-20 m), a light, broken canopy and large open stretches of grass - like a park (Figure 2).
  • Species: neem, palas (flame of the forest), amaltas (golden shower), moringa (drumstick, sahjan), khair (Acacia catechu, source of katha), red sanders; also teak and sal in drier forms, tendu, bel, axlewood, Boswellia and Hardwickia†.
  • Red sanders (Pterocarpus santalinus) - endemic to the southern Eastern Ghats of Andhra Pradesh (Seshachalam and adjoining hills; Chittoor, Kadapa, Nellore, Prakasam districts); prized red heartwood; IUCN Endangered (2021) after a brief "Near Threatened" listing in 2018; CITES Appendix II; heavily smuggled.
[Additional] Why "fast animals"? Open grassland with scattered trees offers little cover, so animals rely on speed and keen eyesight: blackbuck, chinkara (Indian gazelle), chital (spotted deer), nilgai, and predators such as the Asiatic lion (only wild population in Gir, Gujarat - a dry deciduous forest), the tiger (e.g. Ranthambore†) and now the African cheetah.
[Current Affairs] Project Cheetah (as of 20 November 2026).
  • Launched on 17 September 2022 with 8 cheetahs from Namibia released in Kuno National Park (Sheopur district, Madhya Pradesh); 12 from South Africa followed in February 2023 and 9 from Botswana (6 females, 3 males) in February 2026. India's cheetah had been declared extinct in 1952.
  • A second site, Gandhi Sagar Wildlife Sanctuary (Madhya Pradesh), was added in 2025 (PIB year-end review, 2025).
  • On 20 November 2026 a Botswana-born female, KOMG 3, gave birth to four cubs in the wild at Kuno - the first litter from a Botswana female and the second recorded birth in the wild. India's count is now 62, including 43 India-born; 59 at Kuno and 3 at Gandhi Sagar.
  • Setbacks: 8 deaths in 2026 (including a wild litter of four lost in May) and India's first suspected cheetah poaching - a 19-month-old male shot in Morena district on 27 October 2026 (Indian Express; ThePrint; Outlook/PTI, 20-21 November 2026).
[Correction] The notes place the African cheetah in Kuno as a "wildlife sanctuary". Kuno is a National Park (upgraded from a wildlife sanctuary in 2018†). The second cheetah site, Gandhi Sagar, is a wildlife sanctuary. The cheetahs are the African subspecies from Namibia, South Africa and Botswana, not the extinct Asiatic cheetah.

8.3 Tropical Thorn Forests (below 70 cm) - preview [Additional]

  • Where: semi-arid and arid areas of south-west Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Gujarat (Kutch, north Gujarat), Madhya Pradesh and the rain-shadow Deccan (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh) and Uttar Pradesh margins.
  • Features: short, scattered, thorny trees and bushes; xerophytic adaptations - small, hard or waxy leaves, thorns instead of leaves, long roots, thick bark, succulent stems.
  • Species: babul (Acacia nilotica), khejri (Prosopis cineraria, Rajasthan's state tree), ber, wild date palm, euphorbias, cacti; invasive Prosopis juliflora has spread widely.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Cold desert = Ladakh (rain shadow of the Great Himalaya, high altitude); hot desert = Thar; West Rajasthan sub-division normal about 33 cm, core under 25 cm.
  • Mawsynram: 11,872 mm (about 1,187 cm) - WMO record for greatest average annual rainfall (1941-1979). Konkan and Goa has the highest sub-division normal (over 300 cm).
  • 100 cm isohyet: wet, flood-prone, rice-growing east versus dry, drought-prone, millet-wheat west.
  • IMD normals (1971-2020): annual 1,160.1 mm (~116 cm); SW monsoon LPA 868.6 mm (~87 cm) = 74.9%; pre-monsoon ~11%, post-monsoon ~10%.
  • SW monsoon withdrawal starts from west Rajasthan ~17 September, ends ~15 October (normal dates); 2026: 19 September to 15 October.
  • NE monsoon (October-December): Tamil Nadu gets ~48% of its annual rain; IMD's five NE monsoon sub-divisions: TN-Puducherry, Coastal AP, Rayalaseema, South Interior Karnataka, Kerala.
  • Forest classification: Champion and Seth (1968), 16 type groups. "Wet" evergreen, "moist" deciduous.
  • Rain bands: wet evergreen >250 cm; semi-evergreen 200-250; moist deciduous 100-200; dry deciduous 70-100; thorn <70 (NCERT XI <50).
  • Teak - moist (and dry) deciduous, south and west; sal - moist deciduous, north and east; sal has the largest growing stock in Indian forests (11.43%, ISFR 2023).
  • Semi-evergreen species: white cedar, hollock, kail. Dry deciduous: tendu, palas, amaltas, bel, khair, axlewood.
  • Sandalwood (Santalum album) - IUCN Vulnerable; red sanders - IUCN Endangered, endemic to AP's Eastern Ghats, CITES Appendix II.
  • Mangroves (ISFR 2023): 4,991.68 km², 0.15%; WB 42.45% > Gujarat > A&N; east coast ~57%. Indonesia largest in the world (~one-fifth); South Asia ~6.4%; India ~3.4% on current data (NCERT prints 7%).
  • Mangrove adaptations: pneumatophores (Avicennia), stilt roots and vivipary (Rhizophora), salt glands, knee roots (Bruguiera); sundari = Heritiera fomes.
  • MISHTI: Budget 2023-24; launched 5 June 2023; ~540 km²; 9 states + 4 UTs; MGNREGS + CAMPA convergence.
  • MAC: COP27, Sharm el-Sheikh, 8 November 2022; led by UAE and Indonesia; India a founding partner; voluntary.
  • CRZ 2019: mangroves = CRZ-IA; 50 m buffer for patches >1,000 m²; 3x compensatory replanting.
  • Project Cheetah: Kuno NP (MP) since 17 September 2022; Namibia, South Africa, Botswana; Gandhi Sagar WLS second site; count 62 (43 India-born) on 20 November 2026.

Mains Points

  • Rainfall gradient and its consequences: relief, distance from sea and monsoon branches explain the 1,187 cm-to-10 cm range; the 100 cm isohyet maps flood versus drought, rice versus millets, dense versus open forest (1.3).
  • Retreating versus NE monsoon: mechanism of withdrawal (sun, heat low, ITCZ shift, anticyclone); why Tamil Nadu's rainy season is October-December; cyclone link (2).
  • Natural vegetation as a mirror of climate: why monsoon (deciduous) forests dominate; how rainfall, relief and soil create the evergreen-deciduous-thorn sequence and edaphic mangroves (3).
  • Evergreen forests: biodiversity value versus low commercial use; threats in the Western Ghats and the north-east (4).
  • Mangroves - significance and challenges (model frame in 7.9): bio-shield, blue carbon, livelihoods; sea-level rise, aquaculture, dams, pollution; CRZ 2019, MISHTI, MAC.
  • Dry deciduous forests and wildlife: parkland ecology, fire, grazing; Project Cheetah - habitat suitability, genetic diversity (Botswana cohort), mortality and poaching risks (8.2).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • "Tropical moist evergreen" - wrong name; it is wet evergreen. "Moist" goes with deciduous.
  • Teak in rainforests - no; teak is a deciduous tree (UPSC 2015).
  • Sal versus teak: sal north and east; teak south and west.
  • Tendu is dry deciduous (NCERT), not semi-evergreen or evergreen.
  • Sandalwood "Endangered" - no; Vulnerable. Red sanders is Endangered. They are different trees.
  • Mangroves follow salt and tides, not rainfall - they grow on the dry Kutch coast too.
  • Largest mangrove country = Indonesia; largest contiguous mangrove forest = Sundarbans; largest state cover = West Bengal; second = Gujarat (not Andaman).
  • India's share of world mangroves: NCERT prints 7%, but current data give about 3.4%; ~6.4% is South Asia's share. Read the question's source.
  • Salt excretion is by salt glands, not stomata; vivipary = germination on the parent tree.
  • 117 cm is the old (1961-2010) annual normal; the current one is 116 cm. 87 cm is the SW monsoon LPA, not the annual total.
  • Retreating monsoon is not the same as the NE monsoon; Tamil Nadu's winter rain comes from NE winds and Bay cyclones.
  • Kuno is a National Park; Gandhi Sagar is a Wildlife Sanctuary. India's cheetahs are African.
  • MAC (Mangrove Alliance for Climate, COP27, UAE-Indonesia) is not the same as the Global Mangrove Alliance (NGO-science coalition).
