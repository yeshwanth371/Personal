1. Vaigai River (Tamil Nadu)

  • Source: Varusanadu Hills of the Western Ghats (Theni district). Length: 258 km according to Tamil Nadu WRD; Britannica gives about 240 km.
  • Course: east and south-east past Madurai (the temple city stands on its banks) to Palk Strait / Palk Bay near Ramanathapuram. It is an east-flowing river, completing the list from the last class.
  • Vaigai dam stores water for Madurai and the dry Ramanathapuram-Sivaganga region.
  • [Additional] Inter-basin link: water from the west-flowing Periyar is diverted east through the Mullaperiyar tunnel into the Vaigai basin (6.5; Map 2). This is the root of the Kerala-Tamil Nadu Mullaperiyar dispute.
  • Whether the Vaigai usually reaches the sea or ends in the Ramanathapuram "big tank" (a large irrigation tank): [Verification Required]. Most of its water is used up by irrigation tanks along the way.

2. West-Flowing Peninsular Rivers: Why They Differ

Only a few large peninsular rivers flow west: the Narmada and Tapi in rift troughs, and the Mahi, Sabarmati and Luni from the Aravalli-Malwa region. The rest are short, swift Western Ghats streams falling to the Arabian Sea.

NCERT's explanation: three geological events shaped peninsular drainage:
  1. Subsidence of the western flank of the Peninsula and its submergence below the sea in the early Tertiary. This "disturbed the symmetrical plan of the river on either side of the original watershed". The Western Ghats water divide now lies close to the west coast, so west-side rivers are short and steep.
  2. Upheaval of the Himalayas: the northern flank of the Peninsular block subsided and trough faulting followed. "The Narmada and The Tapi flow in trough faults and fill the original cracks with their detritus materials. Hence, there is a lack of alluvial and deltaic deposits in these rivers."
  3. Slight tilting of the Peninsular block from north-west to south-east turned most drainage towards the Bay of Bengal.
Source: NCERT Class XI, India: Physical Environment, ch. 3.
[Correction] The rough notes say west-flowing rivers "are the outcome of subsidence" and are "short rivers". Two refinements:
  • Subsidence of the western flank explains why the Western Ghats rivers are short.
  • The Narmada and Tapi flow west because they occupy trough faults (rift valleys) linked to the Himalayan upheaval.
  • Not all west-flowing rivers are short: the Narmada is 1,312 km long.

2.1 Estuaries, Not Deltas

  • West-flowing rivers end in estuaries: single, funnel-shaped, tide-dominated mouths where fresh and sea water mix into brackish water.
  • Estuaries are rich in nutrients and nursery habitats, so they suit fisheries and aquatic biodiversity more than delta-type agriculture. The Narmada estuary is about 27 km long, south of Bharuch.
[Correction] The rough notes explain the lack of deltas as "less sediment due to hard rocks". That is coaching shorthand, not NCERT's reason. Use the full set of causes:
  1. For the Narmada and Tapi, the rift troughs absorb the detritus ("fill the original cracks"), as NCERT says.
  2. Short, steep courses give little time to build up sediment.
  3. Strong tides and currents in the Gulf of Khambhat and along the Arabian Sea coast remove what reaches the mouth.
  4. The continental shelf is steeper near some river mouths.
"Estvary" should read estuary.

4. Tapi (Tapti)

  • Second longest west-flowing river: 724 km; basin 65,145 km², of which 79% is in Maharashtra, 15% in MP and 6% in Gujarat.
  • Source: Multai, Betul district, Madhya Pradesh (Satpura region).
  • Trough: between the Satpura range (north) and the Satmala-Ajanta hills (south).
  • Course: MP → Maharashtra (the Khandesh region, Burhanpur-Bhusawal) → Gujarat → Gulf of Khambhat (Arabian Sea).
  • Surat lies on the Tapi near its mouth. Ukai dam (Gujarat) is the main reservoir.
  • Kakrapar Atomic Power Station (KAPS): on the Tapi near Surat.
    • Units 1-2 are 220 MW each.
    • KAPP 3 and 4 are India's first indigenous 700 MWe PHWRs (pressurised heavy-water reactors), now licensed for regular operation (NPCIL).
    • District (Tapi/Surat) and exact distance from Surat: [Verification Required].
[Correction] The rough notes place the Tapi "between Satpura and Western Ghat (Ajanta Hills)". The Ajanta (Satmala) hills are not part of the Western Ghats; they are an east-west range of the Deccan plateau. The Western Ghats form the water divide farther west. The Tapi trough lies between the Satpura and the Ajanta-Satmala ranges.

6.5 Periyar (Kerala) and the Mullaperiyar Dispute

  • Longest river of Kerala: 244 km. It reaches the sea near Kochi through the backwaters.
  • Mullaperiyar dam:

    • completed in 1895; the engineer was Col. John Pennycuick †;
    • it lies in Idukki district, Kerala, but is owned and operated by Tamil Nadu;

    1. In 2006 the Supreme Court allowed the level to be raised from 136 ft to 142 ft.
    2. Kerala then passed a law capping the level at 136 ft.
    3. In May 2014 the Supreme Court struck down that law, restrained Kerala from obstructing the rise to 142 ft, and set up a supervisory committee.

[Correction] The rough notes describe the Kerala-Tamil Nadu dispute as being on "dam height increasing". The dispute is about the reservoir water level (136 ft versus 142 ft, with a longer-term 152 ft claim †), dam safety and Kerala's demand for a new dam. It is not about raising the dam's height.

7. Inland Drainage of North-West India: Luni and Ghaggar

7.1 Luni

  • Largest river system of Rajasthan, west of the Aravallis (NCERT).
  • Source: near Pushkar (Ajmer) in two branches, the Saraswati and the Sabarmati (not the Gujarat river), which join at Govindgarh.
  • Course: south-west past Balotra (Barmer) to the Rann of Kutch; the whole system is ephemeral. Lengths of about 495 km and about 530 km (Britannica) are both cited.
  • Its water turns saline downstream of Balotra, as it crosses salt-bearing desert beds †.
[Correction] The rough notes call the Luni an "inland river that does not reach the ocean" and link it to the "white desert". Three refinements:
  • It does not reach the open sea; it ends in the marshy Rann of Kutch, which itself connects with the Arabian Sea. "Lost in the Rann of Kutch" is the exam phrasing (2010 PYQ).
  • NCERT's drainage chapter calls it ephemeral but does not label it "inland drainage".
  • The "white desert" tourism image belongs to the Great Rann near Dhordo (Kutch). Britannica says only that the Luni reaches the north-eastern Rann.

7.2 Ghaggar (Ghaggar-Hakra)

  • Source: the Shiwaliks of north-western Himachal Pradesh, near Kalka. Length: about 320 km (Britannica).
  • Course: HP → Haryana (it receives the Saraswati stream) → Punjab → Haryana → Rajasthan (Hanumangarh), where it dries up in the Thar. The Hakra is the name of its dry continuation in Pakistan's Cholistan †.
  • It is a seasonal (monsoon-fed) inland stream, with floods in wet years.
  • Saraswati revival: Haryana constituted the Haryana Saraswati Heritage Development Board (PIB). An MoU between Haryana and HP (21 January 2022) provides for the Adi Badri dam on the Somb river in Himachal Pradesh, to feed a revived Saraswati channel.
[Correction] Two fixes:
  • The Ghaggar does not begin in Haryana. It rises in the Shiwaliks of Himachal Pradesh, so the sequence is HP-HR-PB-HR-RJ.
  • That the Ghaggar-Hakra is the "successor" of the Vedic Saraswati is a debated hypothesis (supported by some palaeochannel studies, disputed by others), not an established fact. Present it as an identification proposed by some historians and geologists.

9. Indian Climate: An Introduction

  • Climate is the most dynamic part of physical geography. It links local, regional and global processes: local relief, the Himalaya and Indian Ocean, and planetary winds, jet streams and ENSO.
  • Two main elements: temperature, which is high because much of India is in the tropics, and rainfall, which comes mostly from the monsoon.
  • India is commonly described as having a tropical monsoon climate. NCERT's current wording is "hot monsoonal climate", the prevalent type of South and South-East Asia.
  • Latitude: the Tropic of Cancer (23°30'N) divides India into a tropical south and a subtropical north.
  • The Himalaya, in NCERT's words, "provides an invincible shield to protect the subcontinent from the cold northern winds". Winters in north India are therefore far milder than at the same latitudes in Central Asia or China.
[Correction] The rough notes say that because the Himalaya blocks Siberian cold winds, even the subtropical north becomes hot, "which makes tropical climate". NCERT says the northern part lies in the sub-tropical and temperate zone and has an extreme climate with a high daily and annual range of temperature (cold winters with cold waves and fog; very hot summers). The Himalayan shield makes winters milder than they would otherwise be; it does not make the north tropical. India is "monsoonal" as a whole because the monsoon wind and rainfall regime unifies the subcontinent.

9.1 What Is the Monsoon?

  • Monsoon: a seasonal reversal of winds, with an associated rainfall regime. The word comes from the Arabic "mausim" (season) (IMD). The seasonal reversal of winds is the defining trait of a monsoon climate (2014 PYQ).
  • South-west monsoon: June-September, moist winds from the sea; most of India's rain. Normal onset over Kerala is 1 June (south Andaman Sea 20 May).
  • North-east monsoon: October-December. It is the main rainy season for coastal Tamil Nadu, as winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal.
[Correction] The rough notes say "in winter - north-east monsoon winds". IMD's seasons are:
  • Winter: January-February;
  • Pre-monsoon: March-May;
  • South-west monsoon: June-September;
  • Post-monsoon / north-east monsoon: October-December.
Dry north-easterly winds do blow in winter, but the NE monsoon rainfall season is October-December.

10. Mechanism of the Monsoon: Three Groups of Concepts

ConceptMain ideaProposed by / period

NCERT: "no single theory has been able to explain the monsoon fully". It singles out the onset and the break in the monsoon as the hardest to explain.

10.1 Thermal Concept (Halley, 1686)

  • Who and when: the English astronomer Edmond Halley, in "An Historical Account of the Trade Winds, and Monsoons..." in the Royal Society's Philosophical Transactions (1686). It was the first scientific explanation of the monsoon.
  • Basis: specific heat. Land has a lower specific heat than water, so it heats and cools faster; water heats and cools slowly.

The process, step by step:

Summer: sun overhead in the north→land heats faster than sea→intense low pressure over NW India (April-May)→relative high pressure over the Indian Ocean→moist winds blow sea → land (SW monsoon)→rain
Winter: land cools faster than sea→high pressure over north India and Central Asia→low pressure over the warmer ocean→dry winds blow land → sea (NE monsoon)
Shortcomings of the thermal concept:
  1. Scale problem: it treats the monsoon as a giant land-sea breeze, a local thermal circulation scaled up. The real monsoon is part of the planetary (global) circulation, involving the shift of pressure and wind belts and the upper air.
  2. Timing gap: the lowest pressure and highest temperatures over NW India occur in April-May, but the rains begin only in June. If heating alone drove the monsoon, rain should follow the temperature peak.
  3. Shifting lows: it cannot explain why the land lows are not stationary and shift location suddenly.
  4. Missing anti-monsoon aloft: a purely thermal circulation should have a clear, matching return current in the upper troposphere, and observations do not fully match this.
  5. Onset, breaks and variability: it cannot explain the sudden onset ("burst"), the breaks in the monsoon, or year-to-year differences (droughts and floods), all of which depend on remote factors (NCERT).
  6. [Additional] Rainless low: it cannot explain why the strongest heat low, over the Thar, gets very little rain.
  7. [Additional] Cross-equatorial flow: it does not explain the curving of the southern trades across the equator.
Points 1-5 appear in NCERT or standard coaching texts. Points 6-7 are common textbook arguments not checked against an opened source.
[Correction] Three small fixes to the rough notes:
  • The name is Edmond Halley, not Edmund.
  • He was English; this was before the 1707 union that created Great Britain.
  • Its "major shortcoming" is more precisely that it treats the monsoon as a land-sea breeze on a giant scale, ignoring the planetary circulation.
NCERT also notes that differential heating was the accepted view "towards the end of the nineteenth century".

11. Dynamic Concept and Related Terms

11.1 Dynamic Concept (Flohn, 1951)

  • Who and when: Hermann Flohn of the German Weather Bureau, 1951.
  • Core idea: the Asian monsoon results from seasonal changes in the planetary wind system. These come from the seasonal swing of the temperature and pressure belts, which follow the apparent movement of the sun. They are not just local land-sea heating.
  • Two pillars: (a) the northward and southward shift of the ITCZ; (b) Coriolis deflection of winds crossing the equator.
TermMeaning
ITCZ (Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone)NCERT: "a low pressure zone located at the equator where trade winds converge, and so, it is a zone where air tends to ascend". It is the equatorial calm belt traditionally called the doldrums. It moves with the overhead sun.
Trade windsSteady easterlies blowing from the subtropical highs (about 30°) to the ITCZ: NE trades in the northern hemisphere, SE trades in the southern.
Coriolis forceAn apparent force from the earth's rotation. It deflects moving air to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern (Ferrel's law). It is zero at the equator, maximum at the poles, increases with wind speed, and changes only the direction of the wind, not its speed (Figure 4).
Monsoon troughThe ITCZ's July position over north India: NCERT says "around 20°N-25°N latitudes (over the Gangetic plain), sometimes called the monsoon trough".
Equatorial westerliesFlohn's term for the westerly flow that develops when the ITCZ is displaced north. He saw the SW monsoon as an extension of these westerlies (coaching sources; Flohn's wording not checked against the original).

How the dynamic concept produces the SW monsoon:

Sun's apparent shift north (summer solstice, 21 June)→pressure and wind belts shift north→ITCZ moves north, reaching about 20-25°N by July (monsoon trough)→SE trades of the southern hemisphere are drawn north and cross the equator (between 40° and 60°E, NCERT)→Coriolis now deflects them to the right→they blow from the south-west: the SW monsoon
Winter: ITCZ shifts south of the equator→the NE trades re-establish over India→the NE (winter) monsoon; rain on the Tamil Nadu coast after crossing the Bay (October-December)
[Correction] Four fixes to the rough notes:
  • "Given by multiple scholars": the dynamic concept is chiefly credited to Hermann Flohn (1951).
  • "During June northward shift of ITCZ to tropical line": the shift is gradual, and by July the ITCZ lies at about 20-25°N over the Gangetic plain (the monsoon trough). It straddles the Tropic of Cancer rather than "reaching" it in June.
  • "SW monsoon winds are SE trade winds": they originate as the SE trades of the southern hemisphere but become south-westerlies after crossing the equator and being deflected right by the Coriolis force.
  • The spelling is "Coriolis".
[Additional] Why the dynamic concept is better, and still incomplete:
  • It links the monsoon to global circulation, explains the cross-equatorial SW flow and the seasonal reversal, and fits the June-July advance of the trough.
  • It does not fully explain the sudden burst, the breaks, the upper-air easterly jet, or droughts in years when the ITCZ shift is normal. Hence the modern concept.

11.2 Modern Concept: Preview [Additional]

The class only listed this; detail is expected in the next class.

  • Tibetan Plateau heating in summer creates an upper-air high, which helps set up the Tropical Easterly Jet over the peninsula.
  • The Subtropical Westerly Jet retreats north of the Himalaya in June, which is linked to the onset.
  • ENSO (El Nino / La Nina), the Indian Ocean Dipole and the Somali jet are linked to the strength of each year's monsoon.

  • Vaigai: Varusanadu Hills → Madurai → Palk Strait/Bay; east-flowing; receives Periyar water through the Mullaperiyar tunnel.
  • Narmada: Amarkantak (Anuppur, MP), 1,312 km, the largest west-flowing river; rift valley between the Vindhya and Satpura; touches Maharashtra only as a boundary; estuary south of Bharuch (Gulf of Khambhat).
  • Narmada features: Dhuandhar falls and marble rocks at Bhedaghat (Jabalpur). Indira Sagar has India's largest reservoir by gross storage (12.22 BCM). Omkareshwar has a 600 MW floating solar park (planned). Sardar Sarovar: 163 m concrete gravity dam, 2017. Statue of Unity: 182 m, Sadhu Bet, 31 October 2018.
  • Tapi: Multai (Betul, MP), 724 km, between the Satpura and Satmala-Ajanta; Surat; Ukai; Kakrapar (KAPP 3 and 4 are 700 MWe PHWRs).
  • Mahi: Dhar (MP) → Banswara (RJ) → Gujarat; crosses the Tropic of Cancer twice; Mahi Bajaj Sagar; Mahi Banswara 4 x 700 MW nuclear project (foundation stone 25 September 2025).
  • Sabarmati: Udaipur district (RJ) → Ahmedabad and Gandhinagar → Gulf of Khambhat. Kalpasar is a proposed Gujarat dam across the Gulf of Khambhat (Indo-Dutch cooperation).
  • Short Ghats rivers: Damanganga (Daman and Dadra and Nagar Haveli); Mithi (Mumbai, Mahim Creek); Mandovi/Mahadayi (Dudhsagar; tribunal award 2018 for Goa, Karnataka and Maharashtra); Sharavathi (Jog/Gersoppa, 253 m); Periyar (Kerala's longest at 244 km; Mullaperiyar and Idukki).
  • Inland: the Luni (Pushkar → Rann of Kutch; Rajasthan's largest system); the Ghaggar (Shiwaliks, HP → dies in the Thar; the Hakra in Pakistan).
  • Dawki: the Umngot river, Meghalaya; clear water; flows into Bangladesh.
  • Monsoon: from "mausim"; a seasonal reversal of winds. SW monsoon June-September; NE monsoon October-December; Kerala onset 1 June.
  • Thermal concept: Halley, 1686 (specific heat). Dynamic concept: Flohn, 1951 (ITCZ shift + Coriolis). The ITCZ is at 20-25°N in July (monsoon trough). The SE trades cross the equator between 40° and 60°E.
  • Coriolis: deflects right in the NH and left in the SH; zero at the equator, maximum at the poles; increases with wind speed.

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical themes drawn from the notes:

ThemeArgument and example
Why the west coast has no deltasRift-trough rivers (Narmada, Tapi), short steep Ghats streams, tidal flushing and a steep shelf give estuaries. They have fishery and port value but little delta farmland.
Large dams: development and justiceSardar Sarovar: irrigation, drinking water and power, set against displacement, forest loss and estuary salinity. Rehabilitation pari passu (SC 2000), consent, and cumulative assessment.
Interstate water federalismNWDT (1979), the Mahadayi tribunal (2018) and Mullaperiyar (SC 2006 and 2014) show tribunals and courts deciding allocations and safety. Cooperation and data-sharing remain weak.
Dam safety and ageing infrastructureMullaperiyar (1895) in a seismic zone: Kerala's safety fears against Tamil Nadu's water needs. Cite the Dam Safety Act 2021 † as the institutional response.
River revival and heritageSaraswati revival (Adi Badri dam) is cultural and hydrological, but the palaeochannel identification is debated. Mithi shows urban river degradation and flood risk.
Monsoon theoryThe thermal concept is a land-sea breeze writ large; the dynamic concept adds the planetary belt shift and Coriolis; the modern concept adds jets, Tibet and ENSO/IOD. No single theory explains onset, breaks and variability.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Narmada and Maharashtra: it touches Maharashtra only as a boundary river; the Tapi basin is mostly (79%) in Maharashtra.
  • Largest reservoir vs tallest dam: Indira Sagar is the largest reservoir by gross storage; do not call it "the biggest dam" without a measure.
  • Canal, not pipeline: Sardar Sarovar water reaches Rajasthan through the Narmada Main Canal.
  • Ajanta is not the Western Ghats. The Tapi lies between the Satpura and the Satmala-Ajanta.
  • Jog Falls is on the Sharavathi, not the Netravati. Dhuandhar is near Jabalpur, not in Malwa.
  • Mullaperiyar is in Kerala but operated by Tamil Nadu; the dispute is about the water level (136/142 ft), not dam height. Periyar Lake is a reservoir, not a lagoon.
  • Mahadayi = Mandovi; the tribunal had three parties (Goa, Karnataka, Maharashtra).
  • Kalpasar is a Gujarat proposal, not a central project under construction.
  • Luni's two source branches are named Saraswati and Sabarmati; they are not the Gujarat Sabarmati or the Vedic Saraswati.
  • Ghaggar = Saraswati is a hypothesis, not settled fact.
  • Dawki is a town; the river is the Umngot.
  • NE monsoon rain falls in October-December, not January-February.
  • The SW monsoon begins as the SE trades and is turned right by the Coriolis force after crossing the equator.
  • Coriolis changes wind direction, not speed; it is zero at the equator, which is why cyclones rarely form within about 5° of it †.

30-Second Revision

  • West-flowing rivers are short Ghats streams (from subsidence of the western flank) plus the Narmada and Tapi (in rift troughs). They form estuaries, not deltas.
  • Narmada 1,312 km (Amarkantak to Bharuch); Bhedaghat; Indira Sagar (largest reservoir); Sardar Sarovar 163 m; Statue of Unity 182 m; NBA and SC 2000. Tapi 724 km (Multai to Surat); Ukai; Kakrapar.
  • Mahi crosses the Tropic twice. Sabarmati flows past Ahmedabad. Kalpasar is a proposal. Luni ends in the Rann; Ghaggar dies in the Thar. Umngot flows at Dawki.
  • Climate: hot monsoonal; tropical south and subtropical north; the Himalaya is a shield. Thermal concept: Halley 1686 (land-sea heating; a breeze writ large). Dynamic: Flohn 1951 (ITCZ moves to 20-25°N, SE trades cross the equator and turn right into the SW monsoon).

The Narmada river flows to the west, while most other large peninsular rivers flow to the east. Why?

10. UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - General Studies Paper I (5 marks)

There is no formation of deltas by rivers of the Western Ghat. Why?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Short, steep courses from a water divide close to the coast (subsidence of the western flank).
  • Narmada and Tapi in rift troughs that trap sediment (NCERT).
  • Strong tidal action and a steep shelf, so sediment is flushed out and estuaries form.
  • Examples: the Narmada estuary at Bharuch; Mandovi, Sharavathi.
(2.1, Figure 2). Source: MaargX.

11. UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - General Studies Paper I (15 marks)

What characteristics can be assigned to monsoon climate that succeeds in feeding more than 50 percent of the world population residing in Monsoon Asia?

Suggested answer approach [Additional]:
  • Seasonal reversal of winds and concentrated summer rain that coincides with high temperatures, giving a long growing season (9.1, 10).
  • Rain-fed paddy, double cropping with the winter NE monsoon or irrigation.
  • Fertile alluvial plains and deltas.
  • Rivers fed by monsoon rain and Himalayan snowmelt.
  • Limits: variability, droughts and floods, which explain why prediction and irrigation matter.
Source: MaargX.

Corrections Made

Each link returns to the explanation, which keeps the original rough-note claim or its intended meaning.

  1. Subsidence explains the short Ghats rivers; the Narmada and Tapi follow trough faults; the Narmada is long.
  2. No deltas: rift sediment trap, steep courses, tides and shelf, not only "hard rocks"; "estuary".
  3. The Narmada touches Maharashtra only as a boundary river.
  4. Indira Sagar: India's largest reservoir by storage, not just "biggest dam of MP".
  5. Narmada water reaches Rajasthan by canal, not pipeline.
  6. Ajanta-Satmala hills are not part of the Western Ghats.
  7. Mahi Banswara is a nuclear project under construction (2025), not an operating plant "on the river".
  8. Kalpasar is a Government of Gujarat proposal at report/clearance stage, not under construction.
  9. The Damanganga flows through MH, GJ and the UT; the boundary claim is unverified.
  10. The Mahadayi dispute has three parties (Goa, Karnataka, Maharashtra).
  11. Mullaperiyar dispute: water level and safety, not dam height.
  12. The Luni ends in the Rann of Kutch; NCERT calls it ephemeral; the "white desert" is the Great Rann.
  13. The Ghaggar rises in HP; its identification with the Saraswati is a debated hypothesis.
  14. The Dawki river is the Umngot; "cleanest river" is a tourism label.
  15. North India is subtropical with an extreme climate; the Himalaya moderates winters but does not make it tropical.
  16. NE monsoon rainfall season is October-December, not winter.
  17. Edmond Halley (English), 1686; the shortcoming is the "giant land-sea breeze" framing.
  18. Flohn 1951; ITCZ at 20-25°N by July; the SW monsoon begins as the SE trades deflected by Coriolis.
