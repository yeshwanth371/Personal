1. Syntaxial Bends: The Two Great Bends of the Himalaya

At its two ends the Himalayan arc does not simply stop - it bends sharply southward. These hairpin turns are called syntaxial bends (syntaxes), also loosely referred to as the Great Bends. They mark where the northward-moving Indian Plate indents into Eurasia at two corners, and where the mountain trend swings from roughly east-west to roughly north-south.

[Correction] The rough note writes "extensions at easter and western most parts". The accurate expression is: the Himalayas have two syntaxial bends at their eastern and western extremities, where the ranges turn sharply southward. "Syntaxial", not "syntaxial bending" as a separate phenomenon - it is one term.
[Correction] The note says "near Namcha Barwa even called as North-east Hilly region". More precisely, the hills that run southward from the eastern syntaxis, inside India, are collectively called the Purvanchal or the North-Eastern Hills. Namcha Barwa itself lies in Tibet, not in the North-East hilly region.
[Additional] Mechanism worth remembering: a syntaxis forms where a rigid plate corner (an indenter) pushes into another plate. Rapid rock uplift there is coupled with extremely rapid river erosion - a classic example of tectonic-erosional coupling. This makes both syntaxes among the most landslide-prone and seismically active spots in the entire orogen.

2. Purvanchal - The North-Eastern Hills

South of the eastern syntaxis, the Himalayan trend swings from east-west to north-south and continues into India's north-east as a set of low-to-moderate, densely forested, heavily dissected hill ranges known collectively as the Purvanchal (Eastern Hills). They are structurally part of the same young fold belt but are far lower and wetter than the main Himalaya.

4. Northern Mountains and the Hindu Kush Himalaya (HKH)

India's Northern Mountains = Himalayas + Trans-Himalayas. At the wider Asian scale, this belt is one segment of the Hindu Kush Himalaya (HKH) region, which stretches across eight countries.

The HKH extends from the Hindu Kush in northern Afghanistan in the west to the Arakan range in Myanmar in the east, with the Himalaya as its spine, and includes the Tibetan Plateau.

4.2 Why it is Called the "Third Pole"

  • It holds the largest store of snow and ice outside the Arctic and Antarctic - hence "Third Pole".
  • It is also called the "Water Tower of Asia": it feeds ten major river systems including the Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra, Irrawaddy, Salween, Mekong, Yangtze, Yellow, Amu Darya and Tarim.
  • Roughly two billion people depend directly or indirectly on its water, energy, food and ecosystem services.
[Correction] The rough note says the region "covers with full of ice". This overstates it. The HKH is the largest ice mass outside the polar regions, but only a fraction of its total area is glacierised; much of it is rock, cold desert, forest and inhabited valley. Say "greatest concentration of snow and ice outside the poles", not "full of ice".
[Correction] The expansion in the rough note, "International Center for Integrated Mountain Department", is wrong. The correct name is the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD).

5. Significance of the Himalayas / HKH Region

Being the highest natural barrier on the planet's most populous margin, the Himalayas deliver a bundle of climatic, strategic, cultural, economic and ecological services to the Indian subcontinent.

5.1 Climatic Relevance

FunctionMechanismConsequence for India
Barrier to cold airBlocks the intensely cold, dry continental air masses of Central Asia and Siberia from sweeping southNorth India has far milder winters than other places at the same latitude; tropical conditions extend unusually far north.
Orographic trap for the South-West MonsoonThe wall forces the moist monsoon current to rise, cool and condense; it cannot escape northwardHeavy monsoon rainfall over the Himalayan foothills and the Ganga-Brahmaputra plains; extreme rain in the eastern Himalaya and Meghalaya.
Interception of Western DisturbancesExtratropical low-pressure systems steered in by the subtropical westerly jet from the Mediterranean regionWinter rain and snow in the north-west; critical for rabi crops and for recharging the snowpack that feeds spring/summer river flow.
[Correction] The note says "Traps Sout-west monsoon (Rains)". Refined statement: the Himalayas obstruct and deflect the south-west monsoon, forcing orographic ascent and rainfall over the subcontinent instead of allowing the moisture to move into Central Asia. They modify, rather than create, the monsoon - the monsoon also depends on differential land-sea heating, the ITCZ shift and the heating of the Tibetan Plateau.

5.2 Strategic and Cultural Significance

  • Defence geography: the high wall gives depth and protection along the northern frontier; movement is channelled through a limited number of passes and valleys.
  • Definition of a subcontinent: the Himalaya-Hindu Kush arc, together with the seas on three sides, gives the Indian subcontinent a clear natural boundary - which is precisely why European and later scholars treated it as a distinct "subcontinent" rather than a mere part of Asia.
  • Cultural-political consequence: relative insulation from the north allowed a long, largely continuous civilisational development within one natural unit, producing a shared cultural fabric alongside great internal diversity - the classic phrase Unity in Diversity.
  • Sacred geography: Kailash-Mansarovar, Badrinath, Kedarnath, Amarnath, Hemkund Sahib and Tawang make the range a pilgrimage landscape, which also creates concentrated seasonal pressure on fragile valleys.
[Correction] "Well protected from North by Himalayan Shield" needs qualification. The Himalayas are a formidable barrier, but they have never been an impassable wall: passes such as the Khyber and Bolan in the north-west, and Himalayan passes elsewhere, have long carried invasions, migration, trade and pilgrimage. "Shield" is a metaphor, not a geological or military absolute. (Note also that "shield" in geology means an exposed Precambrian craton - a different concept entirely.)
[Correction] "Indian Subcontinent largely remained peaceful" is too strong. The accurate claim is that the northern mountain barrier limited large-scale intrusion from the north and helped preserve continuity of settlement and culture - not that the subcontinent was free of conflict.

6. Main Challenge: Disasters of a Cascading Nature

The central idea for Mains: Himalayan disasters are rarely single events. One trigger sets off a chain - a rock/ice fall becomes a debris flow, which becomes a river blockage, which becomes an outburst flood, which destroys infrastructure downstream. This is a cascading (or compound) disaster, and it is what makes the Himalaya distinct from other hazard-prone regions.

6.1 Layer 1 - Intrinsic Geological Fragility

  • Young, actively converging orogen: the Indian Plate continues to underthrust Eurasia, so strain accumulates and is released as earthquakes.
  • Weak rock mass: highly fractured, sheared and metamorphosed rock, with thick sedimentary sequences in the outer belts.
  • Very steep slopes combined with deep river incision undercutting slope toes.
  • Multiple major fault/thrust systems running along the belt.
StructureCorrect namePosition and significance
HFFHimalayan Frontal Thrust (HFT), also called the Main Frontal Thrust (MFT)Southernmost active thrust, at the boundary between the Shiwalik and the plains; marks the present deformation front.
MBFMain Boundary Thrust (MBT)Separates the Lesser Himalaya from the Shiwalik; a major landslide-prone corridor.
MCTMain Central Thrust (MCT)Separates the Greater Himalayan crystallines from the Lesser Himalaya; associated with a belt of concentrated seismicity.

[Correction] The rough notes use "Fault" for all three (HFF, MBF, MCT). In standard Himalayan geology these are thrusts - reverse faults with low dip that carry older rock over younger. Preferred usage: HFT/MFT, MBT, MCT.
[Correction] The 2015 Nepal (Gorkha) earthquake, listed in the notes next to HFF, was a Mw 7.8 event on 25 April 2015 with its epicentre near Barpak in Gorkha district. It ruptured a segment of the Main Himalayan Thrust and did not produce surface rupture on the frontal thrust. It killed roughly 9,000 people and triggered massive secondary hazards, including the Langtang valley rock-ice avalanche that buried entire villages.

6.2 Layer 2 - Climate Change as a Multiplier

Climate change does not usually create a new hazard type in the Himalaya. It increases the frequency, magnitude and reach of existing ones. Treat it as an aggravating multiplier.

Pathway A - Direct (cryospheric) impact

Global warming→glacier melt, permafrost thaw, ice destabilised→ice/rock avalanche or glacier collapse→debris flow / river blockage / lake breach→flash flood downstream
  • Faster glacier melt increases river discharge in the short term and can overwhelm channels: example - repeated Brahmaputra basin flooding.
  • Retreating glaciers leave moraine-dammed proglacial lakes. These loose debris dams can fail, producing a Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF).
  • Permafrost thaw removes the "cryospheric glue" holding rock and ice on steep faces, raising the likelihood of collapse - the mechanism repeatedly cited after the 2026 Nepal disaster.
  • Avalanches can trigger rockfall, and rockfall can in turn dam a river, which then breaches - the essence of cascading behaviour.
[Correction] The rough note writes "pro-glacier lakes". The correct term is proglacial lake - a lake in front of a retreating glacier, usually impounded by an unconsolidated moraine dam. It is the weakness of that moraine dam, not the glacier itself, that causes most GLOFs.

Pathway B - Indirect (atmospheric moisture) impact

Ocean and air warming→about 7% more water vapour per 1°C→deeper, more energetic cumulonimbus→cloudburst→flash flood, landslide, lake overtopping
[Additional] The "7% per degree" figure is the Clausius-Clapeyron relationship: the saturation water-vapour capacity of air rises by roughly 6-7% for every 1°C of warming. Short, intense rainfall extremes can increase even faster than this in convective storms. This is the scientific basis for saying that cloudbursts are becoming more damaging.
[Correction] The rough note's phrasing "1* incon ocean surface -> 7% moisture" should read: a 1°C rise in temperature allows the atmosphere to hold roughly 7% more moisture. It is an atmospheric capacity relationship, not a property of the ocean surface alone.

[Additional] Why the concave bank matters: on a river bend, the concave (outer) bank is the zone of maximum velocity and active lateral erosion / undercutting; the convex (inner) bank is the depositional side. Building hotels and roads on the concave bank places them exactly where the river is scouring the toe of the slope. This single point converts a vague "unplanned tourism" statement into a precise geomorphological argument.

6.5 Model Answer Skeleton

Question: Examine the causes of ever-increasing disasters of a cascading nature in the Himalayas. (250 words / 15 marks)

Intro (2-3 lines): Define a cascading disaster - a primary trigger that sets off a chain of secondary and tertiary hazards. State that in the Himalaya the chain runs: slope failure → debris flow / river blockage → outburst flood → infrastructure loss.

Body - three drivers:

  1. Tectonic and geomorphic predisposition: active convergence, MHT/MCT/MBT/HFT, sheared rock, steep slopes, deep incision. Anchor: Gorkha 2015 and its Langtang avalanche.
  2. Climate change as multiplier: direct cryospheric route (melt, permafrost thaw, proglacial lakes, GLOF) plus indirect atmospheric route (Clausius-Clapeyron, cloudbursts). Anchors: South Lhonak 2023, Langtang Lirung 2026, Chamoli 2021.
  3. Anthropogenic amplification: over-tourism and green-to-grey conversion, construction on concave banks and steep slopes, road widening and tunnelling, blast mining, mega dams in paraglacial gorges, deforestation and jhum. Anchors: Joshimath 2023, Teesta-III, Medog project concerns.

Link (the exam-winning line): Hazard is natural; disaster is the product of hazard, exposure and vulnerability. Rising exposure - population, tourists, dams, highways - is what converts an increasingly energetic mountain system into ever-larger disasters.

Conclusion: Call for hazard-informed, carrying-capacity-bound development within a regional (HKH) cooperation framework.

7. Steps to Reduce Disaster Risk in the Himalayan Belt

Organise remedies into five tiers. This structure works for both a 250-word Mains answer and quick revision.

1. Build differentlyClimate- and seismic-resilient design; revive proven vernacular systems.
2. Know the hazardMapping, monitoring and early warning using modern geospatial technology.
3. Regulate developmentCarrying capacity, building codes, environmental appraisal, no-build zones.
4. CooperateLocal communities, national institutions, and transboundary/global frameworks.

7.1 Climate- and Seismic-Resilient Structures

  • Design for both shaking and water: seismic detailing plus drainage, slope protection and flood freeboard.
  • Learn from vernacular architecture - traditional Himalayan buildings used deodar timber bands with dry-laid local stone, giving flexibility and energy dissipation during earthquakes. [Additional] Named systems: Kath-Kuni and Koti Banal (Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand) and Dhajji Dewari (Jammu and Kashmir). These are recognised examples of earthquake-resistant traditional construction.
  • Light-weight, low-rise construction on stable ground rather than heavy concrete on fill and debris slopes.
  • Retrofit critical lifeline buildings: hospitals, schools, control rooms, bridges.

7.2 Hazard Mapping, Monitoring and Early Warning

  • Hazard and vulnerability mapping of geological hotspots - landslide susceptibility zonation, active fault mapping, subsidence mapping.
  • Remote sensing and satellite monitoring for glacier change, glacial-lake inventories and slope deformation. ISRO-NRSC's Bhuvan and the GSI Bhukosh landslide database are the standing Indian platforms.
  • Drone and UAV survey for rapid, high-resolution assessment of inaccessible slopes and post-event damage.
  • Doppler Weather Radar network for nowcasting cloudbursts and intense convective rainfall - central to short-lead flash-flood warning.
  • Real-time telemetry and data sharing: river-level and rainfall gauges, lake-level sensors, seismic networks, plus transboundary hydrological data exchange.
  • Last-mile dissemination: SMS/cell-broadcast alerts, sirens, community volunteers. [Additional] The Sikkim 2023 and Nepal 2026 events both showed the same failure mode - the science existed, but warning either was not yet operational or could not outrun a flood that travelled in minutes. Design warning systems for the fastest plausible hazard, not the average one.

7.3 Carrying Capacity and Regulation of Development

  • Carrying-capacity assessment for hill towns and tourist destinations - the maximum population, traffic, construction and waste load a site's water, land, slope and sewerage can sustain.
  • Do not breach the tipping point: development should stay within assessed capacity; beyond a threshold, slope, aquifer and drainage systems change state irreversibly (Joshimath is the cautionary example).
  • Strict enforcement of building codes and byelaws, including height limits, setbacks from rivers, and prohibition of construction on concave banks, old landslide debris and paleo-channels.
  • Environmental Impact Assessment before any large project, with cumulative basin-level assessment rather than project-by-project clearance, and honest appraisal of muck disposal and blasting.
  • Zoning: declare and enforce no-construction zones in identified high-hazard tracts.
[Verification Required] The rough note records that "after SC recommending, Centre has set up an expert panel regarding carrying-capacity assessment based on tourism". It is correct that the carrying capacity of Himalayan towns came before the Supreme Court of India in 2023 and that the Union Environment Ministry moved to have Himalayan States/UTs assess carrying capacity through an expert mechanism. The exact composition, mandate and current status of that panel should be confirmed from the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change before being quoted with specifics.

[Correction] The rough note writes "Jan bhagyadari". The intended term is Jan Bhagidari (people's participation / public participation). Also, the Sendai Framework is not merely "cooperation with other nations" - it is a global DRR framework adopted at Sendai, Japan, to which India is a signatory, and it sets seven global targets and four priorities for action.

7.5 Ecotourism and Sustainable Livelihoods

  • Promote ecotourism on genuinely sustainable lines - capped visitor numbers, waste-neutral operations, local ownership of benefits.
  • Decentralise tourism: village and homestay tourism spreads load away from a few overloaded hill towns, retains income locally, and reduces the incentive for large concrete hotel blocks in fragile spots.
  • Diversify mountain livelihoods - horticulture, non-timber forest produce, medicinal plants under regulated harvest, handicrafts - so that communities are not forced into slope-degrading land use.
  • Cooperation with nature rather than conflict with it: work with the slope, the river and the forest instead of engineering against them. Bioengineering (vegetative slope stabilisation), catchment-area treatment and restoration of natural drainage are cheaper and more durable than repeated hard-engineering repairs.

7.6 Model Answer Skeleton

Question: Discuss the steps required to reduce disaster risk in the Himalayan belt. (250 words / 15 marks)

Intro: Risk = Hazard x Exposure x Vulnerability / Capacity. Hazard cannot be removed in an active orogen, so intervention must target exposure, vulnerability and capacity.

Body - five heads:

  1. Know the risk: landslide susceptibility zonation, active-fault and glacial-lake inventories, remote sensing, drones, Doppler radar, telemetry, real-time and transboundary data sharing, last-mile alerts.
  2. Regulate development: carrying-capacity assessment for hill towns, no-build zones, riverbank setbacks, strict building codes, cumulative basin-level EIA, controlled blasting and muck disposal.
  3. Build resilient: seismic detailing, retrofitting of lifeline structures, revival of Kath-Kuni / Koti Banal / Dhajji Dewari principles using deodar and local stone.
  4. Govern and cooperate: Sendai Framework alignment, CDRI, ICIMOD-led regional cooperation, NDMA guidelines, upstream-downstream hydrological data exchange.
  5. Empower people: Jan Bhagidari, community capacity building, mock drills, ecotourism and decentralised village tourism, bioengineering and catchment treatment.

Conclusion: Development in the Himalaya must be within carrying capacity and with nature; the goal is resilience, not resistance.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • The Himalaya has two syntaxial bends: western at Nanga Parbat (Indus gorge), eastern at Namcha Barwa (Tsangpo/Dihang gorge).
  • Namcha Barwa (7,782 m) lies in Tibet, north of Arunachal Pradesh - not in Arunachal Pradesh.
  • Purvanchal north to south: Patkai Bum - Naga Hills - Manipur Hills - Mizo (Lushai) Hills. The Barail Range runs across and divides the Brahmaputra and Barak valleys.
  • Phawngpui ("Blue Mountain"), 2,157 m - highest peak of Mizoram and of the Lushai Hills; a national park.
  • Saramati - highest peak of Nagaland, on the India-Myanmar border.
  • Karbi Anglong (Mikir) and North Cachar Hills are Peninsular outliers, not Purvanchal folds. Meghalaya plateau is likewise a Gondwana block.
  • Meitei - Imphal Valley; Kuki and Naga communities - surrounding hills.
  • Indian Himalayan Region = 13 States/UTs = 11 States + 2 UTs (J&K and Ladakh); Assam and West Bengal enter only through their hill districts.
  • HKH spans 8 countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, China, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan.
  • HKH = "Third Pole" (largest ice mass outside the poles) and "Water Tower of Asia" (10 major river systems).
  • ICIMOD = International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development; established 1983; headquarters at Khumaltar, Lalitpur, Kathmandu Valley, Nepal.
  • Thrust systems: MHT (basal detachment), MCT, MBT, MFT/HFT.
  • Gorkha earthquake: 25 April 2015, Mw 7.8, epicentre Barpak, Gorkha district, Nepal.
  • Chamoli disaster: 7 February 2021 - rock-and-ice avalanche from the Ronti area; destroyed Rishiganga project, damaged Tapovan Vishnugad.
  • South Lhonak GLOF: 3-4 October 2023, North Sikkim; destroyed the 1,200 MW Teesta-III dam at Chungthang.
  • Bhotekoshi-Trishuli disaster: 26 August 2026, glacier collapse on Langtang Lirung, Rasuwa district, Nepal.
  • Medog / Motuo project - Yarlung Tsangpo Great Bend, Medog County, Tibet; construction began 19 July 2025; planned 60 GW.
  • Sendai Framework 2015-2030 - adopted at Sendai, Japan, March 2015; replaced the Hyogo Framework for Action (2005-2015).
  • CDRI - Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure; launched by India, September 2019; secretariat New Delhi.
  • Clausius-Clapeyron: about 7% more atmospheric moisture per 1°C of warming.

Mains Points

  • Syntaxes as risk hotspots: rapid uplift coupled with rapid incision makes both syntaxial bends the most unstable segments of the orogen - and both are now sites of major infrastructure interest.
  • Purvanchal as a transition zone: physically Himalayan in origin, but ecologically Indo-Burman and culturally distinct; explains the North-East's biodiversity, its hill-valley social geography and its Sixth Schedule governance.
  • Region-building: the IHR concept converts a physiographic belt into a planning unit, allowing mountain-specific policy - but it also merges non-Himalayan blocks such as Meghalaya, so use the term precisely.
  • Transboundary commons: the HKH is a shared cryosphere and shared river system; ICIMOD is the working model of apolitical regional science cooperation.
  • Climate as a multiplier, not a new hazard: two pathways - cryospheric destabilisation and enhanced atmospheric moisture - both converge on the same cascade.
  • Development-disaster feedback: tourism and hydropower create the exposure that turns hazards into catastrophes, and the resulting damage then demands more construction. Carrying capacity breaks this loop.
  • Warning design: systems tuned to slow, seasonal hazards fail against minute-scale cascades. Design for the fastest credible scenario.
  • Balance: the Himalaya delivers water, energy, food, climate regulation and strategic depth; the policy question is not development versus conservation but development within ecological limits.

30-Second Revision

The Himalayan arc turns sharply south at two syntaxes - Nanga Parbat in the west and Namcha Barwa in the east. From the eastern bend the belt continues into India as Purvanchal: Patkai Bum, Naga, Manipur and Mizo Hills, with the Barail dividing the Brahmaputra and Barak valleys. Administratively this is part of the Indian Himalayan Region of 13 States and UTs, and globally part of the eight-country Hindu Kush Himalaya - the "Third Pole" and "Water Tower of Asia", coordinated through ICIMOD, Lalitpur, Nepal. The range blocks Siberian cold, forces monsoon rainfall, admits western disturbances, and provides strategic depth, water, hydropower, biodiversity and horticulture. But it is a young, fractured, steep and seismically active belt threaded by the MHT, MCT, MBT and HFT, where climate change acts as a multiplier and human activity as an amplifier, producing cascading disasters - Chamoli 2021, Joshimath 2023, South Lhonak-Teesta III 2023 and Langtang Lirung 2026. The answer is not to abandon development but to bound it: hazard mapping and early warning, carrying-capacity limits, enforced building codes, resilient and vernacular construction, Sendai and CDRI alignment, ICIMOD-level regional data sharing, and Jan Bhagidari with decentralised ecotourism.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - General Studies Paper I

Bring out the causes for more frequent occurrence of landslides in the Himalayas than in the Western Ghats.

Answer direction: The Himalaya is a young, actively converging orogen with sheared and fractured rock, very steep slopes, deep river incision, high seismicity along the MHT/MCT/MBT/HFT, and glacial and snowmelt processes - none of which apply to the same degree in the older, more stable, deeply weathered Western Ghats. Add human amplifiers: road widening, tunnelling, blasting, hydropower construction and deforestation. Directly maps to Section 6 of these notes.

UPSC CSE Mains 2016 - General Studies Paper I

"The Himalayas are highly prone to landslides." Discuss the causes and suggest suitable measures of mitigation.

Answer direction: Split causes into natural (tectonic, lithological, slope, hydrological, cryospheric) and anthropogenic (construction, blasting, deforestation, drainage blockage). Mitigation should follow the five tiers in Section 7: mapping and warning, regulation and carrying capacity, resilient construction, institutional cooperation, and community capacity building.

UPSC CSE Mains 2018 - General Studies Paper III

Describe various measures taken in India for Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) before and after signing the "Sendai Framework for DRR (2015-2030)". How is this framework different from the "Hyogo Framework for Action, 2005"?

Answer direction: Before Sendai - Disaster Management Act 2005, NDMA/NDRF, National Policy 2009, hazard-specific guidelines. After Sendai - National Disaster Management Plan 2016 (the first national plan aligned to Sendai), CDRI (2019), expanded early-warning networks and Build Back Better in recovery. Difference from Hyogo: Sendai shifts the focus from managing disasters to managing risk, adds seven measurable global targets, emphasises understanding risk and risk governance, and gives a stronger role to the private sector and to resilient infrastructure. Directly supports Section 7.4.

UPSC CSE Mains 2021 - General Studies Paper III

Describe the various causes and the effects of landslides. Mention the important components of the National Landslide Risk Management Strategy.

Answer direction: Causes and effects as above; then list the strategy's components - landslide hazard zonation and mapping, monitoring and early warning, awareness programmes, capacity building and training, preparation of mountain zone regulations and policies, stabilisation and mitigation of existing landslides. The Himalayan case files in Section 6.4 provide the illustrations.

UPSC CSE Mains 2023 - General Studies Paper III

Dam failures are always catastrophic, especially on the downstream side, resulting in colossal loss of life and property. Analyse the various causes of dam failures. Give two examples of large dam failures.

Answer direction: Causes - overtopping from extreme inflow, foundation and seepage failure, structural and design defects, seismic loading, siting in high-energy paraglacial gorges, sediment and debris loading, and operational failure such as inability to open gates in time. Himalayan examples from these notes: the Teesta-III dam at Chungthang destroyed by the South Lhonak GLOF (October 2023) and the Rishiganga project destroyed with severe damage to Tapovan Vishnugad in the Chamoli disaster (February 2021). Emphasise the cascade point - the natural event destroyed the dam, and the dam's failure then multiplied downstream destruction.
