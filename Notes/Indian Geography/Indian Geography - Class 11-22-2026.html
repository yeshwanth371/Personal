1. Tropical Thorn Forests (below 70 cm)

One line: where rainfall is too low for a closed canopy, deciduous forest thins out into an open, thorny, small-leaved scrub of xerophytes - plants built to save water.

1.1 Where They Grow

  • They lie next to the dry deciduous belt, on its drier side (8.2 of the 21 November note).
  • Main areas: Rajasthan and Gujarat, south-west Punjab and Haryana, and the drier parts of Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. Smaller patches grow in the rain-shadow interior of the Deccan (Marathwada, north Karnataka, Rayalaseema) (Map 1).
[Correction] The notes say thorn forests are "generally found in TG and RJ". The pairing that fits thorn forests is Gujarat (GJ) and Rajasthan (RJ); "TG" (Telangana) is probably a slip. Telangana does have thorny scrub, but only in rain-shadow patches. NCERT lists thorn forests in the semi-arid areas of south-west Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh.
Rainfall threshold: the class uses below 70 cm. NCERT Class XI uses less than 50 cm, and some texts put the dry deciduous-thorn boundary at about 75 cm. Read the threshold the way the question frames it: thorn forests are always the driest forest type, below dry deciduous.

Such drought-adapted plants are called xerophytes (Greek xeros = dry).

1.3 Species

  • Khejri (Prosopis cineraria), the state tree of Rajasthan†. It fixes nitrogen and provides fodder (leaves), food (sangri pods) and fuel, and is sacred to the Bishnois. It is called the "lifeline of the Thar".
  • Babul (Vachellia/Acacia nilotica), ber (Ziziphus), khair (Senegalia catechu), wild date palm, euphorbias and cacti; [Additional] rohida (Tecomella undulata) and phog (Calligonum) on sand dunes†.
[Correction] The notes list cactus among the native thorn-forest plants. True cacti (for example Opuntia, prickly pear) are American plants introduced into India. The native cactus-like succulents of Indian thorn forests are euphorbias (Euphorbia). NCERT itself lists "acacias, palms, euphorbias and cacti", so "cactus" is accepted in answers, but euphorbia is the native example.

1.4 Vilayati Keekar (Prosopis juliflora) and Green Methanol [Current Affairs]

  • Prosopis juliflora is a Mexican-origin shrub, called vilayati keekar (north India), gando baval (Gujarat) and velikathan (Tamil Nadu). It was introduced in the 1920s to green Delhi and spread widely in Gujarat from 1961 to check desertification near the Rann of Kutch.
  • It is now ranked among the world's top 100 invasive species. It has crowded out native grasses in the Banni grasslands of Kutch, a pastoral landscape of the Maldhari herders (Map 1).
  • [Current Affairs] (May 2026): India's first green-methanol plant is being built at the Deendayal Port Authority, Kandla (Kutch), using P. juliflora as feedstock. The process is biomass gasification to syngas (H2 + CO + CO2), then methanol synthesis. Thermax is executing the project with gasifier technology from Ankur Scientific. The plant is a 5 tonnes/day demonstration unit; commercial plants would need 100-500 t/day. The methanol is meant as a cleaner marine fuel under the International Maritime Organization's net-zero-by-2050 goal and India's green-ports push.
  • Why it matters: it removes an invasive plant, helps restore grassland and produces low-carbon fuel. The risks are cost, the supply of biomass at scale, and the plant still running on grid power. Operational status after May 2026 was not confirmed [Verification Required].
Exam trap - two Prosopis: Khejri = Prosopis cineraria (native, sacred, Rajasthan's state tree). Vilayati keekar = Prosopis juliflora (exotic, invasive, used as green-methanol feedstock). Same genus, opposite reputations.

2. Community Conservation: Bishnoi, Chipko and Appiko

Class point: conservation works when local communities are engaged. People who depend on a forest for fodder, fuel, water and faith are its most reliable protectors. Each movement below was led by such users, and women played a large part.

2.1 The Bishnoi Sacrifice at Khejarli (1730)

  • Who: the Bishnois, a community in the Marwar (Jodhpur) region. They follow the 29 principles ("bis-noi" = twenty-nine) of Guru Jambheshwar (Jambhoji), a 15th-century teacher; several principles forbid felling green trees and killing animals.
  • What: in September 1730, Maharaja Abhai (Abhay) Singh of Marwar sent men under his minister Giridhar Bhandari to cut khejri trees at Khejarli village (about 25 km south-east of Jodhpur). The wood was needed for a new palace; some accounts say it was to burn lime.
  • Amrita Devi Bishnoi refused, saying "A chopped head is cheaper than a felled tree", and hugged a tree. She and her three daughters were beheaded. Bishnois from about 83-84 villages then volunteered one by one. 363 Bishnois were killed before the king stopped the felling and banned tree cutting and hunting in Bishnoi villages.
  • Legacy: the Amrita Devi Bishnoi Wildlife Protection Award (Government of India); 11 September was declared National Forest Martyrs Day in 2013; an annual fair is held at Khejarli. The event is seen as the forerunner of Chipko.

2.2 The Chipko Movement (Uttarakhand, 1973 onwards)

  • Background: after 1962, new roads in the Garhwal hills opened the forests to contractors from the plains, while villagers were denied even a few trees. The Alaknanda floods of July 1970 linked deforestation to disaster in people's minds. Chandi Prasad Bhatt's cooperative, the Dasholi Gram Swarajya Sangh/Mandal (Gopeshwar, 1964), led the opposition.
  • First action - Mandal, April 1973: ash trees refused to the cooperative were allotted to the Symonds/Simon sports-goods company of Allahabad. Villagers went into the forest to embrace the trees ("chipko" = to hug or cling), and the contractors withdrew.
  • Reni, 26 March 1974: about 2,450 trees were auctioned in the Reni forest above the Alaknanda (Rishiganga-Dhauliganga confluence, near Joshimath). The men were away collecting compensation. Gaura Devi, head of the Mahila Mangal Dal, led some 27 women and girls to stand between the axes and the forest, and the loggers left.
  • Results: a committee on the Reni forests led to a ban on commercial felling in the upper Alaknanda catchment. In 1980 a 15-year ban on felling green trees in the Himalayan forests of Uttar Pradesh followed†. Sunderlal Bahuguna carried the message nationally through padyatras and fasts. The movement received the Right Livelihood Award in 1987.
  • Chipko is also read as an eco-feminist movement, because hill women gathered the fuel, fodder and water.
[Correction] "Ghipko movement - Reni village - Chamoli district" should read Chipko. The location is correct: Reni is in Chamoli district, Uttarakhand (then Uttar Pradesh). Reni (26 March 1974, Gaura Devi) was the most famous action, but the first Chipko action was at Mandal, near Gopeshwar, in April 1973.

2.3 [Additional] Appiko Movement (Karnataka, 1983)

Chipko's southern version. It began in September 1983 at Salkani village near Sirsi (Uttara Kannada), led by Panduranga Hegde, against felling in the Western Ghats. "Appiko" means "hug" in Kannada†. Its slogan was save, grow and use rationally.

Mains frame (community engagement): these movements show that local people (i) protect what they depend on, (ii) hold traditional ecological knowledge, and (iii) can enforce rules more cheaply than guards. Later policy built on this through Joint Forest Management (1990), the Forest Rights Act 2006, community reserves and the community-based design of MISHTI and the Aravalli Green Wall (5).

3. Montane (Mountain) Vegetation

Principle: montane vegetation depends on physiography (altitude), not just rainfall. Temperature falls by about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m (the normal lapse rate). Climbing a high mountain therefore passes through belts like those met on a journey from the tropics to the poles: tropical - subtropical - temperate - coniferous (taiga-like) - alpine (tundra-like).

Chir pine is an evergreen conifer forming subtropical pine forests at roughly 1,000-1,800 m (NCERT: 1,500-1,750 m). It is not deciduous. The point about oily (resinous) needles and forest fires is correct.
[Correction] The notes call the oak belt at about 2,000 m "temperate deciduous". Himalayan oaks (banj, moru, kharsu) are mostly evergreen broad-leaved trees, so NCERT calls this belt wet temperate forest (evergreen broad-leaf oak and chestnut, 1,000-2,000 m). 3.2 Alpine Pastures and Transhumance

  • Transhumance is the seasonal movement of herders and livestock: up to the alpine pastures in summer, down to the valleys and foothills in winter.
  • Gujjar and Bakarwal (Jammu and Kashmir), Gaddi (Himachal Pradesh) and Bhotia (Uttarakhand) are the classic examples. NCERT names the same groups using alpine pastures.
  • Pressure: overgrazing and climate change threaten the bugyals, and some (for example Bedni-Ali) have been closed to camping by court orders†.

3.3 Southern Montane Forests and the Sholas

  • The Western Ghats, Vindhyas and Nilgiris are lower than the Himalaya and nearer the equator. Their higher reaches carry temperate vegetation and their lower slopes subtropical vegetation.
  • The temperate forests of the Nilgiri, Anaimalai and Palani hills are called sholas. These are stunted evergreen trees in sheltered valleys, set in rolling grassland (the shola-grassland mosaic), at roughly 1,500-2,600 m. Species include magnolia, laurel, rhododendron and planted cinchona and wattle.
  • Landmarks: Anamudi (2,695 m, Anaimalai), the highest peak of peninsular India, and Doddabetta (2,637 m, Nilgiris)†.
[Correction] The notes call the sholas "temperate sub-tropical forests - Western Ghats near Anaimudi, Doodbetta peak". Sholas are southern montane wet temperate forests of the Nilgiri, Anaimalai and Palani hills; they are not subtropical. The correct spellings are Anamudi (Anaimalai hills, Kerala) and Doddabetta (Nilgiris, Tamil Nadu).
Also in the notes: "India is quite popular for its richness in flora". [Additional] India is one of the 17 megadiverse countries. It has about 8% of recorded species on about 2.4% of the world's land, and four of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots: Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka and Sundaland (Nicobar)†.

4. Forest Cover of India: FSI and ISFR 2023

4.1 Who Measures It

  • Forest Survey of India (FSI), Dehradun, is an organisation under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC). It was set up in 1981, succeeding the Pre-Investment Survey of Forest Resources (1965, an FAO/UNDP project).
  • FSI publishes the India State of Forest Report (ISFR) every two years since 1987. It combines remote sensing with field data from the National Forest Inventory. ISFR 2023 is the 18th report; it was released on 21 December 2024 and used IRS Resourcesat LISS-III imagery (23.5 m resolution, 1:50,000 scale; mostly October-December 2021).
[Correction] The notes say "since 1987 remote sensing satellite methods". More precisely, the biennial ISFR series began in 1987; FSI itself dates from 1981. The definition in the notes (">1 hectare, >10% green canopy") should read at least 1 hectare with tree canopy density of at least 10%, irrespective of ownership, legal status or land use. Forest cover therefore includes orchards, bamboo, palms and plantations.

5. Policy, Laws and Afforestation Programmes

5.1 The 33% Goal

The National Forest Policy, 1988 (which replaced the 1952 policy) aims to bring one-third (33%) of India's geographical area under forest or tree cover, and two-thirds in the hills. With forest and tree cover at 25.17% (ISFR 2023), India is about 8 percentage points short. Since natural forest cannot expand quickly, the gap has to be closed mainly by tree cover outside forests: agroforestry, urban and social forestry, and restoring degraded open forest and scrub.

5.3 Urban Forestry and the Miyawaki Method

  • The method was developed by the Japanese botanist Akira Miyawaki (1928-2021)†. It uses only native species of the site's potential natural vegetation, planted very densely (about 3-5 saplings per m2†) in several layers (shrub, sub-tree, tree, canopy) on prepared soil enriched with organic matter.
  • Plants are watered and weeded for the first 2-3 years; after that the forest becomes self-sustaining. The method is said to grow forests about 10 times faster and 30 times denser than conventional planting†, which makes it popular for urban forests on small plots. [Additional] The government's Nagar Van Yojana (2020) funds city forests†.
  • Caution: Miyawaki plots are small, costly and water-intensive. They complement, but cannot replace, the protection of natural forests.
[Correction] "Akira miyawki" should read Akira Miyawaki.
Mains frame - way forward (from the notes): conservation of existing forests + reforestation (degraded forest land) + afforestation (new land) + community participation, with native species, monitoring of plantation survival rather than numbers planted, convergence of schemes (GIM, CAMPA, MGNREGS) and incentives for agroforestry. Use the ISFR quality data (VDF and MDF degrading to OF) to argue that quality matters as much as area.

6. Module 5: Indian Soils

6.1 Why Soils, Before Agriculture

The class introduced soil as the base for agriculture: sustainable agriculture is planned by agro-climatic conditions - soil, temperature and rainfall. [Additional] The Planning Commission divided India into 15 agro-climatic regions (1989) on this logic†.

India's soils are very diverse because of its varied physiography, rainfall and climate and parent rocks. [Additional] The standard factors of soil formation are parent material, climate, relief, organisms (vegetation and microbes) and time. Climate is the most important control at the large scale, and parent rock at the local scale (for example basalt gives black soil).

Class phrase check: "black soil - volcanic, iron, basaltic" is correct in essence. Black soil forms from the weathering of basalt (fissure volcanic rock; UPSC 2021). Its dark colour comes mainly from titaniferous magnetite and iron compounds, not from humus. "Saline - least fertile" is a fair summary; arid and laterite soils are also low in fertility.

6.3 Alluvial Soils

  • Formation: deposited by rivers in lowlands - the Indus-Ganga plain, the Brahmaputra valley, the eastern deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri), the Gujarat plains and the Narmada-Tapi valleys.
  • Texture: a mix of sand, silt and clay. It is generally loamy, holds both air and water, and is easy to plough. Texture becomes finer downstream: sandier in the upper plains and in Punjab, clayey in the lower Ganga and the deltas.
  • Two forms in the doabs: Bhangar is old alluvium on higher terraces above flood level, often with kankar (lime nodules) and less fertile. Khadar is new alluvium on the floodplains, renewed by floods every year, finer and more fertile (Figure 4). North of them, along the foothills, lie the Bhabar (coarse pebbles where streams sink) and the Terai (marshy belt where they re-emerge).
  • It is a young, transported soil carried by long Himalayan rivers. Its profile is poorly developed (few distinct horizons), but it is renewed with fresh minerals.
  • Crops: rice, wheat, sugarcane, pulses, oilseeds and jute. It underpins the Green Revolution belt.
[Correction] The notes call alluvial soil "nutrient rich" with "a variety of minerals". It is fertile and mineral-rich, but it is rich mainly in potash and lime and generally poor in nitrogen, humus (organic matter) and phosphorus (NCERT). This is why the Green Revolution plains depend on N and P fertilisers. In dry areas over-irrigation turns parts of it saline or alkaline (UPSC 2018).

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Thorn forests: driest forest type (class <70 cm; NCERT <50 cm); Rajasthan, Gujarat, SW Punjab-Haryana, dry MP-UP, Deccan rain shadow; xerophytes - small waxy leaves, thorns, deep roots.
  • Khejri = Prosopis cineraria (state tree of Rajasthan, sacred to Bishnois); vilayati keekar = P. juliflora (invasive; Banni grasslands; feedstock of India's first green-methanol plant at Kandla, 2026).
  • Khejarli 1730: Amrita Devi Bishnoi + 363 Bishnois; Maharaja Abhai Singh of Marwar; 11 September = National Forest Martyrs Day.
  • Chipko: first action Mandal 1973 (Chandi Prasad Bhatt, DGSM, Gopeshwar); Reni, Chamoli, 26 March 1974 (Gaura Devi); Sunderlal Bahuguna; Right Livelihood Award 1987. Appiko: Salkani, Uttara Kannada, 1983.
  • Himalayan sequence: sal (foothills) - chir pine / oak (1,000-2,000 m) - deodar, fir, spruce (2,000-3,000 m) - birch, juniper, rhododendron + bugyal/marg (3,000-4,000 m) - mosses and lichens.
  • Sholas: Nilgiri, Anaimalai, Palani hills; Anamudi 2,695 m (highest in the Peninsula), Doddabetta 2,637 m. Transhumance: Gujjar-Bakarwal (J&K), Gaddi (HP), Bhotia (UK).
  • FSI: Dehradun, 1981, MoEFCC; ISFR biennial since 1987; ISFR 2023 = 18th (released 21 Dec 2024), still the latest.
  • Forest cover = at least 1 ha and canopy at least 10%, regardless of ownership. VDF 70% and above; MDF 40-70%; OF 10-40%; scrub below 10% (not forest).
  • ISFR 2023: forest 21.76% (7,15,343 sq km); tree 3.41%; total 25.17%; VDF 3.12%, MDF 9.36% > OF 9.28%; scrub 1.33%.
  • Largest forest cover: MP > Arunachal > Chhattisgarh; forest + tree: MP > Arunachal > Maharashtra; % cover: Lakshadweep > Mizoram > A&N.
  • GFRA 2025 (FAO): India 9th in forest area (72.7 Mha; was 10th), 3rd in net annual gain; top 8: Russia, Brazil, Canada, USA, China, DRC, Australia, Indonesia.
  • Policy goal: 33% (NFP 1988); GIM Feb 2014 (NAPCC); CAF Act 2016 (90:10); MISHTI 5 June 2023 (540 sq km, 9 states + 4 UTs); Ek Ped Maa Ke Naam 5 June 2024; Aravalli Green Wall (5 km buffer, 4 states, 29 districts; plan launched 21 May 2025).
  • ICAR: 8 soil groups; alluvial ~40-43% (largest) > red and yellow ~18.5% > black ~15% > forest ~8.7% > arid ~4.4% > laterite ~3.7%.
  • Alluvial soil: rich in potash and lime, poor in N, P and humus; Khadar new, Bhangar old (kankar).

Mains Points

  • Vegetation as an index of climate: the sequence from evergreen to thorn tracks falling rainfall (100 cm and 70 cm thresholds); montane belts track falling temperature with altitude. Both are cause-and-effect chains worth drawing in answers (Figure 1).
  • Community conservation: Bishnoi, Chipko and Appiko show that livelihood, faith and women's participation make conservation durable. Policy followed with JFM (1990) and the FRA (2006). Use them for questions on people's movements and environmental ethics.
  • Invasive species as a resource: using P. juliflora for green methanol shows a circular-economy approach (restoring grassland, low-carbon fuel). Caveats: cost, scale and the need for green power.
  • Quantity v quality of forests: cover is rising slowly (+156 sq km in 2021-23), mostly outside recorded forests (plantations, agroforestry), while dense forest degrades to open forest and the Western Ghats and NE lose cover. A 33% target met by monocultures would not restore ecosystem services.
  • Closing the 33% gap: restore open and degraded forest, use agroforestry and trees outside forests, urban forests (Miyawaki, Nagar Van), landscape projects (Aravalli Green Wall, revised GIM) and CAMPA funds with survival audits and Gram Sabha involvement.
  • Soils and agriculture: parent rock and climate explain the soil map (basalt to black, granite to red, leaching to laterite, rivers to alluvium). Each soil's deficiencies (N and P in alluvium, N and P in black soils, lime in laterite) call for soil-specific nutrient management (Soil Health Cards†) and the management of salinity in canal-irrigated areas.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Khejri v vilayati keekar - both Prosopis; the first is native and sacred, the second invasive.
  • Chipko started at Mandal (1973), not Reni; Reni (1974, Gaura Devi) is the famous women-led action. Chamoli district, then UP.
  • Bugyal = Uttarakhand; marg = Kashmir.
  • Chir pine is evergreen and coniferous (subtropical), not deciduous; Himalayan oaks are mostly evergreen.
  • Teak is not a Himalayan foothill native; sal, shisham and khair are.
  • Sholas are in the Nilgiri, Anaimalai and Palani hills, not in the Himalaya (a repeated CDS and UPSC trap).
  • Forest cover v recorded forest area: cover is a satellite measurement of canopy; recorded area is legal status. About a third of recorded forest has no forest cover.
  • Scrub is not forest cover; tree cover is not forest cover (patches smaller than 1 ha outside recorded forests).
  • ISFR 2023: MDF slightly exceeds OF (the reverse of 2021).
  • Largest forest + tree cover: 3rd is Maharashtra; largest forest cover: 3rd is Chhattisgarh.
  • Global rank comes from FAO GFRA 2025 (India 9th), not from ISFR; Australia is 7th.
  • Dates: GIM 2014 (not 2015), CAF Act 2016 (not 2015), Aravalli Green Wall plan 2025 (not 2026); FCA 1980 renamed in 2023.
  • Alluvial soil is fertile but poor in nitrogen and humus; Khadar = new, Bhangar = old (kankar in Bhangar).
  • Black soil colour comes from titaniferous magnetite, not humus; laterite comes from leaching, not from volcanic rock.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2021 - General Studies Paper I

The black cotton soil of India has been formed due to the weathering of

  1. brown forest soil
  2. fissure volcanic rock
  3. granite and schist
  4. shale and limestone

Answer: (b) fissure volcanic rock. Regur forms from the weathering of Deccan Trap basalt, which erupted through fissures. Granite and gneiss give red soils (6.2).

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 - General Studies Paper I

Which of the following statements regarding laterite soils of India are correct?
1. They are generally red in colour.
2. They are rich in nitrogen and potash.
3. They are well-developed in Rajasthan and UP.
4. Tapioca and cashew nuts grow well on these soils.
Select the correct answer using the codes given below.

  1. 1, 2 and 3
  2. 2, 3 and 4
  3. 1 and 4
  4. 2 and 3 only

Answer: (c) 1 and 4. Leaching removes bases (so the soil is poor in N and K) and leaves iron oxides (red colour). Laterite needs heavy rain, so it is found on the Ghats, not in dry Rajasthan or alluvial UP (6.2, Map 2).

3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2018 - General Studies Paper I

With reference to agricultural soils, consider the following statements:
1. A high content of organic matter in soil drastically reduces its water holding capacity.
2. Soil does not play any role in the sulphur cycle.
3. Irrigation over a period of time can contribute to the salinization of some agricultural lands.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: (b) 3 only. Humus increases water-holding capacity; soil is a major sulphur reservoir. Over-irrigation with poor drainage creates the saline and alkaline (usar) patches of the alluvial plains (6.3).

4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019 - General Studies Paper I

Consider the following statements:
1. As per law, the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Management and Planning Authority exists at both National and State levels.
2. People's participation is mandatory in the compensatory afforestation programmes carried out under the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2

Answer: (a) 1 only. The CAF Act 2016 creates national and state funds and authorities (90% of money to the states), but it does not make people's participation mandatory, which is a common criticism (5.2).
