1. Mountain Passes: Meaning and General Importance

A mountain pass is a comparatively low, traversable gap or saddle across a mountain range or ridge. It commonly develops along structurally weak rock, a river headwater, glacial breach or saddle between peaks. It is a route through or across high relief, not simply any low-lying mountain land.

[Correction] The rough note defines a pass as a "low lying region in mountains". More precisely, it is a low point relative to the adjoining ridge or peaks that permits movement between valleys or basins. Many Himalayan passes remain extremely high in absolute elevation.

Passes matter because they turn a continuous mountain barrier into a set of usable corridors. Roads, tracks and tunnels converge on these gaps, making them focal points of transport and communication. Before modern engineering, caravans used them to exchange salt, wool, horses and grain; pilgrims, pastoral communities and religious ideas followed the same routes.

Their strategic importance follows directly from this geography. A pass can provide access to a frontier valley, airfield or forward military post, while control of the approach road and adjacent heights can determine whether the corridor remains usable. Many passes close after heavy snow, avalanches or landslides, so tunnels and alternative alignments reduce seasonal isolation.

[Additional] The best-known invasion corridors into the subcontinent, Khyber and Bolan, lie west of the Indian Himalaya in present-day Pakistan/Afghanistan. The very high Himalayan passes discussed here were more important for local trade, pilgrimage, cultural exchange and modern frontier logistics than for large invading armies.

Development also has a cost. Slope cutting, quarrying, traffic and unregulated tourism can destabilise fragile terrain and fragment habitats. Climate warming further changes snow cover, permafrost and glacial hazards. Thus, the value of a pass must always be studied together with its environmental vulnerability.

Low saddle in a high barrier→movement converges→road and tunnel corridor→trade, pilgrimage and military logistics→high strategic value

2. Major Himalayan Passes from West to East

[Exam caution] Assigning every pass neatly to "Trans-Himalaya / Great Himalaya / Lesser Himalaya" can mislead because regional ranges curve, merge and use different local names. The table therefore states the specific range and corridor. "La" in Tibetan and related Himalayan usage means pass; avoid writing "Zoji La Pass", which repeats the word.

2.1 Ladakh and the Kashmir-Ladakh Corridor

Karakoram Pass

Karakoram Range | about 5,540 m | historic Leh-Yarkand route

The pass lies in the eastern Karakoram near the India-China boundary in the Ladakh sector. It was one of the highest crossings used by old caravans between Ladakh and Xinjiang. Its wider strategic setting includes the Depsang Plains and Daulat Beg Oldi (DBO), making the northern approach important for surveillance and logistics.

Khardung La

Ladakh Range | about 5,359 m | Leh-Nubra-Shyok corridor

North of Leh, Khardung La opens the route to the Nubra and Shyok valleys. This gives it tourism value and strategic importance for movement toward northern Ladakh and the Siachen base sector. It must not be confused with the Karakoram Pass, which lies farther north-east.

Zoji La

Great Himalayan axis | about 3,528 m | Sonamarg-Dras-Kargil on NH-1

Zoji La connects the Kashmir Valley with Dras, Kargil and Ladakh. It is the principal road gateway between Kashmir and Ladakh and therefore a critical civilian and defence lifeline. Heavy snowfall historically closed the route for long periods; modern tunnels seek to provide more reliable all-weather access.

2.2 Pir Panjal and Himachal Corridors

Banihal Pass

Pir Panjal Range | about 2,832 m | Jammu-Kashmir Valley corridor

Banihal is the historic crossing between the Jammu region and the Kashmir Valley. Modern road and railway routes now tunnel beneath the Pir Panjal, reducing gradients and exposure to snow. This corridor is central to year-round connectivity and national integration.

Rohtang Pass

Eastern Pir Panjal | about 3,978 m | Kullu-Manali to Lahaul

Rohtang crosses from the Kullu Valley into Lahaul. Routes continue from Lahaul toward Spiti and Ladakh, giving the pass tourism, horticultural and strategic value. The Atal Tunnel bypasses the snowbound crest and greatly improves all-season access.

2.3 Central and Eastern Himalayan Corridors

Lipulekh Pass

Kumaon Himalaya | about 5,334 m | Pithoragarh-Tibet route

Lipulekh lies on the route from the Kali valley toward Tibet and is one of India's official Kailash-Manasarovar Yatra routes. It also carried historic trans-Himalayan trade. The nearby Kalapani-Limpiyadhura-Lipulekh sector is disputed between India and Nepal, adding diplomatic and strategic importance.

Nathu La

Dongkya Range | about 4,310 m | Sikkim-Chumbi Valley

Nathu La lies on an old branch of the Silk Route. Border trade reopened in 2006, and the pass offers strategic observation of the narrow Chumbi Valley. It is also an Indian Kailash-Manasarovar route with less trekking than Lipulekh, although the motor journey is longer.

Bomdi La and Sela

West Kameng and Tawang, Arunachal Pradesh | Assam-Dirang-Tawang corridor

Bomdi La lies on the approach from the Assam plains through Bhalukpong and Dirang. The route then crosses the much higher Sela before reaching Tawang. Together they support administration, Monpa cultural and monastery tourism, and defence logistics. The Sela Tunnel bypasses the snow-prone high pass and improves all-weather access.

[Correction] The Shaksgam Valley is not simply "northward of Karakoram Pass in Xinjiang province". It lies north of the main Karakoram watershed in territory ceded by Pakistan to China under the 1963 China-Pakistan boundary agreement; India rejects that agreement and claims the area as part of the erstwhile princely State of Jammu and Kashmir. China administers it within the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, not a province. Its importance to India arises from sovereignty, proximity to the Siachen-Karakoram sector and the wider China-Pakistan strategic linkage.
[Correction] Daulat Beg Oldi is an Advanced Landing Ground, not merely the "nearest Indian air field". It lies near the Depsang Plains, south of the Karakoram Pass, and supports logistics in India's northernmost frontier sector.
[Correction] Bomdi La and Sela provide access toward Tawang, not "Taksang Valley". Taktsang Gompa is a specific monastery near Tawang; it is not the name of the regional valley.
[Correction] Sela should not be memorised as a Lesser Himalayan pass merely because Bomdila lies farther south. It is a high pass of the western Arunachal Himalaya on the Dirang-Tawang corridor; region-specific range terminology is safer than forcing it into the three longitudinal Himalayan divisions.

2.5 Tunnels: Converting Seasonal Passes into All-Weather Corridors

Modern tunnels bypass high, snowbound crests by using lower and more protected alignments. Their main benefits are reduced travel time, gentler gradients, longer annual operating periods and more dependable civilian and military supplies.

  • Zoji La corridor: the approximately 13.14 km Zoji La Tunnel is under construction on NH-1 between the Sonamarg-Baltal and Minamarg/Dras sides. The separate 6.5 km Z-Morh/Sonamarg Tunnel, inaugurated in January 2025, provides all-weather access to Sonamarg. [Verification Required] for Zoji La construction progress and completion date after 04 September 2026.
  • Banihal corridor: distinguish the older 2.85 km Jawahar road tunnel, the approximately 8.45 km Banihal-Qazigund road tunnel opened in 2021, and the approximately 11.2 km Pir Panjal Railway Tunnel on the USBRL.
  • Rohtang corridor: the 9.02 km Atal Tunnel, opened on 03 October 2020, provides all-season Manali-Lahaul access and reduces the Manali-Keylong road distance by about 46 km.
  • Sela corridor: inaugurated on 09 March 2024, the project contains tunnels of approximately 980 m and 1,555 m with approach and link roads. It bypasses snow, sharp bends and the exposed pass crest on the route to Tawang.
[Additional] Strategic tunnels create resilience, but they do not eliminate Himalayan risk. Portals and approach roads remain vulnerable to avalanches, rockfall, cloudbursts, landslides and earthquakes. A complete answer must mention slope stabilisation, avalanche galleries, drainage, early warning, redundant routes and ecological carrying capacity.

3. The Great Northern Plains: Extent and Formation

The Great Northern Plains, also called the Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra Plains, form an extensive alluvial lowland south of the Himalaya and north of the Peninsular Plateau. The plain is about 2,400 km long and broadly 150-300 km wide, though width varies regionally. It is one of the world's largest and most intensively cultivated alluvial tracts.

[Correction] The plain does not merely extend "adjacent to the Shiwaliks till the Peninsular Plateau". Its northern margin lies along the Himalayan foothills, while its southern margin meets the Peninsular uplands along features such as the Aravalli, Vindhyan and Chota Nagpur margins. It includes the Indus plain in the west, Ganga plain in the centre and Brahmaputra plain in the east.

3.1 Tectonic-Depositional Origin

India-Eurasia collision and Himalayan uplift→flexural downwarping south of the mountains→elongated foreland basin/foredeep→Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra and tributaries deliver sediment→millions of years of alluvial filling→Great Northern Plains
[Correction] The rough note calls the depression a "foredeep or syncline". Foredeep/foreland basin is the more accurate term: loading by the rising Himalaya flexed the Indian plate downward. A syncline is a fold with beds dipping toward an axis; the entire Northern Plain should not be treated as one simple syncline.
[Additional] The alluvium is exceptionally thick in parts of the basin and conceals faults and basement ridges. The surface appears flat, but it contains levees, meander scars, oxbow lakes, abandoned channels, backswamps, river terraces, interfluves and active flood basins.

4. Physiographic Belts of the Plains

Moving south from the Shiwalik foothills, changes in gradient, sediment size, surface height and groundwater create four characteristic zones. These zones form a connected sequence rather than four isolated definitions.

4.1 Bhabar: the Coarse Recharge Belt

The Bhabar is a narrow belt, generally about 8-16 km wide, immediately south of the Shiwaliks. Mountain streams suddenly lose gradient and deposit boulders, cobbles and pebbles. Water readily infiltrates this porous material, so many surface streams appear to disappear.

Its dry surface and coarse soil limit conventional farming, but the belt is hydrologically valuable because it recharges foothill aquifers. It also supports forests, grazing and wildlife corridors. Roads, urban growth and unregulated boulder extraction can reduce infiltration and sever elephant and tiger movement routes.

4.2 Terai: the Wet Re-emergence Belt

Immediately south of Bhabar lies the Terai. Here finer sand, silt and clay slow underground flow, forcing groundwater and vanished streams back toward the surface. The result is a humid, marshy and historically forested belt with wetlands and a high water table.

Drainage and forest clearance converted large areas to productive rice, sugarcane, wheat and fodder cultivation. The same water abundance creates waterlogging and flood risk. The Terai also contains rich biodiversity, protected landscapes such as Dudhwa and Valmiki, and the cultural landscape of the Tharu people.

4.3 Bhangar: Older Alluvium

Bhangar forms raised terraces and interfluves above the active floodplain. It is older alluvium and commonly contains calcareous kankar nodules; some areas develop saline or alkaline usar soils. Because it is less frequently flooded, old settlements, roads and towns often favour this higher ground.

4.4 Khadar: Younger Floodplain

Khadar occupies active floodplains, natural levees and low terraces beside present rivers. Floods repeatedly renew its younger deposits of sand, silt and clay, making it fertile but hazardous. Farming benefits from moisture and fresh sediment, while settlements face inundation, erosion and channel migration.

4.5 Doabs: Land Between Rivers

A doab is an interfluve between two rivers, not a fifth age-class of alluvium. A single doab may contain older Bhangar in its centre and narrow Khadar belts beside both channels. Examples include the Ganga-Yamuna Doab and the Bist/Jalandhar Doab between the Beas and Sutlej. These areas became major corridors of canals, tubewells, roads, settlement and cereal-sugarcane cultivation.

[Correction] Terai is not "largely sand". It includes fine sand, silt, clay, organic-rich marsh deposits and a shallow water table. These fine sediments impede drainage compared with the coarse Bhabar.
[Correction] The Duars/Dooars are analogous foothill alluvial floodplains in north Bengal and Assam, named from "doors" or gateways to Bhutan. They are not a universal alternative name for the entire Terai belt.
[Correction] Artesian conditions occur locally where groundwater is confined under pressure; it is inaccurate to say simply that "artesian wells are found throughout Terai". The robust general statement is that the Terai has a shallow, abundant groundwater table.
[Correction] Khadar does not gradually become Bhangar merely because repeated deposition makes it too high to flood. Bhangar is an older alluvial geomorphic surface or terrace, separated from active floodplains through river incision, channel migration, tectonic movement and abandonment over long periods.
[Additional] Bhabar significance: (1) natural groundwater recharge; (2) sal and mixed foothill forests; (3) wildlife movement between Himalayan and Terai habitats; (4) sand, gravel and boulder resources, whose unregulated extraction damages channels; (5) dry, stable surfaces attractive to roads and urban expansion, creating a recharge-versus-development conflict.
[Additional] Terai significance: productive rice-sugarcane-wheat farming, shallow aquifers, wetlands and carbon-rich soils, protected landscapes such as Dudhwa and Valmiki, and the Tharu cultural region. Its Indo-Nepal and Indo-Bhutan setting enables trade and social interaction, but the term porous border is political rather than a physical property of the soil.

5. Geomorphology of the Great Plains

Although the Northern Plains look flat, they are continuously reshaped by flowing water. Near the foothills, rivers lose confinement and spread coarse sediment into alluvial fans; overlapping fans contribute to the piedmont surface of the Bhabar.

Farther downstream, a high and variable sediment load may split a river around temporary bars to create a braided channel, as seen in parts of the Brahmaputra and Kosi systems. Where the gradient is low and banks are more cohesive, lateral erosion produces meanders. Erosion dominates on the outer cut bank, deposition builds a point bar on the inner bend, and a neck cut-off can leave an oxbow lake.

During floods, relatively coarse sediment settles beside the channel to build natural levees, while finer clay reaches low, poorly drained backswamps. If an aggraded channel becomes unstable, the river may suddenly adopt a new course through avulsion; the historical shifting of the Kosi illustrates this dynamic behaviour.

Longer-term river incision leaves abandoned floodplain surfaces as river terraces, many of which form Bhangar uplands. Toward the Bay of Bengal, the lower Ganga-Brahmaputra-Meghna system changes into a distributary and tidal landscape of levees, mudflats, mangrove islands and deltaic channels.

6. Agriculture, Economy, Culture and Population

6.1 Agricultural Pattern

Agriculture changes from the relatively dry north-west to the humid east because rainfall, groundwater, soil drainage and flood frequency change across the plain.

  • Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh: deep alluvium, canals and tubewells support rice-wheat farming, sugarcane, cotton and dairy. High productivity is accompanied by groundwater decline, residue burning, nutrient imbalance and chemical stress.
  • Eastern Uttar Pradesh and Bihar: higher rainfall, fertile Khadar-Bhangar surfaces and shallow groundwater support rice, wheat, maize, pulses, sugarcane and vegetables. Wetlands favour fisheries and makhana, but small holdings, floods and waterlogging reduce income stability.
  • West Bengal: humid deltaic alluvium supports rice, jute, potato and fisheries, while tea grows in the northern foothills. Waterlogging, groundwater arsenic and cyclone-driven salinity affect particular areas.
  • Brahmaputra Valley and Duars: rice, mustard, jute and horticulture occupy alluvial land; tea prefers better-drained uplands. Floods, bank erosion and sand deposition repeatedly disturb fields and settlements.
  • Terai: shallow groundwater and moisture-rich soil favour rice, sugarcane, wheat and fodder, but drainage and wetland conservation are essential.

6.2 Why Population Density is High

Deep fertile alluvium+surface and groundwater+flat buildable land+long growing season+transport and market access+ancient urban-cultural continuity→dense rural and urban settlement

The plains support dense settlement because food, water, transport and relatively level construction land occur together. Density is not uniform: it falls in arid western tracts, ravines, waterlogged flood basins and erosion-prone chars, and rises around irrigated belts, industrial corridors and metropolitan regions.

6.3 Wider Significance

  • Food security: India's principal wheat-rice-sugarcane belt and a major global food-producing plain.
  • Water: the Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra river network and extensive alluvial aquifers support drinking water, irrigation, fisheries and inland navigation.
  • Transport: low gradients favour roads and railways, including the historic Grand Trunk Road corridor, dense Delhi-Kolkata rail links and modern expressways. [Correction] "Punjab-Howrah rail track" is better described as the wider Delhi-Kolkata/eastern trunk rail corridor; no single standard route bears the rough-note name.
  • Industry: rice and flour mills, sugar, dairy, jute, food processing, leather, textiles, paper and makhana/fisheries value chains.
  • Navigation: National Waterway-1 on the Ganga-Bhagirathi-Hooghly river system and National Waterway-2 on the Brahmaputra.
  • Civilisation: the Indus urban tradition developed in the north-western alluvial domain, while later Vedic, Buddhist, Jain, Mughal and colonial centres flourished across the Ganga plain.
  • Cultural landscape: Haridwar, Prayagraj, Varanasi, Bodh Gaya, Patna, Delhi, Agra, Mathura, Amritsar and many other sacred and historic centres.
[Correction] The plains are a cradle of several major civilisational phases, but the Indus Valley Civilisation was not the sole "origin" of all civilisation in the entire Northern Plain. Its mature urban core lay mainly in the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra domains.

7. Present Challenges in the Great Plains

7.1 Groundwater and Soil Stress

Green Revolution procurement, subsidised electricity and reliable tubewell technology encouraged water-intensive rice-wheat monoculture in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh. In many blocks, extraction now exceeds recharge. India is the world's largest groundwater user in absolute terms, but any annual withdrawal figure must be quoted with its CGWB or FAO assessment year.

The same intensive system has produced nutrient imbalance, declining organic carbon, residue burning, compaction and excessive chemical use. Canal command areas also face waterlogging and salinity, while alkaline usar soils reduce productivity in parts of the Ganga plain.

7.2 Water Pollution and Quality

Fertiliser and pesticide runoff, untreated sewage and industrial effluent contaminate rivers and shallow aquifers. In parts of West Bengal, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Assam, naturally occurring arsenic is mobilised in alluvial groundwater; fluoride and salinity dominate other pockets. Thus, water abundance does not automatically mean safe water.

7.3 Floods, Erosion and Encroachment

Flooding is a natural function of sediment-rich rivers, especially in the Kosi-Gandak-Ganga and Brahmaputra systems. Human damage rises when embankments constrict channels, wetlands are filled, drains are blocked and buildings occupy active Khadar. Riverbank erosion and avulsion can remove land even where flood depth is modest.

7.4 Seasonal Urban Environmental Crisis

In winter, emissions from vehicles, industry, construction dust, household fuels and crop-residue burning accumulate beneath temperature inversions across the Indo-Gangetic airshed. In summer, concretisation and loss of blue-green spaces intensify urban heat and water demand. During the monsoon, the same cities face waterlogging because natural drainage and flood storage have been removed.

7.5 Climate and Ecological Pressure

Climate warming increases heat extremes, evapotranspiration and the intensity of heavy rainfall, while Himalayan cryosphere change affects seasonal flow and hazards. At the foothills, highways, mining and settlement fragment Bhabar-Terai wildlife corridors and shrink wetlands. These pressures create linked flood-heat-drought risks rather than isolated disasters.

[Correction] Green Revolution intensification began in the mid-1960s, not only "around the 1970s". It initially transformed wheat production in Punjab, Haryana and western UP; later procurement incentives and irrigation expanded the rice-wheat cycle.
[Correction] Delhi's river zone has historically been called Zone O in the Delhi Master Plan, not "O-Zone" as a universal name for all Indian Khadar. It is a planning designation for the Yamuna river/floodplain area, not a geomorphic synonym. Its boundaries and permitted uses are legally time-sensitive: [Verification Required] against the operative DDA Master Plan and floodplain notifications before citing a specific parcel.
Exam link: Floodplains are not "vacant land". Their ecosystem service is temporary water storage, sediment deposition, aquifer recharge, nutrient cycling and habitat. Encroachment converts a natural flood into a human disaster.

8. Solutions: A Plain-Wide Land-Water-Air Strategy

8.1 Agricultural and Rural Belt

  • Crop diversification: shift unsuitable rice from water-stressed north-western districts toward pulses, oilseeds, maize, millets, fodder and horticulture, backed by procurement and value chains.
  • Agro-climatic alignment: match crop, sowing date and variety with rainfall, aquifer status, soil and heat risk rather than applying one package across the plains.
  • Efficient irrigation: laser levelling, scheduling, direct-seeded rice where locally suitable, alternate wetting and drying, drip/sprinkler for appropriate crops; avoid claiming that micro-irrigation alone solves over-extraction if the saved water expands irrigated area.
  • Aquifer-based management: village water budgets, metering and feeder reform, managed recharge in Bhabar and abandoned channels, and community groundwater plans under schemes such as Atal Bhujal Yojana where applicable.
  • Soil restoration: crop rotation, residue incorporation, green manure, compost, integrated nutrient and pest management, gypsum/reclamation for sodic soils and balanced fertiliser use.
  • Wetland and watershed protection: revive ponds, lakes, oxbows and drainage channels; protect floodplain buffers; manage catchments ridge-to-valley.
  • Flood resilience: floodplain zoning, room-for-the-river approaches, raised shelters, forecasting, resilient crops, sediment-aware embankment policy and insurance.

8.2 Urban Centres

  • Shift from project-by-project control to an airshed approach: clean public transport and freight, industrial fuel transition, dust control, waste and biomass management, and coordinated action across State borders.
  • Build compact, transit-oriented cities around Metro and Regional Rapid Transit while ensuring affordable last-mile access.
  • Segregate waste; compost wet waste, recover biomethane/biogas where viable, and prevent landfill and sewage leakage into floodplains.
  • Use blue-green infrastructure: urban forests, connected parks, restored lakes and drains, permeable paving, bioswales, cool roofs and shaded streets.
  • Enforce floodplain zoning, preserve natural drainage and design sponge-city storage for extreme rainfall.
  • Create rural-urban synergy through food processing, cold chains, circular nutrient flows, distributed services and stronger small towns rather than uncontrolled metropolitan concentration.
[Additional] The governing principle is regional carrying capacity. A solution in one place can transfer cost downstream: embankments may intensify floods elsewhere, pumping may dry neighbouring wells, and "efficient" irrigation may increase total consumption through rebound. Basin and aquifer accounting must accompany engineering.

9. Model Mains Answer: Formation and Physiographic Aspects of the Great Plains (about 150 words)

Answer: The Great Northern Plains are an extensive alluvial foreland basin between the rising Himalaya and the stable Peninsular block. India-Eurasia collision and Himalayan loading flexed the northern edge of the Indian plate downward, creating a foredeep. Over millions of years, the Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra and their tributaries filled this depression with gravel, sand, silt and clay derived mainly from the Himalaya.

A north-south transect reveals systematic sediment and moisture contrasts. At the Shiwalik foot, the 8-16 km-wide Bhabar contains porous boulders and pebbles into which streams sink. Immediately south, water re-emerges in the fine-grained, marshy Terai. Farther across the river plains, raised terraces of older alluvium form Bhangar, often with kankar nodules, while frequently renewed active floodplains form fertile Khadar. Interfluves between rivers are doabs. Meanders, levees, oxbow lakes, backswamps and braided channels show that this apparently flat plain is geomorphically dynamic. Its fertility and water created dense settlement, but also high exposure to floods, erosion, groundwater stress and pollution.

Mains Points

  • Passes are geographic chokepoints: small breaks in relief shape large networks of defence, commerce, pilgrimage and cultural diffusion.
  • Tunnels change seasonality, not geology: they improve access but transfer risk to portals, approach roads and fragile slopes; redundancy and ecological safeguards remain essential.
  • Alluvium explains both prosperity and risk: the same young, water-rich deposits that support food security are mobile floodplains subject to erosion, avulsion and liquefaction.
  • Density follows a resource bundle: soil, water, flatness, climate, transport and historic urban continuity reinforce one another across the plains.
  • North-western groundwater stress is policy-mediated: crop procurement, electricity pricing and irrigation technology converted a semi-arid physical setting into an over-extraction hotspot.
  • Flood control must become flood management: protect storage space, wetlands and channels rather than relying only on embankments and drainage engineering.
  • Air pollution is an airshed problem: basin topography and winter inversion integrate emissions from transport, industry, dust, household fuel and residue burning.
  • Rural and urban systems are coupled: food, water, labour, waste and pollution cross administrative boundaries; smart-city policy without resilient villages and small towns is incomplete.

Rohtang directly connects Kullu and Spiti only.It directly crosses from Kullu/Manali into Lahaul; routes continue toward Spiti and Leh.
Bomdi La opens into "Taksang Valley".Bomdila and Sela lie on the route to Tawang; Taktsang is a monastery near Tawang.
Foredeep equals syncline.The plain occupies a flexural foreland basin; it is not one simple folded syncline.
Bhabar is fertile fine alluvium.It is coarse, porous boulder-pebble piedmont; agriculture is limited but recharge value is high.
Terai is mostly sand and Duars is its universal synonym.Terai contains fine, waterlogged deposits; Duars specifically names eastern foothill gateways of north Bengal-Assam.
Khadar eventually rises and automatically becomes Bhangar.Bhangar is an older abandoned/raised alluvial surface formed through long-term incision, migration and geomorphic change.
Doab is another alluvial age class.It is land between two rivers, often Bhangar centrally and Khadar along channels.
Zone O means every Khadar plain.Zone O is a Delhi planning designation for the Yamuna river zone/floodplain, not an Indian physiographic category.
Flat plains are geomorphically stable.Channels migrate, braid, meander and avulse; low relief can increase flood spread.
Groundwater decline is caused only by low rainfall.Crop choice, procurement, electricity incentives and pumping technology are decisive human drivers.

30-Second Revision

A pass is a relative low saddle that concentrates movement across a mountain barrier. West to east remember Karakoram-Khardung-Zoji-Banihal-Rohtang-Lipulekh-Nathu-Bomdi-Sela. DBO supports the Karakoram-Depsang sector; Khardung opens to Nubra; Zoji links Kashmir with Dras-Kargil-Ladakh; Banihal crosses the Pir Panjal; Rohtang links Kullu with Lahaul; Lipulekh and Nathu La are Kailash-Manasarovar routes; Bomdi La and Sela lead toward Tawang. Tunnels improve all-season access but do not remove slope and avalanche risk. South of the Himalaya, crustal loading created a foredeep filled by Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra alluvium. North to south: coarse porous Bhabar absorbs streams; saturated fine-grained Terai brings them back; raised older Bhangar often contains kankar; active younger Khadar is renewed by floods; a doab is simply an interfluve. Fertile soil, water, flatness and transport explain dense settlement and the food bowl, while groundwater depletion, chemical and sewage pollution, floods, winter smog and urban heat demand crop diversification, aquifer budgeting, soil restoration, protected floodplains, blue-green cities and regional airshed governance.

Siachen Glacier is situated to the

(a) East of Aksai Chin

How can the mountain ecosystem be restored from the negative impact of development initiatives and tourism?

Answer direction: Use Himalayan pass corridors as the case: carrying-capacity-based tourism, environmental assessment at corridor scale, tunnelling spoil management, slope drainage and bio-engineering, avalanche/landslide zonation, controlled construction, public transport, waste take-back, habitat corridors, community benefit-sharing and climate-risk monitoring. Conclude that tunnels improve access but cumulative road-tourism pressure must remain within ecological limits.

UPSC CSE Mains 2016 - General Studies Paper I

The frequency of urban floods due to high intensity rainfall is increasing over the years. Discussing the reasons for urban floods, highlight the mechanisms for preparedness to reduce the risk during such events.

Answer direction: Connect intense rain with floodplain/wetland encroachment, concretisation, blocked natural drains, undersized storm sewers, solid waste and reservoir-release coordination. For plain cities: floodplain zoning, drainage-basin mapping, restored lakes and Yamuna/Ganga buffers, permeable surfaces, nowcasting, ward-level evacuation, protected utilities, raised shelters and unified incident command.

UPSC CSE Mains 2020 - General Studies Paper I

The interlinking of rivers can provide viable solutions to the multi-dimensional inter-related problems of droughts, floods, and interrupted navigation. Critically examine.

Answer direction: Benefits include seasonal/spatial redistribution, irrigation, navigation and storage. Critique "surplus" basin assumptions, sediment and delta impacts, submergence, displacement, interstate conflict, energy cost and climate uncertainty. In the Northern Plains, prioritise wetland restoration, floodplain storage, aquifer recharge, demand management and navigation-compatible environmental flows before treating transfers as a universal solution.
