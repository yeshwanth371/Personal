1. Modern Concept of the Monsoon: Overview

The thermal concept (land-sea heating) and the dynamic concept (ITCZ shift + Coriolis) explain why the monsoon comes. The modern concept (from the 1950s onward, using upper-air data) explains its onset, strength and year-to-year variability through these factors:

2. Jet Streams

2.1 What Is a Jet Stream?

  • Jet streams are narrow bands of very fast winds in the upper troposphere, just below the tropopause. They are thousands of km long but only a few hundred km wide and a few km deep.
  • They form where the north-south temperature gradient is steepest, i.e. along the boundary where warm and cold air masses meet. A steep temperature gradient produces a steep pressure gradient aloft, and so fast winds. This is the class point "pressure difference must be high".
  • Most jets blow from west to east (westerly) and meander in waves. The Tropical Easterly Jet over India in summer is the major exception (3.2).
  • [Additional] Main jets: the Subtropical Westerly Jet (STWJ) near 30° latitude, at about 12 km (200 hPa) †; and the Polar Front Jet at about 50-60°, at about 9-10 km †. Over India the winter STWJ usually blows at about 150-250 km/h †; peaks above 300 km/h occur over East Asia and Japan. Both hemispheres have jets.

2.2 How the Subtropical Westerly Jet Forms

Class explanation (as noted): air heated in the tropics rises, expands and cools adiabatically, and around 8-12 km forms high pressure aloft. Over cold temperate latitudes the opposite holds. The resulting upper-level pressure difference, turned by the Coriolis force near the subtropics, gives the STWJ.

[Correction] The idea of high pressure aloft over the tropics is right, but adiabatic cooling is not its cause. The accepted explanation:
  • Thickness effect: warm air is less dense, so a warm tropical air column is taller. At any given height (say 10-12 km), more air lies above the tropics than above cold latitudes, so the pressure there is higher over the tropics.
  • Cold latitudes do not have "high pressure below and low pressure aloft" as a rule. Their columns are shallower, so pressure falls off faster with height, giving relatively low pressure aloft.
  • This equator-to-pole pressure difference increases with height. Air pushed poleward is turned to the right (eastward) by the Coriolis force in the northern hemisphere. The result is westerly wind that strengthens with height (the "thermal wind").
[Correction] The rough notes say the Coriolis force "moves it down around the subtropical" belt. More precisely, the upper branch of the Hadley cell carries air poleward from the ITCZ. As it moves poleward it conserves its angular momentum and is deflected eastward, speeding up. The westerly jet is therefore concentrated at the poleward edge of the Hadley cell, about 30°N. Below that, the air sinks to form the subtropical high-pressure belt.
tropics warm → tall air column→higher pressure aloft over the tropics→upper air pushed poleward (Hadley cell)→Coriolis turns it eastward + angular momentum→fast westerly jet at ~30°N, ~12 km

2.3 Winter: The STWJ Splits Around Tibet

  • In winter the STWJ lies far south, at about 27-30°N over India (NCERT). The Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau (and the very high Trans-Himalaya, Karakoram and Kunlun) stand in its path.
  • The jet splits into two branches: a northern branch north of the plateau and a southern branch along the southern slope of the Himalaya, over north India. They rejoin to the east of the plateau, near China and Japan (Map 1).
  • The southern branch matters for India:
    • Air sinks beneath its southern side, producing high pressure and dry, stable weather over north-west India in winter.
    • It steers the western disturbances from the Mediterranean region into north-west India. These bring winter rain to Punjab and Haryana (good for rabi wheat) and snow to the western Himalaya.
    • While it stays over India the upper-level westerlies block the monsoon; a late retreat delays onset.
[Correction] The rough notes say the southern branch "creates HP, drags the westerly air, helps in HP usually". The corrected meaning: the southern branch causes sinking air and high pressure over north-west India in winter and drags (steers) the western disturbances eastward into India. The idea that it "delays the monsoon" is correct: its persistence holds back onset.

2.4 Early June: The Southern Branch Withdraws

  • As the sun's apparent northward movement heats Asia, the southern branch weakens in May and usually jumps north of the Tibetan Plateau (to about 35-40°N †) in late May to early June.
  • With the westerly jet gone from over India, the upper-level block is removed. The monsoon trough (low pressure) deepens over north India and the south-west monsoon can advance. The jet returns south in about October, with the monsoon's withdrawal.
[Correction] The rough notes say the southern branch "disappears" and that this "causes LP over India, causes the monsoon to arrive". Two refinements:
  • The branch does not vanish. It shifts north of Tibet.
  • The shift is one of several onset factors that happen together (NCERT lists it with the ITCZ shift, Tibetan heating and the TEJ). It is not the sole cause. The main driver of the low over north India is surface heating, with the ITCZ moving north (dynamic concept).

3. Tibetan Plateau, Tropical Easterly Jet and Somali Jet

3.1 Tibetan Plateau as a Heat Engine

  • The Tibetan Plateau is a large, very high plateau (average elevation about 4,500 m †). It is often called the "Roof of the World".
  • In summer its surface heats strongly. Because the surface itself is so high, it heats the middle troposphere directly, about 4-5 km above sea level. This makes the plateau an elevated heat source, a "heat engine" in the class phrase. Latent heat from monsoon rain adds to the heating.
  • The heated air rises. In the upper troposphere above the plateau it spreads out, forming the Tibetan anticyclone (high pressure aloft) at about 200-100 hPa. Its southern flank gives easterly winds over India: the Tropical Easterly Jet.
[Correction] The rough notes say the plateau is hot "due to direct sun-rays". The sun is overhead only up to the Tropic of Cancer (23.5°N) on about 21 June; Tibet (about 28-38°N) never receives vertical rays. It heats because of long summer days, thin dry air (strong sunshine) and its height: its surface warms air that is already high in the atmosphere. Its popular name is the "Roof of the World", not "Roof of the earth".

3.2 Tropical Easterly Jet (TEJ)

  • Direction: blows from east to west. It is the only major easterly jet.
  • Height and position: core at about 150-100 hPa (14-16 km) †, at about 10-15°N. NCERT places it near 14°N over the peninsula.
  • Speed: about 30-50 m/s (100-180 km/h) †. Season: June to September, strongest in July-August.
  • Extent: from the South China Sea across south India and the Arabian Sea to East and West Africa. Identified by P. Koteswaram (1958) †.
  • Link to the monsoon: it develops after the STWJ leaves India and marks a fully established monsoon. Its strength is linked to monsoon strength; a weak TEJ is associated with weak spells.
[Correction] "TES" in the rough notes is the TEJ (Tropical Easterly Jet). Its core is about 14-16 km (150-100 hPa) high, rather than "approx. 13 km". It is established over India in June, after onset begins, so it marks the established monsoon rather than triggering the onset.

3.3 The Mascarene High and the Somali Jet

Class explanation (as noted): the TEJ descends near Madagascar over the Indian Ocean and intensifies the high pressure there.

[Correction] The TEJ does not sink near Madagascar. It blows westward into Africa, not south. This claim was not found in NCERT or the IMD material checked. The accepted picture (Figure 2):
  • Some of the upper-level air flowing out of the monsoon region does cross the equator. It sinks over the southern subtropical Indian Ocean, a "reverse Hadley" or monsoon cell.
  • That area holds the Mascarene High, a semi-permanent subtropical high east of Madagascar. NCERT puts it at about 20°S and lists its strength and position as an onset factor.
  • A stronger Mascarene High drives stronger cross-equatorial winds and a stronger Somali Jet, which tends to favour the monsoon.
  • [Additional] Somali Jet (Findlater Jet): a low-level jet, core at about 1-1.5 km (850-925 hPa) †. It flows from the Mascarene High area across the equator off the coast of Somalia and Kenya, then curves north-east across the Arabian Sea towards the Western Ghats.
  • Typical speeds are 15-25 m/s, sometimes up to about 50 m/s †. It was described by J. Findlater in 1969. The East African highlands channel it along the coast. It also drives coastal upwelling off Somalia and Oman, cooling the western Arabian Sea.
[Correction] The rough notes call the Somali Jet only "mild support" for the Indian monsoon. In fact it is a main channel of cross-equatorial moisture for the Arabian Sea branch. A large share of the water vapour reaching the west coast travels in it, and its strength is related to monsoon intensity.

3.4 Burst of the Monsoon and the Ideal Onset Conditions

Burst of the monsoon (NCERT): the sudden onset of the rain-bearing south-west winds, with violent thunder and lightning, in contrast to a gradual build-up. It first happens on the Kerala coast.

[Correction] The rough notes attach the term "burst of monsoon" to the intensification of high pressure near Madagascar. The burst is the abrupt arrival of heavy monsoon rain over India, not a pressure process over the Indian Ocean. The circulation factors below together lead to the burst.

Ideal conditions at the end of May / early June (as listed in class, corrected):

  1. The ITCZ shifts north over India and becomes the monsoon trough (low pressure over north-west India).
  2. The southern branch of the STWJ moves north of Tibet, removing the upper-level block.
  3. The heated Tibetan Plateau strengthens the upper-level high and the surface low over north India.
  4. The TEJ develops over south India.
  5. A strong Mascarene High and the Somali Jet push moist south-westerlies across the equator into the Arabian Sea.
ITCZ moves north→STWJ jumps north of Tibet→Tibet heats: upper high + TEJ→Mascarene High + Somali Jet drive moist SW winds→burst over Kerala (~1 June)
[Additional] Onset dates (IMD):
  • Normal onset over Kerala is 1 June, with a standard deviation of about 7 days.
  • IMD's revised normal dates (press release of 15 May 2020): onset normals were based on 1961-2019 and withdrawal normals on 1971-2019. The monsoon now covers the whole country by about 8 July (earlier 15 July). Withdrawal from north-west India begins around 17 September (earlier 1 September).
  • Onset over the Andaman Sea is about 22 May.

4. El Nino and La Nina (ENSO)

ENSO (El Nino-Southern Oscillation) is a coupled ocean-atmosphere cycle in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific, off the coast of Peru and Ecuador. It has three states: neutral (normal), El Nino (warm) and La Nina (cold). The ocean part is El Nino / La Nina; the atmospheric part is the Southern Oscillation, a see-saw of surface pressure between Tahiti (east) and Darwin (west).

4.1 Normal (Neutral) Conditions and the Walker Cell

  1. The SE trade winds blow from east to west along the equator. They drag surface water westward as the South Equatorial Current.
  2. Warm surface water piles up in the western Pacific (the "warm pool" near Indonesia and Australia). There the sea surface is about 8°C warmer † and the sea level is higher.
  3. Off Peru, the displaced surface water is replaced by cold, nutrient-rich water from below (upwelling), helped by the cold Humboldt (Peru) Current. This supports one of the world's richest fisheries (anchovy).
  4. Air rises over the warm west, creating low pressure and heavy rain over Indonesia. It flows east aloft, sinks over the cold eastern Pacific (high pressure, dry), and returns west as the trades. This closed loop is the Walker circulation (Walker cell).
  5. The thermocline (the boundary between warm surface water and cold deep water) is deep in the west and shallow in the east.
[Correction] Two points in the rough notes:
  • "South equatorial warm current": the South Equatorial Current starts cold off Peru (fed by upwelling and the Humboldt Current) and warms as it flows west.
  • "LP is created in Western P and HP in Eastern Pressure" means low pressure over the western Pacific (Indonesia) and high pressure over the eastern Pacific (Peru).
[Additional] Names: Sir Gilbert Walker, head of the India Meteorological Department (1904-1924), discovered the Southern Oscillation in the 1920s while trying to forecast the Indian monsoon. Jacob Bjerknes named the "Walker circulation" in 1969 and linked it to El Nino.

4.2 El Nino: The Warm Phase

  • Trigger: sea temperature rises off Peru and in the central-eastern Pacific through a complex ocean-atmosphere interaction. The SE trades weaken, sometimes with bursts of westerly wind in the west.
  • The South Equatorial Current weakens, so the warm pool spreads east. Eastward-moving ocean waves (Kelvin waves) push the warm water and deepen the thermocline in the east. The eastward Equatorial Counter Current tends to strengthen.
  • Upwelling off Peru is suppressed: warm, nutrient-poor water arrives, fish catches collapse and seabirds die.
  • The Walker cell weakens and its rising branch shifts east. Rain and floods move to the central Pacific and Peru-Ecuador. Air sinks over Indonesia and Australia, bringing drought; the Indian monsoon usually weakens.
  • The weaker trades let even more warm water move east. This feedback (the Bjerknes feedback) is why an El Nino grows for several months.
SE trades weaken→warm pool moves east→upwelling off Peru stops→Walker cell weakens / shifts east→sinking air over Indonesia and India→weaker SW monsoon (usually)
[Correction] The rough notes say El Nino "causes" a counter equatorial current. The main sequence is the weakening of the trades and eastward-moving Kelvin waves, which carry warm water east. A stronger eastward Equatorial Counter Current is part of this, not the root cause. The class phrase "reversal of the Walker cell" is best read as a weakening and eastward shift; a full reversal happens only in the strongest events.

4.3 La Nina: The Cold Phase

  • It is an intensified normal state. The trades are stronger, more warm water piles up in the west, and upwelling off Peru is stronger. The eastern Pacific becomes unusually cold.
  • The Walker cell strengthens. Rain increases over Indonesia and Australia (floods in Australia, 2010-11), and drought increases in Peru and the southern USA.
  • For India, La Nina generally favours a normal or above-normal monsoon and colder winters in the north.

4.4 How an El Nino Is Declared and Graded

  • Region: the Nino 3.4 region, 5°N-5°S and 120°-170°W in the central Pacific.
  • Threshold: a sea-surface temperature anomaly of +0.5°C or more, sustained for five consecutive overlapping three-month seasons. A La Nina is -0.5°C or below on the same basis.
  • Index: from 1 February 2026, NOAA's Climate Prediction Center uses the Relative Oceanic Nino Index (RONI) instead of the ONI. The RONI removes the general warming of the whole tropical ocean, so a warm event has to stand out from that background.
  • Life cycle: an El Nino usually begins in boreal spring (March-June), grows through the Indian monsoon season and peaks around November-January, near Christmas. It decays by the next spring. Events recur every 2-7 years †.
[Correction] "0.5°C or above causes El Nino" is the definition threshold, not a cause. The +0.5°C anomaly must be in the Nino 3.4 region and must persist for five overlapping three-month seasons; a single warm month is not an El Nino. Since February 2026 NOAA measures it with the RONI.

4.5 History of the Name

  • Peruvian fishermen named the warm current that appeared around Christmas "El Nino" ("the boy", i.e. the Christ Child). "La Nina" ("the girl") was coined later by scientists for the opposite phase.
  • Peruvian geographer Camilo Carrillo brought the name into scientific use in 1892.
[Correction] The rough notes say the "first" El Nino was in "1600 AD". There is no standard first El Nino. Coral and sediment records show it is thousands of years old. Written colonial records of events go back to about the 1520s (Quinn's list starts at 1525). The name entered scientific use in 1892.

5. Indian Ocean Dipole and Madden-Julian Oscillation [Additional]

Both were listed in class without details; this short section is added for completeness and will be expanded if covered in the next class.

5.1 Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)

  • The IOD is the difference in sea-surface temperature between the western and eastern tropical Indian Ocean. It is sometimes called the "Indian Nino".
  • It is measured by the Dipole Mode Index (Saji et al., 1999): the west pole (50-70°E, 10°S-10°N) minus the east pole (90-110°E, 10°S-0°). The Bureau of Meteorology uses a threshold of ±0.4°C, held for about 8 weeks.
  • Positive IOD: the west (near Africa) is warmer than the east (near Sumatra-Java). Convection shifts west, which generally favours the Indian SW monsoon and can offset El Nino, as in 1997.
  • Negative IOD: the opposite, and generally unfavourable for India.

5.2 Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO)

  • The MJO is a large eastward-moving pulse of clouds and rain along the equator. It moves at about 4-8 m/s † and returns every 30-60 days (range 30-90). It was described by Madden and Julian in 1971-72 †.
  • When its active phase is over the Indian Ocean (phases 2-4), it favours active spells of the monsoon and helps cyclones form. In other phases it favours breaks.
  • It affects weeks within a season, unlike ENSO and the IOD, which affect whole seasons. IMD's extended-range forecasts track it: on 12 November 2026 the MJO was in phase 3 with an amplitude above 1.

6. Human (Anthropogenic) Influences on the Monsoon

The class listed climate change, air pollution, excessive concretisation, deforestation, wetland encroachment and land-use change.

Climate change (warming)Warmer air holds more moisture (about 7% more per °C †), so extreme rainfall events increase even when the seasonal total does not. Rain becomes more erratic, with longer dry spells.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Modern concept factors: jet streams (STWJ, TEJ, Somali), the Tibetan Plateau, ENSO, the IOD, the MJO and human factors.
  • Jet streams: fast, narrow upper-tropospheric winds where the temperature gradient is steepest. They occur in both hemispheres. STWJ: about 30°, about 12 km (200 hPa), westerly.
  • Winter STWJ: about 27-30°N over India; it splits around Tibet. The southern branch brings sinking air over NW India, steers western disturbances, and moves north of Tibet in late May to early June.
  • Tibetan Plateau: an elevated summer heat source that builds an upper-level anticyclone.
  • TEJ: easterly; 14-16 km; about 14°N over the peninsula; June-September; South China Sea to Africa; Koteswaram (1958).
  • Somali (Findlater) Jet: low-level (1-1.5 km), cross-equatorial off East Africa; a major moisture source. Mascarene High: about 20-30°S, east of Madagascar.
  • Burst: sudden onset with thunder and lightning. Kerala onset 1 June ± 7 days; whole country by 8 July (IMD 2020 normals). The 2026 onset was 4 June.
  • ENSO: Walker cell (Bjerknes, 1969, after Gilbert Walker of IMD). El Nino brings weak trades, a warm east Pacific and no Peru upwelling. La Nina brings strong trades and a cold east Pacific.
  • Definition: Nino 3.4 (5°N-5°S, 120-170°W) at +0.5°C or more for 5 overlapping seasons. NOAA has used the RONI since February 2026. Strength bands are unofficial; "super" is a media term.
  • Name: Peruvian fishermen (Christ Child, around Christmas); scientific use from 1892 (Carrillo).
  • IOD: west pole 50-70°E minus east pole 90-110°E; a positive IOD favours the monsoon. MJO: eastward-moving, 30-60 days, drives active and break spells.
  • 2026: a very strong El Nino (Nino 3.4 at +2.5°C, 12 November 2026); the monsoon was 87% of LPA.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Jet streams are not only in the northern hemisphere (Prelims 2020).
  • STWJ = westerly; TEJ = easterly. The TEJ blows from east to west and is higher (14-16 km) than the STWJ (about 12 km).
  • Somali Jet is low-level (about 1-1.5 km), not an upper-air jet like the STWJ and TEJ.
  • High pressure aloft over the tropics comes from the warm, tall air column, not from adiabatic cooling.
  • Tibet never gets vertical sun rays; the overhead sun stops at the Tropic of Cancer.
  • The TEJ does not sink near Madagascar; the Mascarene High is a southern subtropical high.
  • "Burst" is the sudden onset of rain over India, not a pressure change over the ocean.
  • El Nino is a warm anomaly in the central-eastern Pacific, not the Indian Ocean. La Nina is cold in the Pacific, not the Indian Ocean (Prelims 2011).
  • El Nino does not always mean drought in India (1997); La Nina does affect the monsoon.
  • IOD compares the western and eastern Indian Ocean, not the Indian and Pacific Oceans (Prelims 2017).
  • +0.5°C must be sustained (5 overlapping seasons) in Nino 3.4. "Super El Nino" and "severe" are not official grades.
  • El Nino peaks in November-January, after the Indian monsoon, which falls in its growing phase.

30-Second Revision

  • Modern concept = jets + Tibet + ENSO + IOD + MJO + human factors.
  • STWJ (about 12 km, westerly) splits around Tibet in winter. The southern branch brings dry sinking air and western disturbances to north India. It jumps north of Tibet in early June, alongside onset.
  • Tibet heats and builds an upper anticyclone, which drives the TEJ (easterly, 14-16 km, about 14°N, June-September). The Mascarene High and Somali Jet (low-level) carry moisture, leading to the burst over Kerala on about 1 June.
  • El Nino: weak trades, warm east Pacific, no Peru upwelling and a weak or shifted Walker cell, so usually a weaker Indian monsoon. La Nina: the reverse. Threshold: +0.5°C in Nino 3.4. Peak in November-January.
  • 2026-27: very strong El Nino (+2.5°C); monsoon 87% of LPA. Positive IOD and the active MJO phase help the monsoon.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2020 - General Studies Paper I

Consider the following statements:

  1. Jet streams occur in the Northern Hemisphere only.
  2. Only some cyclones develop an eye.
  3. The temperature inside the eye of a cyclone is nearly 10°C lesser than that of the surroundings.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 2 only
  4. 1 and 3 only
Correct answer: (c) 2 only.
  • Statement 1 is wrong: jet streams occur in both hemispheres (2.1, Figure 1).
  • Statement 2 is right: only mature, intense cyclones develop an eye.
  • Statement 3 is wrong: the eye is warmer than its surroundings, because of sinking air.
Acme IAS gives (c) as the official key; one compilation (PMF IAS) gives (b). Source: Acme IAS; UnlockIAS.

2. UPSC CSE Prelims 2011 - General Studies Paper I

La Nina is suspected to have caused recent floods in Australia. How is La Nina different from El Nino?

  1. La Nina is characterised by unusually cold ocean temperature in equatorial Indian Ocean whereas El Nino is characterised by unusually warm ocean temperature in the equatorial Pacific Ocean.
  2. El Nino has adverse effect on south-west monsoon of India, but La Nina has no effect on monsoon climate.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Correct answer: (d) Neither 1 nor 2. La Nina is a cold anomaly in the equatorial Pacific, not the Indian Ocean. It does affect the monsoon, usually favourably (4.3, 4.6). Source: Acme IAS; UnlockIAS.

3. UPSC CSE Prelims 2017 - General Studies Paper I

With reference to 'Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)' sometimes mentioned in the news while forecasting Indian monsoon, which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. IOD phenomenon is characterised by a difference in sea surface temperature between tropical Western Indian Ocean and tropical Eastern Pacific Ocean.
  2. An IOD phenomenon can influence an El Nino's impact on the monsoon.

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Correct answer: (b) 2 only. The IOD compares the western and eastern Indian Ocean, not the Pacific. A positive IOD can offset El Nino, as in 1997 (5.1, Figure 5). Source: PMF IAS; UPSC PYQ Boost.

4. UPSC CSE Prelims 2002 - General Studies

For short-term climate prediction, which one of the following events, detected in the last decade, is associated with occasional weak monsoon rains in the Indian subcontinent?

  1. La Nina
  2. Movement of Jet Streams
  3. El Nino and Southern Oscillations
  4. Greenhouse effects on global level
Correct answer: (c) El Nino and Southern Oscillations. ENSO is the leading predictor of a weak monsoon (section 4). Source: PMF IAS.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2014 - General Studies Paper I (10 marks)

Most of the unusual climatic happenings are explained as an outcome of the El-Nino effect. Do you agree?

Answer approach:
  • Define El Nino and the Walker cell (4.2, Figure 3).
  • Agree in part. El Nino brings Indian and Australian droughts, Peruvian floods and global temperature records.
  • Qualify it. The IOD (1997), the MJO, La Nina, the polar vortex, aerosols and climate change also explain extremes; correlation is not one-to-one (4.6).
  • Conclude: El Nino is a major, not exclusive, driver.
Source: Acme IAS.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2015 - General Studies Paper I (12.5 marks)

How far do you agree that the behaviour of the Indian monsoon has been changing due to humanizing landscapes? Discuss.

Answer approach:
  • Describe the observed changes (MoES 2020): a 6% decline in June-September rainfall and 75% more heavy-rain extremes.
  • Explain the human links: aerosols, deforestation, urban heat islands, concretisation and wetland loss (section 6).
  • Note the natural drivers too: ENSO, the IOD and the MJO.
  • Conclude with a balanced view and suggest remedies.
Source: EaseMyPrep; MaargX.

Corrections Made

Each link returns to the explanation, which keeps the original rough-note claim or its intended meaning.

  1. Spellings: MJO, Dipole, La Nina, anthropogenic.
  2. High pressure aloft over the tropics comes from warm, tall air columns (thickness), not adiabatic cooling.
  3. Coriolis turns the Hadley cell's poleward upper flow eastward; the jet sits at its poleward edge, about 30°N.
  4. The southern branch causes sinking air over NW India and steers western disturbances.
  5. The southern branch shifts north of Tibet rather than disappearing; it is one of several onset factors.
  6. Tibet never gets vertical sun rays; "Roof of the World".
  7. TES is the TEJ; core at 14-16 km; it marks the established monsoon.
  8. The TEJ does not sink near Madagascar; the Mascarene High's role is clarified.
  9. The Somali Jet is a main moisture channel, not "mild support".
  10. "Burst" is the sudden onset of rain over India.
  11. The South Equatorial Current starts cold off Peru; low pressure in the west, high in the east Pacific.
  12. El Nino is driven by weakened trades and Kelvin waves; the Walker cell weakens and shifts rather than reversing outright.
  13. +0.5°C is a sustained Nino 3.4 threshold (RONI since 2026), not a cause.
  14. Strength bands are unofficial; the top band is "very strong"; "super" is a media term.
  15. No "first El Nino in 1600 AD"; records go back to the 1520s; the name entered science in 1892.
  16. El Nino usually, not always, weakens the monsoon.
