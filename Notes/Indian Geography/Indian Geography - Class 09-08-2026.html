1. Lakshadweep: India's Coral-Atoll Territory

Lakshadweep is India's smallest Union Territory by land area: an Arabian Sea archipelago of 36 islands covering about 32 sq km. The official description includes 12 atolls, three reefs, five submerged banks and ten inhabited islands. Kavaratti is the capital.

[Correction] Lakshadweep is the smallest Union Territory, not the smallest “territory” in an undefined sense. Its tiny 32 sq km land area must not be confused with its much larger lagoon, territorial-water and Exclusive Economic Zone areas.

1.1 Islands, Environment and Economy

FeatureRevision point
Named inhabited islandsKavaratti, Agatti, Amini, Kadmat, Kiltan, Chetlat, Bitra, Andrott, Kalpeni and Minicoy. Bitra is the smallest inhabited island; Minicoy is the southernmost inhabited island.
ReliefLow-lying coral islands sit only a few metres above mean sea level and therefore face erosion, storm-surge, freshwater and sea-level risks.
BiodiversityCoral reefs, lagoons, seagrass, reef fish, tuna, turtles and seabirds form linked ecosystems. Healthy reefs protect shorelines and sustain fisheries.
LivelihoodsFishing, coconut cultivation, coir production and regulated tourism are important. Pole-and-line tuna fishing is strongly associated with Minicoy.
Tourism limitsWhite carbonate sand, lagoons and reefs attract visitors, but freshwater, waste disposal, reef carrying capacity and transport constrain growth.
[Correction] “Mini Coy” is Minicoy. The rough note also merged two channels: the Nine Degree Channel separates Minicoy from the rest of Lakshadweep, while the Eight Degree Channel separates Minicoy/Lakshadweep from the Maldives.
[Correction] Coral is not algae. A reef-building coral is an animal colony of polyps; microscopic photosynthetic dinoflagellates commonly called zooxanthellae live symbiotically in its tissues and supply much of its energy.

1.2 How an Atoll and Coral Reef Develop

RequirementWhy it matters
Warm tropical waterMost reef-building corals thrive roughly between 23.5 degrees N and 23.5 degrees S, though local limits vary.
Clear, shallow, sunlit waterLight supports photosynthesis by symbiotic algae; excessive sediment blocks light and can smother polyps.
Normal marine salinity and oxygenationLarge freshwater input and stagnant or polluted water stress reef organisms.

1.3 Coral Bleaching, Climate Risk and Response

marine heat stress→coral-algal relationship disrupted→algae expelled / pigments lost→white skeleton visible→starvation, disease and possible mortality if stress persists
[Correction] Bleaching is not automatically coral death. A bleached coral is alive but severely stressed and may recover if temperature and other pressures fall; prolonged or repeated stress can cause mortality and reef erosion.
[Correction] “5 mm/year since 2024 by UN” is not a valid trend statement. WMO's State of the Global Climate 2024 reports global mean sea-level rise averaging about 4.7 mm/year during 2015-2024, versus 2.1 mm/year during 1993-2002. Local Lakshadweep relative sea level can differ because of ocean circulation, winds and vertical land movement.
[Additional] Reefs face compound pressures from ocean warming, acidification, destructive fishing, dredging, sewage, plastics, sediment and poorly managed tourism. Global emission cuts limit heat and sea-level risk; local action improves reef recovery through water-quality control, no-take areas, sustainable fisheries, visitor limits, early warning and reef monitoring.
Security framing: India-Maldives relations should not be described as inevitable “hostility”. Chinese investment and strategic competition are geopolitical concerns, but policy analysis should distinguish verified infrastructure, diplomatic changes and military facts from speculation.

3. Thar Desert: Formation, Features and Resources

The Thar or Great Indian Desert is a hot subtropical desert extending across western Rajasthan into Pakistan's Sindh and Punjab. Smaller extensions occur in Gujarat, Haryana and Punjab. It is dominated by sandy plains and dunes but also contains rocky surfaces, saline depressions and older alluvial tracts.

[Correction] The Thar is not simply the “Marwar region”. Marwar is a historical-cultural region of western Rajasthan centred on Jodhpur and forms only part of the wider Thar. “Warwad culture” is corrected to Marwar/Marwari culture.

3.1 Why the Thar Is Arid

ControlEffect
Subtropical settingSeasonal subsidence and high-pressure conditions suppress cloud development, especially outside the summer monsoon.
Aravalli orientationThe range runs broadly south-west to north-east, nearly parallel to the Arabian Sea branch of the south-west monsoon; it causes little broad orographic uplift over western Rajasthan.
Continentality and moisture lossDistance from the Bay of Bengal and progressive rainout weaken the Bay branch by the time it reaches north-west India.
Highly variable monsoonLow and erratic rainfall, high potential evapotranspiration and recurrent drought prevent persistent integrated surface drainage.
Wind actionStrong pre-monsoon pressure gradients, convection and dry loose sediment generate dust storms and reshape dunes.
[Correction] The south-west monsoon does not wholly “bypass” the Thar. It brings limited, highly variable rain. The Aravallis' parallel alignment and weak relief reduce uplift; the distant Bay branch has already lost much moisture.
[Correction] Dust storms and dunes are not explained simply by “westerly dry winds from Iranian/Arabian deserts”. Local heating, strong pre-monsoon winds, sparse vegetation and abundant loose sediment are central. Western disturbances can bring winter rain and weather from the west, but they are a different phenomenon.
weak orographic uplift + continentality→low, variable rain→sparse vegetation and dry sediment→wind erosion and transport→dunes and dust storms

3.2 Drainage, Landforms and Economy

  • Luni: the principal river of the Indian desert; rises near the Aravallis and ends in the marshy Rann of Kachchh region. Its lower water becomes increasingly saline.
  • Ghaggar: a seasonal river that loses itself in the plains/desert; palaeochannel research is important, but equating it conclusively with the Vedic Saraswati remains debated.
  • Inland drainage: ephemeral streams terminate in playas or saline lakes such as Sambhar, Didwana, Kuchaman and Pachpadra rather than reaching the sea.
  • Dunes: longitudinal, transverse and crescentic barchans occur according to wind regime, sediment and vegetation.
  • Resources: petroleum and natural gas in the Rajasthan Basin, lignite, gypsum, limestone, salt, solar and wind energy; the Indira Gandhi Canal supports irrigation but can cause waterlogging and salinity where drainage is poor.
[Correction] A precise “Saraswati dried after Harappan times” chronology is not established by the rough note. River capture, monsoon weakening and channel migration affected north-western palaeodrainage at different times. [Verification Required] for any claim equating a particular Ghaggar-Hakra channel phase with the Vedic Saraswati.
[Additional] The Thar's eastern margin is more densely settled and cultivated than its arid west. Irrigation, roads and shelterbelts change land use, but grazing pressure, invasive species, groundwater salinity and desertification require watershed-scale management.

4. Drainage System of India: Concepts and Patterns

Drainage is the organized flow of surface water through streams and rivers over a region. Its form is controlled by relief and slope, rock structure, tectonics, climate, runoff, sediment and geological history. A drainage system is the connected main river and its tributaries, not merely another word for “rivers”.

[Correction] Replace “glacier pressure” with glacial melt and glacial erosion. Glaciers can feed and reshape Himalayan valleys, but drainage depends more broadly on slope, lithology, structures, rainfall/snowmelt, tectonics and base level.

4.1 Basin, Catchment, Watershed and Tributary

TermMeaning
TributaryA stream or river that joins a larger stream/main stem. It need not always be “minor”; some tributaries can exceed the named main stem in length or discharge.
ConfluenceThe point at which two streams meet.
Catchment / drainage basinThe land area from which runoff drains to a common outlet. Usage often treats catchment and basin as synonyms, although “catchment” may describe a sub-basin in project work.
Watershed / drainage divideStrictly, the high boundary separating adjacent basins. In Indian planning, “watershed” also commonly means a small hydrological planning unit; size alone does not define the scientific term.
River regimeThe seasonal pattern of discharge, controlled by rain, snow/ice melt, groundwater storage, dams and withdrawals.
[Correction] A main river is not defined by being older, deeper, longer, more voluminous or religiously more important. River naming is historical and conventional. The Yamuna is the Ganga's longest tributary in India, but that does not make “Yamuna should be the main river” a valid hydrological rule.
[Additional] The Ganga basin is India's largest river basin. Smaller watershed units can be easier for local treatment, but upstream-downstream links require basin-scale coordination.

4.2 Major Drainage Patterns

PatternControl and Indian example
DendriticBranching like a tree where rock resistance is broadly uniform; common across many alluvial and plateau surfaces.
RadialStreams flow outward from a central high. Amarkantak is a standard example; volcanic cones can also produce this pattern, so “volcanic” is a cause, not the pattern's name.
RectangularRight-angle bends follow joints and faults in strongly fractured bedrock; associated with parts of the Vindhyan terrain.
TrellisLong parallel main streams with short tributaries joining nearly at right angles where alternating hard-soft folded or tilted strata create parallel ridges and valleys.
CentripetalStreams converge into an enclosed basin or depression; common around inland saline basins of Rajasthan.

5. Himalayan and Peninsular River Systems

India's major drainage is commonly grouped into Himalayan and Peninsular systems. The comparison is useful, but every statement has exceptions.

FeatureHimalayan systemsPeninsular systems
Source and regimeRain, snow and glacier melt; major rivers are generally perennial, but discharge is strongly seasonal.Mostly rainfall-fed and more seasonal; large reservoirs and groundwater sustain regulated/base flow in places.
Age and structureYoung mountain relief, active uplift and unstable slopes; some major courses are antecedent.Older stable block with mature, structurally guided valleys; superimposed and antecedent reaches also occur.
Gradient and valleyVery steep and fast in mountains with gorges; gentle, meandering courses across northern plains.Generally lower gradients and shallower valleys on plateaus, but waterfalls, gorges and rift valleys occur locally.
Basin and dischargeUsually large basins and high discharge; Ganga has India's largest basin.Generally smaller basins and lower discharge, though Godavari and other east-flowing systems are extensive.
Sediment and landformsHigh mountain erosion supplies heavy sediment; plains show floodplains, levees, meanders, oxbow lakes, bhangar, khadar and doabs.Lower average sediment load and more fixed channels over resistant rock; east-flowing rivers build large deltas, while Narmada and Tapi form estuaries.
Flood behaviourLarge monsoon floods are frequent in the plains due to intense rain, snowmelt contribution, sediment and channel migration.Not flood-free: cyclones and intense monsoon rain can cause severe Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi and short west-coast floods.
[Correction] “Himalayan rivers are older than the Himalayas” applies only to antecedent courses that maintained paths while uplift occurred; it is not a blanket age statement for every Himalayan stream. An antecedent river predates the uplifted structure it cuts across.
[Correction] Superimposed drainage means a river established on a former cover surface retains its course while cutting into different underlying structures. It is not an automatic defining feature of all Peninsular rivers.
[Correction] Peninsular rivers do form depositional plains, floodplains, doabs and meanders. The Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri have major deltas; Peninsular floods can be destructive. The contrast is relative, not absolute.

5.1 Meanders, Oxbow Lakes and Channel Change

gentle gradient→outer-bank erosion + inner-bank deposition→meander neck narrows→flood cuts through neck→abandoned loop becomes oxbow lake
[Correction] Kanwar/Kabar Tal (Kabartal) in Bihar is widely described by its Ramsar site information as an oxbow wetland formed through meandering of the Gandak river system. The rough note's specific “Burhi Gandak” attribution and “largest in Asia” superlative are not retained without a consistent authoritative basis. [Verification Required] for any exam source using that superlative.
[Additional] River shifting is natural in low-gradient alluvial plains, especially the Brahmaputra, Kosi and Ganga systems. Embankments can reduce frequent inundation locally but may raise beds, block tributary drainage and worsen damage if breached; floodplain zoning and sediment-aware management are essential.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Lakshadweep: 36 islands, 32 sq km, 12 atolls, three reefs, five submerged banks and ten inhabited islands; Kavaratti is the capital.
  • Nine Degree Channel: Minicoy-rest of Lakshadweep. Eight Degree Channel: Minicoy/Lakshadweep-Maldives.
  • Coral polyps are animals; symbiotic zooxanthellae photosynthesise. Bleaching is stress and pigment/algal loss, not immediate death.
  • Major Indian reef regions: Lakshadweep, Andaman-Nicobar, Gulf of Mannar-Palk Bay and Gulf of Kachchh; Netrani has coral communities.
  • Sriharikota is a barrier island east of Pulicat Lagoon; Pamban lies near Rama Setu; Elephanta is in Mumbai Harbour.
  • Thar aridity reflects subtropical subsidence, continentality, Aravalli alignment and variable monsoon rainfall.
  • Luni is the main river of the Indian desert; Rajasthan has important centripetal/inland drainage and saline lakes.
  • Drainage patterns: dendritic, radial, rectangular, trellis and centripetal.
  • Ganga has India's largest drainage basin; Himalayan/Peninsular comparisons have important exceptions.
  • Antecedent drainage predates uplift; superimposed drainage inherits a course from a removed cover surface.

Mains Points

  • Island resilience: coral health, freshwater security, coastal setbacks, early warning and waste limits are one integrated planning problem.
  • Climate justice: low-lying islands contribute little to warming but face disproportionate bleaching, erosion, salinisation and displacement risk.
  • Thar development: renewable energy, irrigation and hydrocarbons must be balanced with grassland ecology, pastoral livelihoods, groundwater and land degradation.
  • River management: use basin-wide data while implementing soil-water conservation at watershed scale; preserve floodplains and environmental flows.
  • Avoid binaries: Himalayan rivers are not uniformly perennial/flooding and Peninsular rivers are not uniformly seasonal/stable; climate, geology and regulation create regional exceptions.

30-Second Revision

Lakshadweep is a 36-island, 32-sq-km coral-atoll UT: Nine Degree Channel separates Minicoy from its main group; Eight Degree Channel separates it from Maldives. Coral animals depend on photosynthetic algae; heat can cause bleaching, but not instant death. India's principal reef regions also include Andaman-Nicobar, Gulf of Mannar-Palk Bay and Gulf of Kachchh. The Thar is arid because of subtropical conditions, continentality, weak Aravalli uplift and variable monsoon rain; Luni and saline inland basins are key. Drainage follows relief, rock and climate: remember dendritic, radial, rectangular, trellis and centripetal patterns. Himalayan systems are generally larger and perennial, while Peninsular systems are older and more rain-dependent, but antecedence, floods, meanders and sediment contrasts all require exceptions.

UPSC CSE Prelims 2015 - General Studies Paper I

Which one of the following pairs of islands is separated from each other by the “Ten Degree Channel”?

  1. Andaman and Nicobar
  2. Nicobar and Sumatra
  3. Maldives and Lakshadweep
  4. Sumatra and Java

Correct answer: (a) Andaman and Nicobar. This tests channel-based island mapping. Do not confuse Ten Degree (Andaman-Nicobar), Nine Degree (main Lakshadweep-Minicoy) and Eight Degree (Minicoy-Maldives).

Which of the following have coral reefs?

  1. Andaman and Nicobar Islands
  2. Gulf of Kachchh
  3. Gulf of Mannar
  4. Sunderbans

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  1. 1, 2 and 3 only
  2. 2 and 4 only
  3. 1 and 3 only

Correct answer: (a) 1, 2 and 3 only. The Sundarbans are a mangrove-delta ecosystem, not one of India's coral-reef regions.

UPSC CSE Prelims 2021 - General Studies Paper I

With reference to India, Didwana, Kuchaman, Sargol and Khatu are the names of

  1. glaciers
  2. mangrove areas
  3. Ramsar sites
  4. saline lakes

Correct answer: (d) saline lakes. These names relate to arid Rajasthan's enclosed drainage and salt-lake basins.

UPSC CSE Prelims 2013 - General Studies Paper I

The Narmada river flows to the west, while most other large peninsular rivers flow to the east. Why?

  1. It occupies a linear rift valley.
  2. It flows between the Vindhyas and the Satpuras.
  3. The land slopes to the west from Central India.

Select the correct answer using the codes given below:

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 and 3
  3. 1 and 3
  4. None

Correct answer: (a) 1 only. Rift structure controls the Narmada's westward course. Flowing between two ranges describes its position but does not by itself explain direction; Central India's general slope is eastward.

UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - General Studies Paper I

There is no formation of deltas by rivers of the Western Ghats. Why?
