1. The Peninsular Plateau: India's Oldest Landmass

The Peninsular Plateau is a tableland composed of old crystalline, igneous and metamorphic rocks. It formed out of the fragmentation and drifting of the ancient supercontinent Gondwana, and is therefore one of the oldest and most stable landmasses on Earth. It is called "peninsular" because it forms the core of the Indian peninsula, surrounded on three sides by water - the Arabian Sea to the west, the Indian Ocean to the south and the Bay of Bengal to the east.

[Correction] The rough note says "Peninsular land is part of Gondwana land earlier". The precise statement is: the Peninsular block was part of the supercontinent Gondwana (not "Gondwana land" as a separate place). Gondwana comprised present-day South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, Madagascar and the Indian Peninsula. India separated from it and drifted north-east to collide with Eurasia - the collision that raised the Himalaya. The Peninsular block itself was never part of that collision; it acted as a rigid indenter.

[Additional] Terminology that pays in Prelims:
  • Craton - an old, stable block of continental lithosphere. India has five: Dharwar, Bastar, Singhbhum, Bundelkhand and Aravalli (Marwar-Mewar).
  • Shield - the part of a craton where the basement rock is exposed at the surface. Hence "the Indian Shield".
  • Precambrian is not an "era". It is all of geological time before about 541 Ma, and contains the Hadean, Archaean and Proterozoic eons.

2. Rocks of the Peninsula: Getting the Classification Right

The rough notes contain the most common rock-classification confusions in the syllabus. Each is corrected below, because Prelims routinely tests exactly these pairs.

[Correction] "Volcanic rocks -> Eg, Granite" is incorrect. Granite is an intrusive (plutonic) igneous rock - it crystallises slowly deep below the surface, which is why it is coarse-grained. Its extrusive/volcanic equivalent, with the same chemistry but fine grains, is rhyolite. The typical volcanic rock of India is basalt (Deccan Traps), whose intrusive equivalent is gabbro.
[Correction] "Metamorphic rock (Deep rocks), formed by high pressure by the gravitational pull". Metamorphism is caused by heat, pressure and chemically active fluids acting on a pre-existing rock in the solid state. The pressure comes from burial under overlying rock (lithostatic load) and from tectonic stress at plate margins - not from "gravitational pull" as an independent force.

[Additional] Why is the Peninsula mineral-rich? Chain of reasoning worth memorising for Mains:
Ancient crust→repeated magmatism and metamorphism→metallic mineralisation in old crystalline rock→prolonged erosion strips the cover→ore bodies exposed at or near the surface

This is why the Peninsula holds nearly all of India's iron ore, manganese, bauxite, mica, copper, chromite and coal, while the young Himalaya and the alluvial Northern Plains are metallic-mineral poor.

It is the modern analogue of the Gondwana break-up that detached the Indian Peninsula. Sequence to remember: continental rifting → sea-floor spreading → drifting fragments → collision and orogeny.

4. The Central Highlands: Hills and Plateaus

The Central Highlands are a combination of residual hill ranges and lava or sandstone plateaus, tilting generally northwards, so that their rivers - Chambal, Betwa, Ken, Son - flow north-east into the Yamuna and the Ganga.

5. The Aravalli Range

The Aravalli is the oldest fold-mountain range in India and among the oldest in the world, raised during the Precambrian Aravalli-Delhi Orogeny. Prolonged denudation has reduced it to a chain of residual hills - discontinuous, low and rounded - a textbook illustration of an old, dead orogen in contrast to the still-rising Himalaya.

[Additional] A precise way to state the climatic role, because "the Aravalli stops the desert" is too loose for Mains: the range does not physically block sand movement like a wall. It works by (a) reducing near-surface wind velocity, (b) trapping wind-blown sand on its windward flanks, and (c) supporting a vegetation cover that binds soil and raises surface roughness. When mining and construction remove the hills and their vegetation, all three mechanisms fail simultaneously - which is exactly why the Aravalli gaps are the concern, not the intact hills.

6. The Aravalli Under Stress: Desertification and Degradation

Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas resulting from various factors including climatic variations and human activities - the definition used by the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD). Note carefully: desertification is not the physical advance of an existing desert; it is the creation of desert-like conditions in place.

[Exam point] Distinguish three terms that are constantly interchanged in answers:
  • Land degradation - loss of biological or economic productivity of land, anywhere, in any climate.
  • Desertification - land degradation specifically in drylands (arid, semi-arid, dry sub-humid zones).
  • Desert encroachment / sand-dune migration - the physical lateral movement of sand. Only one of several possible mechanisms of desertification.

6.1 Causes

[Correction] The rough note lists "Soil erosion, Land erosion" as separate causes. "Land erosion" is not a technical term. Use soil erosion (sheet, rill, gully and wind erosion) and, where relevant, mass wasting or gullying / ravine formation - the Chambal badlands being India's classic ravine landscape.
[Correction] The note writes "Prolonged drought (El-Nino)". Correct spelling El Nino (El Nino-Southern Oscillation, ENSO). It is a Pacific phenomenon that is statistically associated with weaker south-west monsoon rainfall over India, and therefore with drought years - but the association is not deterministic. Several El Nino years have produced near-normal Indian monsoons.

6.2 Recent Developments on the Aravalli (time-sensitive)

[Time-sensitive - status as of 05 September 2026] The rough note flags "*** Get the details of recent developments on the Aravalli Hills". The verified sequence is:
  1. 20 November 2025 - the Supreme Court issued an order defining an "Aravalli hill" as a landform rising 100 m or more above the surrounding terrain, with an "Aravalli range" being two such hills within 500 m of each other. [Correction] This is the most important correction of the day. The rough note reads: "volcanic plateau (Hotspot, Reunion Hotspot (Near meditarrean sea) -> Magnetite rich lava -> Basalt -> Dark -> Black Sea". Four errors:
  1. The Reunion hotspot lies in the western INDIAN OCEAN, beneath and near Reunion Island, east of Madagascar - nowhere near the Mediterranean Sea. As the Indian plate drifted north over this stationary mantle plume around 66 Ma, the plume head produced the Deccan flood basalts; the plate then moved on, leaving the Laccadive-Maldive-Chagos ridge as a hotspot track and Reunion as the present-day expression.
  2. The lava is not "magnetite rich" as a defining property. Basaltic magma is mafic - rich in magnesium and iron-bearing (ferromagnesian) minerals such as pyroxene and olivine, together with calcium-rich plagioclase feldspar. Magnetite is present only as an accessory mineral.
  3. Basalt is dark because of that ferromagnesian mineralogy - correct in the note, but for the right reason.
  4. "Black Sea" is wrong. The weathering of basalt produces black soil - regur, or "black cotton soil", a Vertisol. The Black Sea is an inland sea between Europe and Asia and has nothing to do with this.
[Additional] Exam-ready facts about the Deccan Traps:
  • "Trap" comes from the Swedish trappa, "stair" - the step-like terraced topography of successive lava flows.
  • Original extent is estimated to have been far larger than the roughly 500,000 sq km that survives today.
  • The eruption straddles the Cretaceous-Palaeogene (K-Pg) boundary and is one of the two leading hypotheses - alongside the Chicxulub impact - for the end-Cretaceous mass extinction.
  • Associated minerals: zeolites, agate, chalcedony; the basalt itself is quarried as road metal and building stone.
  • Hydrogeology: basalt is a poor primary aquifer; groundwater occurs mainly in weathered, jointed and vesicular zones - a key reason for water stress on the Deccan and Malwa uplands.
  • The Tropic of Cancer crosses Madhya Pradesh through this general belt - a useful map anchor.

8. The Bundelkhand Plateau

Bundelkhand occupies the heart of the Central Highlands, straddling the Uttar Pradesh - Madhya Pradesh border. Its defining feature is ancient hard crystalline rock, and almost every one of its problems follows from that single geological fact.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • The Peninsular Plateau is a Gondwana fragment of old crystalline igneous and metamorphic rock, largely Precambrian, tectonically stable.
  • Stratigraphic ladder: Archaean gneiss (>2,500 Ma) → Dharwar (about 3,000-2,500 Ma) → Aravalli Supergroup (2,200-1,800 Ma) → Delhi Supergroup (1,600-900 Ma) / Cuddapah (1,900-1,100 Ma) → Vindhyan → Gondwana coal → Deccan Traps (about 66 Ma).
  • Granite is intrusive; rhyolite is its volcanic equivalent; basalt is the common volcanic rock of India.
  • Three divisions: Central Highlands, Deccan Plateau, Meghalaya (Shillong) Plateau.
  • Narmada-Son lineament separates Central Highlands from the Deccan. Malda Gap (Garo-Rajmahal Gap) separates the Meghalaya Plateau from the Rajmahal-Chota Nagpur block.
  • Narmada and Tapi flow in rift valleys (grabens), westward, forming estuaries, not deltas.
  • Maharashtra and Telangana plateaus are Deccan, not Central Highlands.
  • Aravalli: oldest fold mountains in India; Aravalli-Delhi Orogeny; about 670 km; SW to NE; four units - Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana, Delhi; northern segment = Delhi Ridge.
  • Guru Shikhar, 1,722 m, Mount Abu, Sirohi district - highest peak of the Aravalli and the highest point between the Himalaya and the Nilgiris.
  • Aravalli rivers: Banas, Luni, Sabarmati, Sahibi. Luni rises in the Pushkar valley near Ajmer and ends in the Rann of Kutch. Chambal rises at Janapav, Vindhyan range, near Mhow, MP - not in the Aravalli.
  • Aravalli minerals: Khetri (copper, Jhunjhunu), Zawar (zinc-lead-silver, Udaipur), Makrana (marble, Nagaur), Jhamarkotra (rock phosphate).
  • Sariska Tiger Reserve (Alwar) - first reserve in the world to have tigers successfully relocated after local extinction. Hill Forts of Rajasthan - UNESCO World Heritage Site, 2013.
  • Aravalli dominant tree = Dhok (Anogeissus pendula); Khejri (Prosopis cineraria) = State tree of Rajasthan.
  • Aravalli Green Wall Project - MoEFCC, launched 25 March 2023; 5 km buffer; four States; Phase-I flagged off on World Environment Day, 5 June 2025.
  • Great Green Wall = Africa (African Union, 2007). Three-North Shelterbelt Programme = China (1978). Aravalli Green Wall = India (2023). Do not merge them.
  • UNCCD - adopted 1994, in force 1996, secretariat Bonn; one of the three Rio Conventions with UNFCCC and CBD; COP-14 at New Delhi, 2019 - Delhi Declaration; India's pledge: restore 26 million hectares by 2030.
  • Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas of India is prepared by ISRO - Space Applications Centre, Ahmedabad.
  • Malwa Plateau: Deccan Trap extension; average elevation about 500 m; slopes north; black regur soil; drained by Mahi, Chambal, Betwa, Shipra; Reunion hotspot lies in the Indian Ocean.
  • Bundelkhand: Archaean Bundelkhand Granite; 13 districts (7 UP + 6 MP); rivers Ken and Betwa, both right-bank tributaries of the Yamuna; diamonds at Panna (Majhgawan pipe, NMDC).
  • Ken-Betwa Link Project: approved December 2021; foundation stone 25 December 2024; Daudhan Dam, Panna district, MP; about Rs 44,605 crore; 10.62 lakh ha irrigation, 62 lakh people, 103 MW hydro + 27 MW solar; target 2030; first project under the ILR National Perspective Plan.
  • Rajmahal Traps - Early Cretaceous (about 118 Ma), Kerguelen plume, Jharkhand. Deccan Traps - about 66 Ma, Reunion plume.

Mains Points

  • Stability as an economic asset: the Peninsula's tectonic quiescence and prolonged erosion together explain why India's metallic minerals, coal and heavy industry are concentrated there - geology determines the industrial map.
  • Old orogen versus young orogen: the Aravalli and the Himalaya are the same category of landform - fold mountains - at opposite ends of the erosional life cycle. Use the pair to illustrate the concept of a geomorphic cycle.
  • The Aravalli as ecological infrastructure: its value to Delhi-NCR is not scenic but functional - windbreak, aquifer recharge, dust and heat moderation, wildlife corridor. Framing it as infrastructure converts a conservation argument into an economic one.
  • Definition drives protection: the 2025-26 litigation over what legally counts as an "Aravalli hill" shows that in environmental governance, the definitional boundary is the regulatory boundary. Weak definitions produce lawful degradation.
  • Desertification is a governance failure before it is a climate failure: in the Aravalli belt, anthropogenic drivers dominate and climate acts as a multiplier. Remedies must therefore be primarily regulatory and institutional.
  • Restoration must be ecological, not merely arithmetical: plantation counted in saplings, using exotics such as Prosopis juliflora, can worsen water stress. Native-species, ecosystem-based restoration is the only durable model.
  • Geology sets the development constraint: Bundelkhand's water crisis is written into its Archaean granite. Any solution - interlinking, tanks, watershed treatment - must work with that hydrogeology rather than against it.
  • Interlinking redistributes, it does not create: Ken-Betwa relieves a spatial mismatch at real ecological cost in the Panna core zone. Pair supply augmentation with demand management to write a balanced answer.
  • Learning across drylands: the Sahel, the Three-North Shelterbelt and the Aravalli Green Wall are three responses to one global problem. India's comparative advantage is community institutions - johads, sacred groves such as Mangar Bani, and JFM committees.

30-Second Revision

The Peninsular Plateau is a Gondwana fragment of Precambrian crystalline rock - old, eroded, stable and mineral-rich - the exact opposite of the Himalaya. Its ladder runs Archaean gneiss, Dharwar, Aravalli-Delhi orogeny, Cuddapah and Vindhyan basins, Gondwana coal, and finally the Deccan Traps at about 66 Ma from the Reunion hotspot in the Indian Ocean. It divides into the Central Highlands, the Deccan Plateau and the detached Meghalaya Plateau, separated respectively by the Narmada-Son lineament and the alluvium-filled Malda Gap. In the Central Highlands the Aravalli - India's oldest fold mountains, about 670 km from Gujarat to the Delhi Ridge, peaking at Guru Shikhar, 1,722 m - functions as a windbreak against the Thar, a recharge zone, a leopard corridor from Sariska to Delhi, and a mineral belt at Khetri, Zawar and Makrana. That belt is now failing to desertification driven mainly by mining, construction, overgrazing and groundwater depletion, with drought as a multiplier; the response runs from the Aravalli Green Wall Project (2023, 5 km buffer, four States) and native-species restoration to watershed treatment and the UNCCD (1994, Bonn; COP-14 New Delhi 2019; 26 million ha by 2030). To its east, the basaltic Malwa Plateau gives black regur soil and the Chambal, Mahi and Betwa; further east, granitic Bundelkhand gives Panna diamonds but almost no groundwater - hence chronic drought, and hence the Ken-Betwa Link Project, foundation stone December 2024, Daudhan Dam in Panna, about Rs 44,605 crore, 10.62 lakh hectares of irrigation, targeted for 2030 and contested over the Panna Tiger Reserve.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

UPSC CSE Mains 2022 - General Studies Paper I

Discuss the natural resource potentials of the "Deccan Trap".

Answer direction: Begin with formation - flood basalt from the Reunion hotspot around 66 Ma, step-like terrain, roughly 500,000 sq km surviving. Then the resources: black regur (Vertisol) soil supporting cotton, soybean, jowar and sugarcane; basalt as road metal and building stone; zeolites, agate and chalcedony; bauxite from laterite capping on the traps; groundwater in weathered, jointed and vesicular zones only - hence the limitation; hydropower and irrigation potential of the Godavari-Krishna systems crossing it; and tourism-heritage value at Ajanta and Ellora, cut into these basalts. Directly extends Section 7 of these notes, since the Malwa Plateau is the northern lobe of the same trap.

UPSC CSE Mains 2020 - General Studies Paper I

The process of desertification does not have climatic boundaries. Justify with examples.

Answer direction: The core argument is that desertification is defined by land degradation in drylands, not by a fixed climatic line, and its drivers - deforestation, overgrazing, mining, unsustainable irrigation and salinisation, groundwater depletion - operate across humid, semi-arid and even cold regions. Indian examples that map onto today's notes: the semi-arid Aravalli belt degrading under mining and NCR construction; the Chambal ravines; waterlogging and salinity in canal-irrigated Punjab and Haryana; the cold desert of Ladakh and Spiti; and degradation on the humid Meghalaya plateau from shifting cultivation and rat-hole coal mining. Global parallels: the Sahel and the Gobi-Taklamakan margins. Conclude with land-degradation neutrality and the UNCCD framework.

UPSC CSE Mains 2018 - General Studies Paper I

Define mantle plume and explain its role in plate tectonics.

Answer direction: Define a mantle plume as a narrow, buoyant upwelling of abnormally hot rock rising from deep in the mantle, producing intraplate volcanism at a relatively fixed "hotspot" while the plate moves over it. Role: generation of flood basalt provinces when the plume head first impinges (the Deccan Traps and the Malwa Plateau, about 66 Ma, from the Reunion plume); creation of hotspot tracks that record plate motion (Laccadive-Maldive-Chagos ridge; Hawaii-Emperor chain); contribution to continental rifting and break-up (Gondwana fragmentation; the Kerguelen plume and the Rajmahal Traps); and possible links to mass extinctions at the Cretaceous-Palaeogene boundary. Directly supports the corrected hotspot discussion in Section 7.

UPSC CSE Mains 2017 - General Studies Paper I

In what way micro-watershed development projects help in water conservation in drought-prone and semi-arid regions of India?

Answer direction: Explain ridge-to-valley treatment - contour bunding, staggered trenching, gully plugging, check dams, percolation tanks and farm ponds - and show how each intercepts runoff, extends the time of concentration, raises infiltration and recharges shallow aquifers, while reducing soil erosion and siltation. Use exactly the two regions in these notes: the Aravalli belt of Rajasthan and Haryana, where the traditional johad is the community form of a micro-watershed, and Bundelkhand, where hard Archaean granite means storage must be created at the surface and in the weathered zone rather than deep underground. Add the institutional layer - PMKSY watershed component, MGNREGA convergence and community participation.
