1. ENSO Concluded: La Nina and the Southern Oscillation

1.1 El Nino as a Coupled Phenomenon

  • El Nino has no single cause. It arises from a complex ocean-atmosphere interaction: weaker trade winds let warm water move east, and the warmer east Pacific weakens the trades further (previous note, 4.2).
  • Because the ocean and the atmosphere drive each other, ENSO is called a coupled ocean-atmosphere phenomenon. It is the single largest driver of year-to-year climate variability on Earth, and a major climate driver for the Indian monsoon.

1.2 La Nina

  • Name: Spanish for "the little girl", the counterpart of El Nino ("the little boy", i.e. the Christ Child).
  • When declared: when the central-eastern equatorial Pacific (the Nino 3.4 region) is -0.5°C or colder than normal, sustained over five overlapping three-month seasons. The cold water extends west from the Peru coast along the equator.
  • Mechanism: the SE trade winds become stronger. More warm water piles up in the west, and upwelling off Peru strengthens.
  • The result is a stronger Walker cell: more rising air and rain over Indonesia and the west Pacific, and more sinking air over the east Pacific (previous note, Figure 3).
  • India: La Nina generally favours a normal or above-normal SW monsoon, though not always. In the "triple-dip" La Nina of 2020-22, the monsoon was 109% of the Long Period Average (LPA) in 2020, 99% in 2021 and 106% in 2022 (IMD).
[Correction] Two points in the rough notes:
  • "La Nina meant little girl, from Peruvian fishermen": the meaning is right, but the term was not a fishermen's name. It came into use among scientists in the mid-1980s, popularised by S. G. Philander (1985). Only "El Nino" comes from the fishermen.
  • "Strong monsoon for India" should read "usually a normal or good monsoon". 2021, a La Nina year, was slightly below the LPA (99%).

Theoretical explanation: how La Nina forms, sustains itself and ends

[Additional] Added on request. The physics below is standard ENSO theory. Points not re-checked against a page opened for this note carry †.

Step 1 - Start from the normal state. Along the equatorial Pacific the SE trade winds blow from east to west. They drag the warm surface water (the South Equatorial Current) westward, so a deep pool of warm water (about 28-30°C†) collects near Indonesia, the West Pacific Warm Pool. Sea level there stands slightly higher than off Peru†. Three things follow (Figure 1, top):

  • Thermocline tilt: the thermocline (the boundary between the warm surface layer and the cold deep water) is deep in the west and shallow in the east.
  • Upwelling off Peru: because the thermocline is shallow in the east, the winds that push surface water away from the coast and the equator easily pull up cold, nutrient-rich water. This feeds the cold Peru (Humboldt) Current and its fisheries.
  • Walker cell: warm water in the west means low pressure and rising, rain-making air over Indonesia. Cold water in the east means high pressure and sinking, dry air. Air returns westward at the surface (the trades) and eastward aloft.

Step 2 - La Nina is the normal state exaggerated. It is the cold phase of ENSO, the mirror image of El Nino. Any push that strengthens the trades starts it. Often this is the end of an El Nino (see Step 4).

  • Stronger trades push even more warm water west, so the thermocline tilts more steeply. It sinks further in the west and rises close to the surface in the east.
  • Upwelling off Peru then brings up colder water, and a cold tongue spreads west along the equator. When Nino 3.4 stays at -0.5°C or colder, La Nina is declared (1.2).

Step 3 - Why it sustains itself: the Bjerknes feedback. Jacob Bjerknes (1969) explained ENSO as a positive (self-reinforcing) feedback between ocean and atmosphere. In La Nina it runs like this:

colder east Pacific → larger east-west temperature contrast → higher pressure in the east (Tahiti), lower pressure in the west (Darwin) → stronger trades → more westward warm water and stronger upwelling → even colder east Pacific (the loop repeats)
  • The pressure side of this loop is the positive phase of the Southern Oscillation: a positive SOI (1.3). This is why La Nina is a coupled phenomenon (1.1). The ocean cools because the winds strengthen, and the winds strengthen because the ocean cools.
  • The result is a stronger Walker cell whose rising branch moves west, over the Maritime Continent. This brings heavy rain to Indonesia and eastern Australia and drought to coastal Peru and Ecuador.

Step 4 - Why it ends, and why it often follows El Nino. A positive feedback alone would never stop. Two ideas explain the swing between phases:

  • Recharge-discharge oscillator (F.-F. Jin, 1997): during El Nino, warm water is discharged away from the equator and the equatorial thermocline becomes shallow. This primes the ocean for La Nina, because cold water is now close to the surface. During La Nina, the strong trades slowly recharge warm water into the western Pacific, which later sets up the next El Nino.
  • Ocean waves (delayed oscillator): wind changes send slow underwater waves (westward Rossby and eastward Kelvin waves) across the basin. When these reflect back months later, they reverse the thermocline anomaly in the east.
  • Seasonal timing: like El Nino, La Nina usually peaks around November-January and weakens in the northern spring. Spring is also when forecasts are least reliable, the "spring predictability barrier"†.
  • Asymmetry: La Nina often lasts two or three years ("double-dip" or "triple-dip", as in 2020-23), while El Nino usually lasts about one year. This is because the ocean recharges slowly†.

Step 5 - Why La Nina usually helps the Indian monsoon (teleconnection).

  • In El Nino the rising branch of the Walker cell moves east, to the central Pacific. Its sinking branch moves over the Indo-west Pacific and India, which suppresses clouds and weakens the monsoon.
  • In La Nina the rising branch is strong and stays over the Maritime Continent and the eastern Indian Ocean. Air ascends over the region, there is no extra subsidence over India, and the monsoon trough and low-pressure systems stay active. The usual result is a normal or above-normal monsoon (2020: 109%; 2022: 106%).
  • It is a tendency, not a rule. A negative IOD, an unfavourable MJO or local factors can cancel it. 2021 was a La Nina year at 99% (c01).
  • Other Indian effects†: La Nina winters tend to be colder in north India, with more cold waves. The NE (winter) monsoon over Tamil Nadu tends to be weaker in La Nina and stronger in El Nino, the opposite of the SW monsoon.

1.3 The Southern Oscillation

  • The Southern Oscillation (SO) is a see-saw of surface air pressure across the tropical Pacific and Indian Ocean. When pressure is high at Darwin (northern Australia, the west), it is low at Tahiti (the central-east Pacific), and vice versa.
  • Discovery: Sir Gilbert Walker, head of the IMD, identified and named it in the 1920s while trying to forecast the Indian monsoon after the famines of the 1870s-1890s. Jacob Bjerknes showed in the 1960s that the SO and El Nino are two sides of one system.
  • Southern Oscillation Index (SOI): the standardised pressure difference Tahiti minus Darwin. The Australian Bureau of Meteorology (BoM) uses the Troup SOI, multiplied by 10. A sustained SOI below about -7 often indicates El Nino; one above about +7 is typical of La Nina.
  • Phases: neutral, the warm phase (El Nino, negative SOI) and the cool phase (La Nina, positive SOI). These are the three phases of the whole ENSO cycle.
[Correction] The rough notes say "El Nino, also called the Southern Oscillation". They are not two names for one thing:
  • El Nino is the ocean part: the warming of the equatorial Pacific.
  • The Southern Oscillation is the atmospheric part: the Tahiti-Darwin pressure see-saw.
  • Together they form ENSO (El Nino-Southern Oscillation). The "normal, warming and cooling phases" are the phases of ENSO.

2. Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)

  • What it is: the difference in sea-surface temperature between the western tropical Indian Ocean (off East Africa) and the eastern tropical Indian Ocean (off Sumatra and Java). It is often called the "Indian Nino".
  • Discovery: Saji, Goswami, Vinayachandran and Yamagata (Nature, 1999).
  • Measurement: the Dipole Mode Index, the west pole (50-70°E, 10°S-10°N) minus the east pole (90-110°E, 10°S-0°) (Map 1). The BoM threshold is ±0.4°C, sustained for about 8 weeks.
  • Timing (BoM): IOD events usually start around May-June, peak in August-October, and decay quickly when the monsoon arrives in the southern hemisphere (about December).

Theoretical explanation: how the IOD develops

[Additional] Added on request. The physics below is standard IOD theory (Saji et al., 1999; BoM). Points not re-checked against a page opened for this note carry †.

Step 1 - The Indian Ocean is set up differently from the Pacific.

  • The Indian Ocean has no steady easterly trade winds along the equator. Over the year the equatorial winds are weak and mostly westerly, and they reverse with the monsoon.
  • So the warm water and the deeper thermocline lie in the east, near Sumatra and Java. Normally the eastern Indian Ocean is the warmer side, and there is no permanent cold tongue as there is off Peru.
  • The only seasonal upwelling in the east occurs off Sumatra and Java during June-October. There, south-easterly winds blow along the coast and push surface water offshore.

Step 2 - The trigger (May-June). A positive IOD begins when those south-easterly winds off Sumatra-Java become unusually strong. Two things can cause this:

  • El Nino: its shifted Walker circulation puts sinking air and high pressure over Indonesia, strengthening the easterly winds over the eastern Indian Ocean.
  • Causes within the Indian Ocean itself: for example, as in 2019, which had no El Nino. This is why the IOD can occur on its own (2.1).

Step 3 - Growth through the Bjerknes feedback (June-September). The same ocean-atmosphere loop that drives ENSO now operates in the Indian Ocean (Figure 3, top):

stronger south-easterlies off Sumatra-Java → more upwelling, so the east cools → higher pressure and less convection in the east → anomalous easterly winds along the equator → warm surface water pushed west; thermocline rises in the east and deepens in the west → east cools further, west warms (the loop repeats)
  • The result is a positive Dipole Mode Index: the west pole is warmer than the east by more than 0.4°C (Map 1).
  • The Indian Ocean's own Walker-type circulation changes. Air rises over the warm western Indian Ocean and East Africa, bringing rain and floods there. It sinks over Indonesia and Australia, bringing drought and bushfire risk.

Step 4 - The negative IOD is the reverse (Figure 3, bottom). Westerly wind anomalies strengthen along the equator. They pile warm water against Sumatra-Java, deepen the thermocline in the east and suppress upwelling there. The west becomes relatively cool. Convection and rain move over Indonesia and Australia, and East Africa and the western Indian Ocean turn drier.

Step 5 - Peak and decay (September-December).

  • The IOD peaks in September-October. It collapses in about November-December, when the winds reverse.
  • Two things end it. The ITCZ moves south, and the Australian (north-west) monsoon begins. The winds along Sumatra-Java then turn north-westerly, which shuts off the upwelling and breaks the feedback loop.
  • So the IOD is locked to the season: it exists only in about June-December†. ENSO, by contrast, can persist for a year or more. The Indian Ocean basin is also smaller, so the IOD signal is weaker and harder to forecast than ENSO.

Step 6 - Why a positive IOD helps India's SW monsoon.

  • Warmer western Indian Ocean and Arabian Sea: more evaporation, so the south-westerly monsoon flow carries more moisture towards India.
  • Convection shifts west: the rising branch moves to the western and central Indian Ocean, closer to India. The sinking branch moves to the eastern Indian Ocean and Indonesia. This pattern produces anomalous moisture convergence and rising air over the Indian subcontinent.
  • It offsets El Nino: Ashok et al. (2001, Geophysical Research Letters) showed that a positive IOD weakens the inverse El Nino-monsoon link. This explains why 1997 (very strong El Nino plus positive IOD) still gave a near-normal monsoon (about 102%).
  • 2019: a strong positive IOD without El Nino. The monsoon was 110% of LPA (IMD). The same event was linked to floods in East Africa and to Australia's 2019-20 bushfires†.
  • A negative IOD does the reverse. It keeps convection over Indonesia and tends to weaken the monsoon, especially when it coincides with El Nino.
  • NE monsoon†: because the IOD peaks in October, a positive IOD also tends to enhance October-December rain over Tamil Nadu and south peninsular India.

2.1 How the IOD and ENSO Interact

  • The rough notes say "El Nino: chance of positive IOD; La Nina: chance of negative IOD". This is a tendency, not a rule. BoM says a positive IOD often, but not always, coincides with El Nino, and a negative IOD can often occur during La Nina. Both are linked through the Walker circulation.
  • Examples:
    • 1997: a very strong El Nino with a strong positive IOD. The monsoon was about normal (about 102%), because the positive IOD offset the El Nino.
    • 2019: a strong positive IOD without El Nino. This shows the IOD can develop independently.
    • 2026: BoM reports a positive IOD alongside a very strong El Nino (13 November 2026). IMD (12 November) still classed the IOD as neutral ([Verification Required]).

3. How IMD Forecasts the Monsoon

  • Two forecasting systems are used together:
    • A statistical ensemble forecasting system, based on past relationships with predictors such as ENSO, the IOD and ocean temperatures.
    • A dynamical system. Since 2021 IMD has used a Multi-Model Ensemble (MME) of coupled ocean-atmosphere climate models from several global centres. This includes IMD's own Monsoon Mission Climate Forecasting System (MMCFS).
  • Two stages: a first forecast in mid-April and an update in late May (for example 15 April and 27 May in 2025). The update adds forecasts for each region and month.
  • Accuracy (PIB): the average absolute forecast error was 2.28% of the LPA in 2021-24, compared with 7.5% in 2017-20.
  • National Monsoon Mission: launched by the Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES) in 2012. It is executed and coordinated by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM), Pune. Phase 1 ran from 2012 to 2017 and phase 2 started in October 2017.
  • US link: MoES signed an MoU with the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). The MMCFS is adapted from NOAA's NCEP Climate Forecast System version 2 (CFSv2).
[Correction] The rough notes say "IMD follows the dynamic model of forecasting". IMD uses both statistical and dynamical models. The dynamical part has been a multi-model ensemble since 2021. The NOAA collaboration is correct: the MMCFS is built on NOAA's CFSv2 under a MoES-NOAA MoU, through the Monsoon Mission run by IITM Pune.
[Additional] Extended-range forecasts: for 2-4 weeks ahead, IMD tracks the MJO and ENSO/IOD status. Its 12 November 2026 bulletin reported very strong El Nino conditions, a neutral IOD and the MJO in phase 3.

4. Examine: "The Southern Oscillation and the IOD Are Major Climatic Drivers of the SW Monsoon"

The class asked for this discussion. Below is a Mains-style answer frame.

Introduction: the SW monsoon gives India about 75% of its annual rainfall †. Its year-to-year variation is driven largely by two ocean-atmosphere systems: ENSO (with the Southern Oscillation) in the Pacific and the IOD in the Indian Ocean.

Arguments in favour:

  • The SO is the strongest single predictor. Walker discovered it while trying to forecast the monsoon. Most major droughts (2002, 2009, 2015, 2026) were El Nino years, and La Nina years (2020, 2022) were good.
  • The IOD modifies ENSO. A positive IOD offset a very strong El Nino in 1997. Both the SO and the IOD are core inputs to IMD's statistical and dynamical forecasts (section 3).
  • Both act on the monsoon's basic engine: the pressure contrast and moisture supply between the ocean and the subcontinent.

Limits (why "major", not "sole"):

  • The link is probabilistic: 1997 was normal despite El Nino, and 2021 was slightly below normal despite La Nina.
  • Other drivers:
    • The MJO and monsoon intraseasonal oscillations decide active and break spells.
    • Eurasian and Himalayan snow cover † matters, as do the Tibetan heating and jets (13 November note).
    • Atlantic influences † also play a part.
    • Human factors such as aerosols, land-use change and warming (section 6).
  • Regional rainfall can diverge from the national total. In 2025 the all-India total was 108% of the LPA, but NE India got only 80%.

5. Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO)

  • What it is (NOAA): an eastward-moving disturbance of clouds, rainfall, winds and pressure that travels around the tropics. It returns to its starting point in about 30-60 days, moving at about 4-8 m/s.
  • It has two halves: an enhanced (wet) phase with low pressure, rising air and heavy rain, and a suppressed (dry) phase ahead of and behind it. Named after Roland Madden and Paul Julian (1971-72) †.
  • Eight phases, numbered by where the wet envelope is: phases 2-3 over the Indian Ocean, 4-5 over the Maritime Continent (Indonesia), 6-7 over the western Pacific, and 8 and 1 over the western hemisphere and Africa.
  • Monsoon effect:
    • When the wet phase is over the Indian Ocean during the monsoon, India tends to get active spells with good rainfall. Low-pressure systems and cyclones form more easily.
    • When it is over the Pacific, the Indian region tends to be in the suppressed phase, with weak or break spells.
  • Time scale: it affects weeks within a season (sub-seasonal or intraseasonal), unlike ENSO and the IOD, which shape the whole season. This is the class point "temporary / weekly effect".
  • [Additional] In summer, the intraseasonal signal over India also moves northward from the equator to the Himalaya in bands. This is called the Boreal Summer Intraseasonal Oscillation (BSISO) †, and it is the main driver of active-break cycles.
[Correction] The rough notes describe the MJO as a "moving low pressure system of tropical waters". The MJO is an atmospheric disturbance: a pulse of clouds, rain, wind and pressure that moves over tropical oceans, not a feature of the water. It does interact with the ocean: its westerly wind bursts excite oceanic Kelvin waves that travel east beneath the surface, along the thermocline †. These waves can help trigger El Nino.

6. Human (Anthropogenic) Influences on the Monsoon

6.1 Global Warming: Land, Ocean and Ice

  • Climate change, pollution, deforestation, concretisation and wetland encroachment are all linked to global warming: land and ocean heating, and melting ice in Tibet and the Himalaya.
  • Ice-albedo feedback: as snow and ice melt, darker rock and soil are exposed. These absorb more sunlight (lower albedo), which causes more heating. This feeds back on Tibetan heating, which matters for the monsoon (13 November note, 3.1).
  • Ocean warming: a warmer Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean supply more moisture. That favours tall cumulonimbus clouds and very heavy, short bursts of rain, including cloudbursts.
[Verification Required] The rough notes link Himalayan and Tibetan ice loss and lower albedo to monsoon change. The albedo mechanism is standard physics, but no assessment checked for this note quantified its effect on the monsoon. Treat it as a plausible mechanism, not a measured result.
[Additional] Cloudburst (IMD): about 100 mm of rain or more in one hour over a small area of about 20-30 sq km. It is common in the Himalaya, where moist monsoon air is forced up steep slopes (orographic lifting). It causes flash floods and landslides (see PYQ, Mains 2024).

6.2 More Intense Rain, Fewer Moderate Days

  • MoES, Assessment of Climate Change over the Indian Region (2020):
    • Daily rainfall extremes of more than 150 mm/day rose by about 75% over central India (1950-2015).
    • Dry spells became more frequent: 27% higher in 1981-2011 than in 1951-1980. Wet spells became more intense.
    • June-September rainfall declined by about 6% (1951-2015), especially over the Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Western Ghats.
  • Roxy et al. (2017, Nature Communications): found a threefold rise in widespread extreme rain events over central India from 1950 to 2015. The cause was increasingly variable low-level westerly winds over the Arabian Sea, which deliver surges of moisture.
  • Consequences: rain arrives in fewer, heavier bursts, so flash floods and prolonged dry spells (drought) both become likelier. Overall the monsoon becomes more erratic.
  • IPCC AR6 (Working Group I, chapter 8): the South Asian monsoon weakened in the second half of the 20th century, mainly due to human-made aerosols, which outweighed the increase expected from greenhouse gases. Monsoon rainfall is projected to increase with further warming, along with more extremes.
[Correction] The rough notes credit an "IMD report" with the finding that single-day rain intensity has increased. The key figures come from the MoES (2020) climate assessment, which was led by IITM Pune with IMD data: +75% in extremes over 150 mm/day over central India and +27% in dry spells. The claim "number of rainy days decreased" is widely stated but was not confirmed in an official source checked for this note: [Verification Required].

6.3 Shifts in Season and Region

  • Season: the rough notes say early June gets less rain and the rains continue into October. Later withdrawal is supported. IMD's 2020 revised normals moved the start of withdrawal from north-west India from 1 September to 17 September. In 2026, June got only 65% of its LPA while September got 92% (IMD).
  • Region (IMD state-wise trend reports, January 2020, 1989-2018):
    • Significant decreasing monsoon rainfall trends in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Nagaland.
    • NE India has been unusually dry recently. In 2025, East and NE India got 1,089.9 mm, the 2nd lowest since 1901 (80% of LPA for NE India). In 2026 it got 1,017.1 mm, the lowest since 1901.
    • Rajasthan: no significant seasonal trend, but more heavy-rain days in south-east Rajasthan, Saurashtra and Kutch. In 2025 NW India got 127% of its LPA.
  • Western disturbance (WD)-monsoon interaction: when a WD from the west meets monsoon moisture over the western Himalaya, extreme rain can result.
    • Himachal Pradesh, 7-10 July 2023: rainfall was 436% above normal (IMD Shimla).
    • Punjab, August-September 2025: one of the worst floods since 1988. By 8 September all 23 districts, about 2,050 villages and nearly 20 lakh people were affected, with an official death toll of 48 (later reports above 55). Heavy rain in the Himachal and J&K catchments and releases from the Bhakra, Pong and Ranjit Sagar dams drove it. The monsoon trough, WDs and cyclonic systems were cited as weather drivers.
[Correction] Two refinements to the rough notes:
  • "Early June less rain, more rain till October" is not an established long-term trend. Later withdrawal is reflected in IMD's revised normals, and a weak June (65% in 2026) is a single-year example.
  • "Rajasthan more rains than the past": IMD finds no significant seasonal trend for Rajasthan, only more heavy-rain days in its south-east, plus individual wet years such as 2025. "NE states less than the past average" is supported.

6.4 Air Pollution (Aerosols)

  • Asian Brown Cloud: a UNEP report (2002, led by Ramanathan and Crutzen) described a layer of aerosols (black carbon, sulphates, dust) over South Asia. It dims sunlight at the surface, keeping the land cooler. That weakens the land-sea temperature contrast and the low pressure over India, and so the monsoon.
  • Bollasina et al. (2011, Science) attributed the observed decline in monsoon rainfall in the second half of the 20th century mainly to human-made aerosols. IPCC AR6 agrees (6.2).
  • IITM Pune COVID-19 lockdown study (Fadnavis et al., 2021, Environmental Research Letters, a model study): lower emissions in 2020 let about 4 W/m² more sunlight reach the surface over parts of South Asia. This strengthened the monsoon, raising rainfall by about 4% over India and 5-15% over central India.
  • Nuance: absorbing aerosols (black carbon, dust) heat the air over the Tibetan Plateau and Himalayan foothills. This may strengthen early-season rain (the "elevated heat pump" hypothesis, Lau et al., 2006 †), while overall dimming weakens the monsoon.
[Correction] The rough notes say "Asian brown haze - moderate LP on Indian monsoon - weaker monsoon (early June)". The corrected meaning: the haze weakens the low pressure over the land by dimming sunlight, which weakens the monsoon overall, not only in early June. The usual name is the Asian Brown Cloud (Atmospheric Brown Cloud). The IITM study during COVID found that the cleaner air strengthened the monsoon, which is the same mechanism seen in reverse.

6.5 Deforestation, Wetlands and Urbanisation

  • Deforestation: fewer trees mean less transpiration, so less moisture is recycled into rain and the monsoon weakens. Paul et al. (2016, Scientific Reports) used a model comparing India's land use in 1987 and 2005. They found deforestation weakened monsoon rainfall through less evapotranspiration and less recycled rainfall.
  • Wetlands: wetlands supply evaporation and store floodwater. Filling them in means less local moisture † and higher flood peaks (Mumbai's mangroves and the Mithi river; 12 November note).
  • Concretisation and urbanisation:
    • Concrete and asphalt trap heat, creating an urban heat island. This is a warmer microclimate with a local low pressure that can draw in moist air and intensify downpours.
    • Sealed surfaces turn rain into instant runoff, causing urban floods.
    • Mumbai, 26 July 2005: 944 mm in 24 hours at Santacruz, against only 73 mm at Colaba about 27 km away. The rain itself came mainly from large weather systems interacting with local coastal features. The damage came from encroachment on floodplains, mangroves and the Mithi river.

6.6 Discussion: How Human Changes in Land Use Affect the Monsoon

The class asked for this discussion. Below is a Mains-style answer frame (compare PYQ, Mains 2015).

forests cleared, wetlands filled, cities sealed, air polluted→less evapotranspiration + altered surface heating + aerosol dimming→weaker land-sea contrast and less recycled moisture→weaker, more erratic seasonal rain→but local heat islands + warmer seas intensify extremes
  1. Weaker seasonal monsoon: deforestation and wetland loss reduce recycled moisture (Paul et al., 2016). Aerosols reduce surface heating (Bollasina et al., 2011; IPCC AR6). Together they help explain the 6% decline from 1951 to 2015 (MoES 2020).
  2. More extremes: urban heat islands, warmer seas and moisture surges bring heavier bursts, cloudbursts and urban floods. Extremes above 150 mm/day rose 75% over central India.
  3. Changed rainfall distribution: more dry spells (+27%), decline over the Indo-Gangetic Plains and NE India, and more heavy-rain days in parts of the north-west.
  4. Feedbacks: lost vegetation raises surface temperature and lowers soil moisture, reinforcing drought. Ice loss lowers albedo in the Himalaya.
  5. Counter-point: natural drivers (ENSO, the IOD, the MJO) still dominate year-to-year variation, and model studies differ in how large the land-use effect is. Human influence acts as a trend and amplifier.
  6. Way forward:
    • Afforestation and protecting catchment forests.
    • Wetland conservation, such as the Ramsar sites and the Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 †.
    • Sponge-city design, permeable surfaces and restored urban drains.
    • Clean air under NCAP †.
    • Climate-resilient agriculture and better impact-based forecasting.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • ENSO = El Nino (ocean) + Southern Oscillation (atmosphere). It is a coupled phenomenon and the largest driver of year-to-year climate variation.
  • Southern Oscillation: a Tahiti-Darwin pressure see-saw, discovered by Gilbert Walker (IMD) in the 1920s. SOI = Tahiti minus Darwin. A sustained SOI below -7 means El Nino; above +7 means La Nina (BoM).
  • La Nina ("little girl"): Nino 3.4 at -0.5°C or colder; stronger trades; stronger Walker cell; usually a good monsoon. 2020: 109%, 2021: 99%, 2022: 106% of LPA.
  • ENSO and IOD theory: both grow by the Bjerknes feedback (Bjerknes, 1969): SST contrast → pressure contrast → winds → thermocline and upwelling → larger SST contrast. La Nina = steeper Pacific thermocline, colder Peru upwelling, stronger Walker cell; phase swings explained by the recharge-discharge oscillator (Jin, 1997). Positive IOD = easterly anomalies, upwelling off Sumatra-Java; it decays by about December when the winds reverse (1.2, 2).
  • IOD ("Indian Nino"; Saji et al., 1999): west pole 50-70°E minus east pole 90-110°E. Positive means the west, off East Africa, is warmer, which favours the monsoon. Develops May-June, peaks August-October.
  • IMD forecasting: statistical + dynamical MME (since 2021), including the MMCFS. Two stages (April, late May). Monsoon Mission 2012 (MoES, run by IITM Pune). The MMCFS is based on NOAA's CFSv2.
  • MJO: eastward-moving; 30-60 days; 4-8 m/s; 8 phases (2-3 Indian Ocean, 4-5 Maritime Continent, 6-7 W Pacific). Over the Indian Ocean it brings active spells; over the Pacific, breaks.
  • Cloudburst: 100 mm or more in one hour over about 20-30 sq km.
  • MoES 2020: extremes over 150 mm/day up 75% over central India (1950-2015); dry spells up 27%; June-September rain down about 6% (1951-2015).
  • Asian Brown Cloud (UNEP 2002): aerosols dim the land, weakening the monsoon. The IITM COVID study found cleaner air brought about 4% more rain.
  • Season totals (IMD): 2025 was 108% of LPA (NE India 80%); 2026 was 87% (NE region lowest since 1901).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • El Nino is not "also called" the Southern Oscillation; they are the ocean and atmosphere halves of ENSO.
  • Negative SOI = El Nino; positive SOI = La Nina. The sign is the opposite of the sea-temperature anomaly.
  • "La Nina" was coined by scientists in the 1980s; only "El Nino" comes from the Peruvian fishermen.
  • The IOD is within the Indian Ocean (west versus east), not Indian Ocean versus Pacific (Prelims 2017).
  • Positive IOD = warm WEST (off East Africa), not west Africa. A positive IOD with El Nino is common, but not a rule (2019 had one without El Nino).
  • The Indian Ocean normally has its warm pool in the EAST (off Sumatra-Java), unlike the Pacific whose cold side is the east. A positive IOD reverses this: the east cools by upwelling.
  • The thermocline is shallow in the east in La Nina (more upwelling, colder Peru waters); in El Nino it flattens and deepens in the east.
  • IMD uses statistical and dynamical models, not only dynamical ones. MMCFS stands for Monsoon Mission Climate Forecasting System.
  • The MJO is atmospheric and moves east. Its effect lasts weeks, not a whole season.
  • Aerosols weaken the monsoon overall; the cleaner air of the COVID lockdown strengthened it.
  • The +75% extreme-rain figure applies to central India, events over 150 mm/day, 1950-2015 (MoES 2020).
  • Mumbai 2005 was not caused by the heat island alone. The rain came from weather systems; the damage came from encroachment.

30-Second Revision

  • ENSO = El Nino + SO (Tahiti-Darwin; negative SOI means El Nino). La Nina: strong trades, strong Walker cell, usually a good monsoon (2020: 109%).
  • IOD: positive (warm west) helps the monsoon; negative hurts it. It often pairs with ENSO but not always (1997 offset, 2019 independent).
  • Theory: Bjerknes feedback drives both ENSO and the IOD. La Nina = normal state exaggerated (steep thermocline, cold upwelling, strong Walker cell; often multi-year). IOD = seasonal (June-December), killed by the wind reversal.
  • IMD: statistical + MME (MMCFS, from NOAA's CFSv2); Monsoon Mission 2012, IITM Pune; error 2.28% (2021-24).
  • MJO: eastward, 30-60 days. Over the Indian Ocean it brings active spells; over the Pacific, breaks.
  • Human factors: aerosols, deforestation and wetland loss weaken the season (6% decline). Warm seas and heat islands bring extremes (+75%), cloudbursts and urban floods. Dry spells are up 27%. NE India is declining.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2024 - General Studies Paper I (10 marks)

What is the phenomenon of 'cloudbursts'? Explain.

Answer approach:
