1. Spatial Framework: From the Central Highlands to the Deccan

The Narmada rift valley is the structural divide between the Central Highlands to the north and the Deccan Plateau to the south. From west to east, the Central Highlands include Malwa, Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand and continue towards Chota Nagpur; south of the rift lie the Satpura uplands and the triangular Deccan tableland.

2.2 Chota Nagpur Plateau

The Chota Nagpur Plateau is a dissected eastern extension of the Peninsular block, centred on Jharkhand and extending into adjoining Odisha, West Bengal and Chhattisgarh. Its major surfaces include the Ranchi and Hazaribagh plateaus, with the Damodar trough between them.

  • Rock and minerals: ancient granite-gneiss and the Singhbhum Craton, crossed by Gondwana sedimentary troughs. Coal occurs in the Damodar valley (Jharia, Bokaro, Raniganj region); iron ore in Singhbhum; copper along the Singhbhum shear zone; mica around Koderma; bauxite on plateau caps.
  • Drainage: the Damodar is the key industrial river; Subarnarekha, North Koel and South Koel also drain the plateau. Waterfalls form where streams descend plateau scarps.
  • Ecology and society: sal-dominated forests and large Adivasi populations, including Munda, Oraon, Ho and Santhal communities.
  • Rajmahal Hills: lie in north-eastern Jharkhand and consist mainly of Early Cretaceous Rajmahal Trap basalt; they are an eastward physiographic continuation but are geologically distinct from the old Singhbhum basement.
[Correction] "Most oldest rocks" is incorrect English and too absolute. Use: the Singhbhum Craton contains some of India's oldest exposed crustal rocks. Claims about the single oldest Indian rock depend on dating method and sample and should not be reduced to a ranking.
[Correction] The Santhals are an important Adivasi community of Jharkhand and the Rajmahal-Santhal Pargana region, but Chota Nagpur is not a one-community "Santhal belt". Munda, Oraon, Ho and several other communities are equally important.
[Correction] Rajmahal Hills are a landform and geological province, not merely a "historic place". Their exam value comes from Rajmahal volcanism, the Ganga bend, the Malda Gap and the Santhal Pargana location.
[Additional] The plateau's mineral-energy base explains the concentration of heavy industry around Jamshedpur, Bokaro, Dhanbad and the Damodar valley: coal + iron ore + water + rail connectivity. It also explains mining-related displacement, forest fragmentation, subsidence and river pollution.

3. Vindhya-Satpura Structure and the Narmada Rift Valley

3.1 Vindhyan Range

The Vindhyan highland forms a discontinuous escarpment north of the Narmada, extending mainly across Madhya Pradesh and towards eastern Gujarat, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar through associated uplands. It is composed predominantly of nearly horizontal Proterozoic sandstone, shale and limestone.

[Correction] Calling the Vindhya a conventional folded mountain range is misleading. Much of its relief is a structural escarpment and residual highland produced by faulting and differential erosion of old sedimentary strata.

3.2 How the Narmada Rift Valley Formed

Ancient Narmada-Son crustal weakness→tensional faulting and repeated reactivation→central crustal block subsides→graben / rift trough→Narmada occupies the west-draining linear valley
[Additional] The Narmada rift did not form in one simple event. It follows an ancient crustal lineament repeatedly reactivated during Gondwana rifting, Deccan volcanism and later intraplate tectonics. Sediment accumulated in the trough while differential erosion sharpened the Vindhya-Satpura relief.
[Correction] "Between Vindhya and Satpura - block shape" should be stated as: the Narmada occupies a linear graben between the Vindhyan escarpment to the north and the Satpura upland to the south. The bordering ranges are not both simple textbook horsts along their entire length.

3.3 Satpura Range

  • Runs broadly east-west south of the Narmada, forming part of the northern rim of the Deccan Plateau and much of the MP-Maharashtra divide.
  • Western part includes the Rajpipla hills; central part includes the Mahadeo Hills; eastern continuation includes the Maikal Hills.
  • Dhupgarh (Dhoopgarh), 1,350 m, near Pachmarhi, is the highest point of the Satpura Range and Madhya Pradesh.
  • Amarkantak lies on the Maikal uplands near the MP-Chhattisgarh border. It is associated with the sources of the Narmada and Son (and the Johila), and forms part of the Achanakmar-Amarkantak Biosphere Reserve.
[Correction] The spelling is Dhupgarh / Dhoopgarh, not "Dhugarh". Amarkantak is not itself one of the two Satpura "hills"; it is a plateau-pilgrimage town in the Maikal uplands. The Narmada flows west and the Son north-east, making it a major water divide.
[Correction] "Old block mountains now known as hills due to erosion" is an oversimplification. Satpura relief reflects faulting, uplift, resistant rock and long denudation; parts are fault-block uplands, but the whole range should not be assigned a single origin.

4. Deccan Plateau and its Regional Units

The Deccan Plateau is the triangular Peninsular tableland south of the Narmada, bounded broadly by the Western Ghats, Eastern Ghats and Satpura-Maikal uplands. Its general tilt is west to east, which explains the long east-flowing Godavari, Krishna, Pennar and Kaveri systems.

5.1 Model 250-Word Answer: Discuss the Deccan Traps

The Deccan Traps are one of Earth's largest continental flood-basalt provinces and form much of west-central Peninsular India. They were produced mainly around 66 million years ago, near the Cretaceous-Palaeogene boundary, when vast volumes of mafic lava erupted through fissures. Their formation is widely linked to the Reunion mantle plume as the Indian Plate moved rapidly northward. Repeated lava sheets cooled into horizontally stacked basalt flows; later erosion created characteristic step-like or "trap" topography.

The province is most extensive in Maharashtra and adjoining Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat and Karnataka. Its northern lava extension underlies much of Malwa, while basalt also covers large areas of Saurashtra. Weathering of basalt under suitable conditions produced clay-rich black regur soil, supporting cotton, soybean, millets, sugarcane and a major textile-agro-industrial economy. Basalt provides building stone and road metal; vesicular cavities yield zeolites, agate and chalcedony. Lateritic caps may host bauxite. Rivers such as the Godavari and Krishna originate in the Western Ghats and cross the trap country, supporting irrigation and hydropower. Ajanta and Ellora add exceptional cultural value.

However, the plateau has serious constraints. Basalt has low primary porosity, so groundwater is concentrated in weathered, fractured, jointed and vesicular zones. Uneven monsoon rainfall, hard-rock aquifers, water-intensive crops, soil erosion and recurrent drought create water stress. Quarrying, urban growth and habitat fragmentation impose further costs. Thus the Deccan Traps demonstrate how one geological event shaped India's soils, drainage, farming, minerals, settlement and heritage, while also setting strict hydrogeological limits. Sustainable watershed management, aquifer-based planning, micro-irrigation and crop diversification are essential.

6. Western Ghats: Geological Past and Physiography

The Western Ghats, or Sahyadri, are the high, mostly continuous western escarpment of the Peninsular Plateau. They run roughly 1,600 km parallel to the western coast through Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, generally 30-50 km inland. The major interruption is the approximately 30 km-wide Palghat Gap near 11 degrees N.

[Correction] UNESCO describes the Western Ghats-Sri Lanka region as one of the world's eight "hottest hotspots" of biological diversity. This does not mean "the eighth hottest hotspot" or a heat ranking.
[Correction] UNESCO inscribed the serial property Western Ghats on the World Heritage List in 2012 under criteria (ix) and (x). The property has 39 component parts grouped in seven sub-clusters, not the entire 1,600 km range as one continuous heritage property.

6.1 Geological Evolution

Gondwana breakup→India-Madagascar separation and western-margin rifting→faulting + uplift of plateau edge→Deccan volcanism blankets northern sector→long erosion and scarp retreat→present Ghats and short west-flowing streams
[Correction] The Western Ghats were not created solely by Deccan volcanism. They are primarily a rifted, uplifted and eroded continental margin escarpment associated with the breakup of Gondwana and opening of the western Indian Ocean. Deccan basalt later covered and modified the northern Sahyadri; southern sectors expose much older crystalline rocks.
[Correction] "The western part of the Western Ghats subsided along the fault" is too simple. Rifting produced relative uplift, faulting and subsidence along India's western margin; subsequent denudation caused scarp retreat. The present coastal margin reflects several structures and cannot be represented by one fault block.
[Correction] India's western coast is often described in school geography as a submerged coast, explaining estuaries and natural harbours, whereas the east coast is more depositional and deltaic. In scientific use, however, the Konkan, Kanara and Malabar sectors show varied tectonic and sea-level histories; do not treat every coastal feature as proof of uniform submergence.

6.3 Physical and Geographical Aspects of the Western Ghats

Length and widthApproximately 1,600 km long and commonly about 30-50 km inland from the coast. The mountain belt and associated uplands vary greatly in width; the UNESCO landscape covers a much broader area than the narrow crest line.

Elevation patternAverage elevations are commonly about 900-1,600 m but vary considerably. Heights are generally lower in parts of the central Ghats and rise again in the Nilgiri-Anaimalai-Cardamom massifs. Anamudi, 2,695 m, is the highest point.
ContinuityThe Western Ghats are more continuous than the Eastern Ghats. They are crossed by passes and gaps, especially Thal Ghat, Bhor Ghat, Goa Gap and the major Palghat Gap.
Geological characterNorthern Sahyadri is dominated by horizontally layered Deccan basalt, producing flat-topped summits and stepped slopes. Southern sectors expose ancient gneiss, granite, charnockite, schist and other crystalline rocks, producing rounded massifs and high plateaus.
Drainage divideThe crest forms the principal water divide of Peninsular India. Short, steep rivers flow west to the Arabian Sea, while longer rivers and tributaries flow east across the Deccan to the Bay of Bengal.
ClimateHeavy orographic rainfall occurs on the windward western slopes. Rainfall decreases sharply across the crest, creating a leeward rain-shadow over the interior Deccan. Temperature falls with altitude, allowing montane climates within tropical latitudes.
SoilsLateritic soils occur widely under intense weathering and leaching in high-rainfall zones; red and loamy forest soils occur in crystalline terrains; black soil is associated with basaltic areas east of and within the northern Ghats. Valley bottoms contain deeper alluvial and colluvial material.
VegetationWindward lowlands support tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen forests; intermediate rainfall zones support moist deciduous forests; eastern rain-shadow slopes grade into dry deciduous and thorn vegetation. High southern massifs contain shola forests in sheltered valleys and grasslands on exposed upper slopes.
Human geographyDense settlement and major ports occupy the coastal side, while passes connect them with Deccan cities and markets. Plantation belts produce tea, coffee, rubber and spices. Forest-dependent and Adivasi communities, hydropower projects, tourism centres and rapidly urbanising hill settlements share the landscape.

6.3.1 Northern, Central and Southern Regional Divisions

  • Northern Western Ghats / Sahyadri: extend through Gujarat and Maharashtra to the Goa region. Deccan basalt produces mesa-like summits, steep scarps, deeply cut valleys and flat-topped fort hills. Thal and Bhor Ghats provide strategic transport routes between Mumbai and the Deccan interior.
  • Central Western Ghats: extend mainly through Goa and Karnataka. They are locally called the Sahyadri or Malnad highlands. Heavy rainfall feeds the Mandovi, Kali, Sharavathi and Netravati systems; evergreen forests, laterite surfaces and dissected relief are prominent.
  • Southern Western Ghats: include the Nilgiri, Anaimalai, Palani, Cardamom and Agasthyamalai massifs across Kerala and Tamil Nadu. These contain the greatest elevations, major shola-grassland systems and exceptionally high biological endemism. The Palghat Gap separates the Nilgiri block from the Anaimalai-Palani highlands.
[Additional] These three divisions are useful for explanation but their boundaries vary among authors. Treat them as broad physiographic regions, not formal administrative units.

6.3.3 Orographic Rainfall and the Rain Shadow

Moist air arriving from the Arabian Sea encounters the steep western escarpment and is forced to rise. Expansion causes cooling, condensation and heavy rainfall on the windward side. After crossing the crest, the air descends towards the Deccan Plateau, compresses and warms, lowering its relative humidity. This creates the drier leeward belt of interior Maharashtra and Karnataka and contributes to drought risk farther east in Telangana and Rayalaseema.

[Additional] Relief does not act alone. Rainfall also varies with the direction of monsoon winds, distance from the sea, local gaps, elevation and the movement of depressions from the Bay of Bengal. The rain shadow is therefore a broad pattern, not a uniformly dry strip.

6.3.4 Gaps as Transport and Climate Corridors

  • Thal Ghat: carries the Mumbai-Nashik route towards northern Maharashtra.
  • Bhor Ghat: carries the Mumbai-Pune route and historically linked the port economy with the Deccan interior.
  • Goa Gap: a lower central section influencing transport and the transition between northern and central biological communities.
  • Palghat Gap: the broadest major break; carries important road, rail and power corridors between Kerala and Tamil Nadu and permits air exchange between the humid coast and the Coimbatore interior.

These corridors lowered the transport barrier created by the escarpment. They therefore influenced the location of roads, railways, market towns, migration routes and cultural exchange between the western coast and inland plateau.

6.3.5 Soils, Farming and Plantation Geography

High rainfall causes strong chemical weathering and leaching, often leaving iron- and aluminium-rich lateritic material. Tea prefers cooler high slopes with acidic, well-drained soils; coffee is concentrated especially in the shaded uplands of Karnataka and adjoining states; rubber favours warm, humid lower elevations; cardamom and other spices thrive in moist southern highlands. Paddy occupies suitable valleys and terraced fields.

[Correction] Plantation crops are not naturally occurring "gifts" of the Ghats. Their distribution reflects a combination of altitude, temperature, rainfall, soil drainage, shade, labour, colonial estate history, transport and markets. Plantation expansion can also replace natural forest and simplify habitats.

6.3.6 Settlement, Transport and Economic Geography

The steep scarp historically separated Arabian Sea ports from plateau interiors. Pass routes helped Mumbai connect with Pune and Nashik and enabled movement between Kerala and the Coimbatore region. Today roads, railways, tunnels, transmission lines and pipelines concentrate in these corridors. Hill stations such as Mahabaleshwar, Ooty, Kodaikanal and Munnar developed around cooler climates, plantations and tourism.

Hydropower uses high rainfall and steep gradients, while reservoirs transfer water across the divide for cities, industry and irrigation. This creates an important geographical tension: water generated in ecologically sensitive headwaters is demanded by densely populated coastal and interior regions. Basin planning must therefore consider environmental flows, sediment, downstream users and cumulative dam impacts.

6.3.7 Why the Western Ghats are More Continuous than the Eastern Ghats

The Western Ghats form a relatively coherent rift-margin escarpment along the raised western edge of the plateau. The Eastern Ghats are much older, lower and more discontinuous because the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri have cut broad passages through them and long erosion has reduced their relief. This difference explains why the Western Ghats form a stronger monsoon barrier and a more definite drainage divide.

Integrated exam point: Link the full geographical chain: rift-margin escarpment → steep west and gentle east slopes → orographic rain and leeward rain shadow → short west-flowing and long east-flowing rivers → evergreen forests and plantation belts west, drought-prone farming east → passes controlling transport and settlement → intense pressure on a narrow biodiversity-rich mountain corridor.

7.4 Model Answer: Physiography, Significance and Challenges of the Western Ghats

The Western Ghats are an approximately 1,600 km-long escarpment parallel to India's west coast, extending through six states. They are an ancient, rifted continental-margin feature, older than the Himalaya. Their western face is steep, while the eastern slope merges gradually with the Deccan Plateau. Basalt dominates the northern Sahyadri and older crystalline rocks the south. Important breaks include Thal Ghat, Bhor Ghat and the Palghat Gap; major uplands include the Nilgiri, Anaimalai, Palani, Cardamom and Agasthyamalai hills. Anamudi (2,695 m) is the highest peak.

The Ghats intercept the Arabian Sea monsoon, regulate Peninsular climate and feed major rivers, earning the description "water tower of Peninsular India". Their elevation and rainfall gradients created exceptional endemism; the Western Ghats-Sri Lanka region is among the world's eight hottest biodiversity hotspots. They support hydropower, plantation crops, medicines, fisheries, tourism and the livelihoods of forest-dependent communities. UNESCO inscribed 39 component sites as a World Heritage property in 2012.

These services face pressure from quarrying, mining, linear infrastructure, dams, unplanned tourism, urban growth, plantation expansion and habitat fragmentation. Road cutting, drainage alteration and forest loss interact with extreme monsoon rainfall to intensify landslides. Climate change adds heat, rainfall uncertainty and species-range shifts.

A balanced response requires landscape-level cumulative assessment, strict protection of high-value natural ecosystems, ecological restoration with native species, slope-sensitive infrastructure, carrying-capacity limits and local participation under forest-rights safeguards. The Gadgil panel's graded, decentralised approach and Kasturirangan panel's mapped natural-landscape approach provide complementary lessons: conservation boundaries must be scientifically credible, socially negotiated and legally enforceable.

8.1 Baghelkhand Plateau: Why Location Controls its Economy

[Additional] Baghelkhand is a transitional upland between Bundelkhand to the west, the Ganga plain and Kaimur scarp to the north, the Maikal-Amarkantak highlands to the south and the Chota Nagpur-Chhattisgarh region to the east. This location places it across the upper and middle Son drainage system. The Son and its tributaries dissect the sandstone and limestone surface, creating scarps, valleys and isolated tablelands rather than one uniformly flat plateau.

Its economy follows its geology. Vindhyan limestone supplies raw material for cement plants, while Gondwana sedimentary basins around Singrauli contain coal. Coal, reservoir water and rail links encouraged a major thermal-power and industrial complex. The same concentration creates environmental costs: opencast mining removes vegetation and overburden, ash ponds and industrial discharge threaten water quality, and reservoirs and mines can displace settlements. Thus Baghelkhand is a useful example of the chain geology → mineral location → industrial concentration → regional growth and ecological pressure.

8.2 Chota Nagpur Plateau: India's Mineral-Industrial Core

[Additional] Chota Nagpur is not one level surface. It is a collection of plateaus, scarps and basins produced by repeated uplift and prolonged river dissection. The Ranchi and Hazaribagh plateaus are separated broadly by the Damodar trough, while the Pat lands form higher, flat-topped western surfaces. Rivers crossing resistant rock descend through waterfalls, including Hundru on the Subarnarekha and Dassam on the Kanchi.

Two geological settings occur close together. Very old crystalline rocks and banded iron formations contain iron ore, copper, mica and bauxite; younger Gondwana troughs contain coal. Their proximity reduced the cost of bringing bulky coal and ore together and helped Jamshedpur, Bokaro, Dhanbad and nearby centres develop iron and steel, engineering and power industries. The Damodar Valley Corporation later added multipurpose dams, electricity and flood moderation.

The plateau is also a cultural landscape, not an empty mineral store. Adivasi communities have long depended on forests, shifting and settled cultivation, grazing and minor forest produce. Mining leases, dams and industry can overlap customary land. Good answers should therefore connect mineral development with the Fifth Schedule, land rights, rehabilitation, forest conservation, mine closure, subsidence control and pollution of the Damodar system.

8.3 Vindhyan Range: An Escarpment Rather than a Young Mountain Chain

[Additional] Vindhyan rocks accumulated as sediments in a very old basin and were later compacted into sandstone, shale and limestone. Because many beds remained comparatively horizontal, later uplift and faulting did not create a Himalayan-style folded chain. Rivers and weathering removed softer layers faster than resistant sandstone, leaving steep scarps, flat-topped mesas and residual hills.

The range is important as a watershed and cultural divide. North-flowing tributaries such as the Chambal, Betwa and Ken ultimately join the Yamuna-Ganga system, whereas the Narmada occupies the structural trough immediately south. Vindhyan limestone supports cement production, sandstone is widely used as building stone, and the Panna region is known for diamonds associated with igneous pipes cutting the sedimentary succession.

8.4 Narmada Rift Valley: Structure Before Drainage

[Additional] The essential idea is that the valley existed as a zone of crustal weakness before the modern river acquired its course. Normal and oblique fault movement lowered parts of the central block relative to adjoining uplands. This produced a long, narrow structural depression. The Narmada then followed the easiest available westward route through that depression, so it is a structurally controlled river.

The rift explains several connected facts: a remarkably straight valley, steep bordering scarps, a narrow alluvial tract, westward drainage across the general eastward tilt of Peninsular India and an estuary at the Gulf of Khambhat. The valley also contains thick sediments that preserve fossils and records of Quaternary environmental change. Because old faults can be reactivated, the Narmada-Son lineament is tectonically more significant than the popular phrase "stable Peninsular block" may suggest.

Process distinction: a rift valley forms mainly through crustal extension and fault displacement; an ordinary river valley forms mainly through fluvial erosion. Once the Narmada occupied the rift, river erosion and sedimentation further modified it, but they were not the original structural cause.

8.5 Satpura Range and Amarkantak: Upland, Watershed and Refuge

[Additional] Satpura is a broad belt of dissected uplands rather than one narrow ridge. Fault-bounded blocks, resistant volcanic and crystalline rocks, and long erosion produced separate hill groups. The Mahadeo Hills around Pachmarhi occupy the central sector; the Maikal uplands rise farther east towards Amarkantak.

Elevation and forest cover make the Satpura-Maikal belt a major ecological corridor between western and eastern central India. It includes tiger landscapes and dry-to-moist deciduous forests. Its watershed role is equally important: streams flow north to the Narmada, south towards the Tapi-Godavari systems, and east or north-east from Amarkantak. The Narmada begins westward and the Son north-eastward from the Amarkantak region, illustrating how a small highland can separate drainage towards opposite sides of the subcontinent.

8.6 Deccan Plateau: Why Most Rivers Flow East

[Additional] The Deccan is asymmetrical. Its western rim is high and lies close to the Arabian Sea, while the plateau surface descends gradually towards the Bay of Bengal. Consequently, Godavari, Krishna, Pennar and Kaveri travel long distances eastward and develop broad drainage basins and deltas. West-flowing rivers are generally shorter, steeper and more suitable for hydropower, but have smaller catchments.

The plateau's apparent unity hides geological diversity. The north-west is dominated by Deccan basalt; Karnataka exposes Dharwar crystalline rocks and greenstone belts; Telangana has granite-gneiss terrain and Gondwana rocks in the Godavari valley; Rayalaseema includes granites and Cuddapah basin rocks; Bastar rests on a very old craton. Therefore soils, aquifers, minerals and farming differ sharply from one sub-region to another.

Karnataka Plateau: Malnad and Maidan

Malnad means hill country. It is the wetter, forested western highland adjoining the Ghats, with plantation crops and many river headwaters. Maidan is the lower and more open eastern plateau, where rainfall decreases and tanks, reservoirs and groundwater become more important. The Dharwar geological belts host iron, manganese and historic gold mineralisation. This west-east contrast is a direct result of relief and monsoon exposure.

Telangana Plateau: Tanks on Hard Rock

Much of Telangana consists of jointed granite and gneiss. Such rocks possess little primary pore space, so groundwater is stored mainly in weathered mantles and fractures. Traditional chains of tanks captured monsoon runoff and transferred overflow from one tank to the next. Their restoration is therefore not only heritage conservation but adaptation to hard-rock hydrogeology and variable rainfall.

Rayalaseema Plateau: A Drought-Prone Interior

Rayalaseema lies beyond the strong rain-bearing influence of the Western Ghats and often receives low, variable rainfall from both monsoon systems. High evaporation, seasonal streams, thin soils in places and heavy dependence on groundwater magnify drought. Watershed treatment, tank revival, micro-irrigation and crops suited to dry conditions are more durable than relying only on deeper borewells.

Bastar Plateau: Forests, Minerals and Rights

Bastar combines old crystalline rocks, forested uplands and the Indravati drainage system. Bailadila's high-grade iron ore illustrates the mineral wealth of the Bastar Craton, while Abujmarh's rugged relief and forests have limited transport access. The same landscape supports Adivasi livelihoods and biodiversity. Development policy must therefore evaluate cumulative forest loss, secure free and informed participation through applicable legal processes, share benefits, rehabilitate mined land and avoid treating mineral extraction as the only development pathway.

8.7 Deccan Traps: From Mantle Process to Human Geography

[Additional] A flood-basalt eruption differs from a familiar cone volcano. Lava emerges repeatedly from long fissures and spreads as extensive, low-viscosity sheets. Thousands of flows can build a thick volcanic province. In the Deccan, later erosion cut through these layers, leaving flat summits and staircase-like slopes. Hard compact flow interiors alternate with more porous weathered tops and vesicular zones.

This layered structure controls groundwater. Rain cannot be assumed to soak evenly into solid basalt. It enters through joints, fractures, weathered flow contacts and vesicles, creating local aquifers separated by dense rock. A well may be productive while a nearby deeper well is dry. Watershed and groundwater plans must therefore map individual flows and fractures rather than treat the entire basalt province as one underground reservoir.

Black soil forms when basalt weathers and clay minerals accumulate on gentle surfaces with restricted drainage. Its shrink-swell clays hold moisture and crack deeply in dry weather. This favours cotton but can damage building foundations and roads. The soil is naturally productive in some respects, yet fertiliser balance and water management remain necessary; "black" does not automatically mean uniformly fertile.

8.8 Saurashtra Plateau: A Basaltic Peninsula with Radial Drainage

[Additional] Saurashtra projects into the Arabian Sea and is bounded by the Gulfs of Kachchh and Khambhat. Deccan basalt covers much of the region, while the Gir upland forms an important relief and ecological centre. Short seasonal rivers drain outward towards different coasts, giving a broadly radial tendency around uplands.

The Gir landscape is the last wild stronghold of the Asiatic lion. Conservation success has increased the importance of maintaining dispersal corridors beyond the protected core and managing disease, wells, roads and human-wildlife interaction. Water scarcity and variable rainfall make check dams, groundwater recharge and drought-resilient agriculture major regional concerns.

8.9 Western Ghats: Escarpment, Monsoon Barrier and Biodiversity Engine

[Additional] The Ghats' steep western face rises close to the sea. Moist south-west monsoon winds are forced upward, cool and condense, producing heavy windward rain. After crossing the crest, air descends, warms and becomes relatively dry, creating the rain-shadow interiors of Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana and Rayalaseema. Relief therefore links coastal rainfall directly to interior drought.

North-south variation is equally important. The northern Sahyadri is largely a deeply cut Deccan-basalt landscape with flat-topped forts and stepped slopes. South of the major gaps, ancient gneiss, charnockite and other crystalline rocks dominate higher massifs such as Nilgiri and Anaimalai. Altitude produces montane shola forest-grassland mosaics, while warm wet lowlands support evergreen forests.

Isolation on separate massifs and sharp changes in rainfall and elevation allowed populations to evolve independently, producing high endemism. Amphibians and freshwater fish are especially sensitive because many occupy small stream or forest ranges. A road, quarry or dam may therefore fragment the entire known habitat of a narrowly distributed species even when the area cleared appears small.

Why the Gaps and Passes Matter

Thal and Bhor Ghats became transport corridors because they offer practicable routes through the steep northern escarpment. Palghat is much wider and lower: it channels road and rail traffic, allows exchange of air between Kerala and Tamil Nadu, and separates the Nilgiri from the Anaimalai massifs. Gaps therefore influence transport, wind, rainfall, settlement and even biological distribution.

Why West- and East-Flowing Rivers Differ

West-flowing streams begin near the crest and reach the Arabian Sea quickly, producing rapids, waterfalls and estuaries. East-flowing rivers descend gently across a much wider plateau, receive many tributaries, carry sediment farther and build large deltas. The contrast explains both the hydropower potential of Sharavathi and Koyna and the irrigation-delta importance of Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri.

8.10 Western Ghats Conservation Committees: The Real Difference

[Additional] The debate is not simply "Gadgil protects and Kasturirangan develops". Both recognised ecological fragility, but they used different units and governance philosophies. Gadgil began with the entire mountain system and graded sensitivity from ESZ-1 to ESZ-3, emphasising local democratic decision-making. Kasturirangan separated natural from cultural landscapes using geospatial analysis and concentrated restrictions on the mapped natural portion.

The policy difficulty is that ecological processes do not stop at a forest boundary. Wildlife movement, streams, slope stability and cumulative quarry impacts can cross from mapped natural land into farms, plantations and settlements. Conversely, a rule designed without livelihood consultation can create resistance even when ecologically justified. Effective governance therefore needs transparent village-level maps, ground verification, cumulative rather than project-by-project assessment, disaster-risk zoning, livelihood safeguards and predictable compensation.

Answer-writing conclusion: combine the ecological caution and decentralisation of the Gadgil approach with the spatial mapping and livelihood differentiation of Kasturirangan, while updating both with climate-risk and cumulative-impact data.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Baghelkhand: north-eastern MP, Son basin, Kaimur-Vindhyan rim; Singrauli coalfield crosses MP-UP.
  • Chota Nagpur: centred on Jharkhand; Ranchi-Hazaribagh plateaus; Damodar trough; Singhbhum iron and copper; Jharia-Bokaro coal.
  • Rajmahal Hills: Early Cretaceous basalt in north-eastern Jharkhand, geologically distinct from Deccan Trap basalt.
  • Vindhya north - Narmada rift - Satpura south. Narmada flows west through a fault-guided graben and forms an estuary.
  • Dhupgarh, 1,350 m: highest Satpura and MP peak. Amarkantak: Narmada-Son water divide in Maikal uplands.
  • Deccan slopes broadly west to east; most major rivers flow to the Bay of Bengal.
  • Malnad = wetter western Karnataka highland; Maidan = drier eastern plateau.
  • Dandakaranya is broader than Bastar Plateau. Bailadila iron ore and Abujmarh are Bastar anchors.
  • Deccan Traps: flood basalt, about 66 Ma, Reunion plume association; trap means stair; black regur develops from basalt weathering under suitable conditions.
  • Malwa and Saurashtra contain Deccan basalt but are not jointly a standard "Western Plateau" division.
  • Western Ghats: about 1,600 km, six states; major break = Palghat Gap, about 30 km wide near 11 degrees N.
  • Doddabetta, 2,637 m = highest Nilgiri peak. Anamudi, 2,695 m = highest Western Ghats and Peninsular India peak.
  • Nilgiri: India's first biosphere reserve (1986); UNESCO MAB network (2000).
  • UNESCO Western Ghats: inscribed 2012 under criteria (ix) and (x); 39 components in seven sub-clusters.
  • Gadgil 2011: entire Ghats as ESA with ESZ-1/2/3 and decentralised governance. Kasturirangan 2013: about 37% natural landscape as ESA.

Mains Points

  • Geology writes the economic map: Singhbhum ores + Damodar coal + river water produced the Chota Nagpur heavy-industry belt.
  • Structure controls drainage: the Narmada's rift-guided westward flow contrasts with the consequent eastward rivers of the tilted Deccan surface.
  • Basalt is both opportunity and constraint: regur and building stone support production, but hard-rock aquifers make groundwater uneven and drought management essential.
  • Western Ghats are ecological infrastructure: monsoon interception, headwaters, carbon storage, slope stability, pollination and biodiversity are economic services, not decorative values.
  • Hazards are co-produced: extreme rain becomes disaster when quarrying, deforestation, road cuts and blocked drainage reduce slope stability.
  • Conservation must join science and consent: Gadgil supplies graded local governance; Kasturirangan supplies mapped differentiation. Neither works without gram sabha participation and enforceable land-use rules.
  • Bastar needs rights-based development: mineral extraction must incorporate Forest Rights Act safeguards, rehabilitation, benefit sharing and cumulative ecological assessment.

30-Second Revision

North-east MP's Baghelkhand follows the Son and Singrauli; farther east, Chota Nagpur combines Singhbhum's ancient ores with Damodar's Gondwana coal. The Vindhya-Narmada-Satpura sequence is structural: Vindhyan escarpment north, a down-faulted Narmada graben in the middle and Satpura upland south; Dhupgarh is the Satpura high point and Amarkantak the Narmada-Son divide. South lies the east-tilted Deccan: basaltic Maharashtra, crystalline Karnataka-Telangana-Rayalaseema and forested mineral-rich Bastar. The Deccan Traps are Reunion plume-related flood basalts about 66 Ma old, producing stair relief and black soil but uneven hard-rock groundwater. The Western Ghats are a 1,600 km rift-margin escarpment across six states, highest at Anamudi, broken notably by Palghat Gap. They intercept the monsoon, feed Peninsular rivers and form a biodiversity hotspot. UNESCO inscribed 39 component sites in 2012. Gadgil (2011) proposed graded ESZ-1/2/3 over the entire Ghats; Kasturirangan (2013) mapped about 37% natural landscape as ESA.

UPSC CSE Mains 2022 - General Studies Paper I

Discuss the natural resource potentials of the "Deccan Trap".

Answer direction: Begin with flood-basalt geology and the Reunion plume. Organise resources as black regur agriculture; basalt road/building stone; zeolite-agate-chalcedony; lateritic bauxite; groundwater in weathered, jointed and vesicular zones; Godavari-Krishna irrigation and hydropower; and Ajanta-Ellora heritage. End with water stress and sustainable watershed-aquifer management.

UPSC CSE Mains 2021 - General Studies Paper I

Differentiate the causes of landslides in the Himalayan region and Western Ghats.

Answer direction: Himalaya: young rising fold mountains, active faults and earthquakes, weak folded rock, steep relief, glacier-river undercutting plus roads and deforestation. Western Ghats: old but deeply weathered escarpment, intense monsoon saturation, laterite and soil-mantled slopes, quarrying, road cuts, drainage blockage and plantation/settlement change. Both are worsened by extreme rainfall and unscientific construction, but the tectonic setting differs.

UPSC CSE Mains 2018 - General Studies Paper I

Define mantle plume and explain its role in plate tectonics.

Answer direction: Define a plume as a buoyant upwelling of anomalously hot mantle material. Explain plume-head flood basalt and plume-tail hotspot tracks; use the Deccan Traps-Reunion system as the Indian example. Link plumes cautiously to continental doming, rifting, intraplate volcanism and records of plate direction and speed.

UPSC CSE Mains 2013 - General Studies Paper I

What do you understand by the theory of continental drift? Discuss the prominent evidences in its support.

Connect India directly: the Peninsular block was part of Gondwana, separated and moved north, while western-margin rifting and later Reunion plume-related volcanism helped shape the Deccan and Western Ghats. Conclude that plate tectonics supplied the mechanism Wegener lacked.
