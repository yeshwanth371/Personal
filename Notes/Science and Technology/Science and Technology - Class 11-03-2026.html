42. Polar Orbit and Remote Sensing Satellites (completes Section 40.3)

42.1 Polar orbit

  • A polar orbit passes over or near both poles; as the Earth rotates beneath it, the satellite sees a new strip on each pass and covers the whole globe in a few days.
  • Class figure: the satellite goes from one pole to the other in about 1.5 hours. Checked: about 90-100 minutes is the time for one full orbit (pole to pole and back) at 500-900 km; one pole-to-pole half takes about 45-50 minutes (c13).
  • Remote sensing satellites are placed in this orbit (mostly in its sun-synchronous form, Section 40.2 of the 2 Nov note). They are of two types: optical / passive and microwave / active (c14).

[Correction] "From one pole to another pole in around 1.5 hr": 1.5 hours (about 90-100 minutes) is the orbital period of a low polar orbit - a complete circuit through both poles. Write "completes one orbit, pole to pole and back, in about 90-100 minutes (about 14-15 orbits a day)". NISAR, for example, flies at 747 km (NASA quick facts).

[Correction] "Optical = passive" and "microwave = active" is a useful classroom pairing but not a strict rule. Passive means the sensor records energy it does not produce: besides reflected sunlight this includes thermal infrared (heat emitted by the Earth, which can be recorded at night, as INSAT weather imagers do) and passive microwave radiometers. Active means the sensor sends its own signal: radar/SAR, and also lidar (laser). So write "optical sensors that depend on reflected sunlight cannot image at night or through cloud".

[Additional] Spatial vs spectral resolution: "resolution" can mean the size of the smallest ground area one pixel records (spatial: 42 m, 0.9 m) or the width of each wavelength band (spectral). Hyperspectral = high spectral resolution, not necessarily high spatial resolution. The class definition ("the range is narrow and the bands are contiguous") is correct.

42.4 Microwave (active) remote sensing

  • Microwave satellites do not rely on reflected sunlight; they carry their own source of radiation, which lets them work 24/7 under all weather conditions. Examples: RISAT and NISAR.
  • [Additional] Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR): a side-looking radar that uses the satellite's motion to "synthesise" a very long antenna, giving fine resolution from a small real antenna.
  • [Additional] Bands: X-band (about 3 cm; Drishti, many commercial SARs), C-band (about 5.6 cm; RISAT-1/1A), S-band (9.4 cm on NISAR), L-band (24 cm on NISAR) (NASA). The longer the wavelength, the deeper the signal penetrates vegetation, dry soil, snow and ice; shorter wavelengths respond to finer surface detail.

43.2 Why a dawn-dusk orbit?

A dawn-dusk orbit is a special sun-synchronous polar orbit whose plane follows the day-night boundary (terminator): the satellite passes over each place at about local sunrise or sunset. The class gave two reasons:

  1. Sunlight for the solar panels - the satellite stays in sunlight for almost the whole orbit, so power is steady (important for a power-hungry radar). Checked as a general property of dawn-dusk orbits.
  2. To avoid ionospheric disturbance - the ionosphere's electron content is highest around midday and early afternoon and lowest around dawn; a charged ionosphere delays and distorts radar signals and rotates their polarisation (Faraday rotation), and the effect is strongest at lower frequencies such as L-band (MDPI review). NASA notes the S-band signal is less sensitive to ionospheric disturbances (NASA). Whether this was a stated design reason for NISAR's orbit was not confirmed in the sources read [Verification Required] (c15).

[Correction] "Ionospheric disturbance which receives the peak around noon and causes dispersion of radars": the ionosphere's electron content typically peaks in the early afternoon (local time), not exactly at noon. The ionosphere is a dispersive medium (its effect depends on frequency), so it introduces delays, phase errors and polarisation rotation in radar signals rather than "dispersing the radar". Keep the idea: dawn and dusk passes face a quieter ionosphere.

Class statement: "The L band radar provides vertical penetration whereas S band radar is for horizontal coverage." Checked (c16):

[Correction] "L-band for vertical penetration, S-band for horizontal coverage" is an oversimplification. L-band does penetrate deeper, but S-band is not a "coverage" radar: both bands image the same wide swath, and the S-band is chosen for its sensitivity to light vegetation and crops and to India-specific science (coasts, ocean winds, geology) (NASA). ISRO's first S-band products include a national forest above-ground biomass map and a rabi-season active crop cover map (ISRO, 17 Aug 2026). Write "L-band: deeper penetration; S-band: sensitivity to lighter vegetation and surface features; together they cover a wide range of changes".

43.4 SweepSAR: wide swath without losing resolution

  • A conventional SAR has to choose between a wide swath (coarser detail) and fine resolution (narrow strip). NISAR uses SweepSAR to get both - a swath of about 240 km at high resolution (ISRO).
  • Hardware: a 12 m gold-plated wire-mesh reflector on a 9 m boom, and an array of feed elements on the instrument structure below it. The feed sends the pulse up to the reflector, which focuses it down to illuminate the whole swath (NASA).
  • Scan-on-receive: echoes return first from the near edge of the swath and later from the far edge. As they arrive, the reflector focuses each part of the echo onto a different feed element, so the echo "sweeps" across the feed array. The onboard electronics pick the elements on which the echo is falling at each instant and combine them into one high-gain receive beam that follows the echo across the swath.
  • This is what the class meant by "the reflected radar moves across the cross section of the antenna to enable the computer to pick up the relevant data". [Correction] The echo moves across the feed array (the elements under the reflector), not across "the antenna" in general (c17).

[Correction] "It has an onboard antenna along with a radar": more precisely, NISAR's antenna is an array-fed reflector - a large reflector plus a feed array - shared by both radars, which NASA describes as "SweepSAR scan-on-receive wide swath mapping systems" (NASA).

43.5 Uses

Class list: monitoring polar ice caps, changes in landforms, biomass estimation, coastal landforms, agricultural patterns. [Additional] Also earthquakes, volcanoes, landslides and land subsidence (centimetre-scale ground movement), soil moisture, wetlands and surface water, sea ice, oil spills and disaster response (NASA; eoPortal; ISRO).

44.2 Why combine optical and SAR?

  • Optical imagery: very fine, easy-to-read images, but blocked by clouds and unusable at night.
  • SAR: works 24/7 in all weather, but its images are harder to read than a photograph.
  • Old way: two separate satellites, imaging at different times and angles; their images then have to be matched (co-registered) on the ground - slow, delaying decisions, especially on the battlefield.
  • Drishti: both sensors image the same place at the same time from the same geometry, and the onboard SyncFusion system aligns and fuses the two into one analysis-ready product (eoPortal). Developers claim up to three times more actionable information than a single-sensor satellite (India Today).

46. Earth Observation Satellites in the Geostationary Orbit

46.1 Characteristics of the geostationary orbit

  • Altitude: almost 36,000 km - precisely about 35,786 km above the equator.
  • Period: almost 24 hours - precisely one sidereal day, about 23 h 56 min - so the satellite remains fixed over a place on the equator.
  • Coverage: 3 satellites spaced about 120 degrees apart can cover most of the world except the polar regions (beyond roughly 81 degrees latitude, a geostationary satellite is below the horizon).
  • Use for EO: a permanent watch over one region - meteorological services, disaster management (cyclones, floods, fires), agricultural monitoring and security.

[Additional] Geostationary vs geosynchronous: every geostationary orbit is geosynchronous (24-hour period), but only one with zero inclination over the equator stays fixed in the sky. ISRO describes EOS-05 as its first imaging satellite "from Geosynchronous orbit" (ISRO).

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Polar LEO: one orbit in about 90-100 min (14-15 a day); whole-globe coverage; mostly sun-synchronous.
  • Passive sensors record reflected sunlight (or emitted heat); active sensors (radar/SAR, lidar) send their own signal. SAR = day-night, all-weather.
  • Multispectral: few broad bands with gaps. Hyperspectral: hundreds of narrow, contiguous bands; identifies materials.
  • NISAR: NASA (L-band SAR, 24 cm; 12 m reflector) + ISRO (S-band SAR, 9.4 cm; bus; launch); GSLV-F16, 30 Jul 2025; dawn-dusk SSO, 747 km, 98.4 degrees; swath about 242 km; 12-day repeat (about 6-day average sampling); SweepSAR; first dual-frequency (L + S) radar satellite; data free (ASF; Bhoonidhi).
  • Mission Drishti: GalaxEye (Bengaluru, IIT Madras alumni, 2021); 3 May 2026, Falcon 9, Vandenberg; 190 kg; SSO about 500 km; world's first OptoSAR (X-band SAR + 7-band multispectral); onboard SyncFusion; 8-12 satellite constellation planned. Precursor: GLX-SQ on POEM-4 (PSLV-C60, Dec 2024).
  • SBS-III: CCS Oct 2024; Rs 26,968 crore; 52 satellites (21 ISRO + 31 private); LEO and GEO; complete by 2029; operated by the Defence Space Agency (est. 2019; DG Air Vice Marshal).
  • GEO: 35,786 km; period 23 h 56 min; 3 satellites cover all but the poles (beyond about 81 degrees).
  • INSAT-3D / 3DR / 3DS: meteorology; 3DS on GSLV-F14, 17 Feb 2024 (6-channel imager, 19-channel sounder).
  • GISAT-1 (EOS-03): lost, GSLV-F10, 12 Aug 2021 (cryogenic stage). EOS-05 (GISAT-1A): GSLV-F17, Sep 2026, India's first imaging satellite from geosynchronous orbit; 42 m multispectral, 191/318 m hyperspectral; India every 30 min, selected areas every 5 min.

Mains Points

  • Why SAR matters for India: the monsoon cloud cover and night-time limit optical imaging when floods, cyclones and border incidents need watching most; SAR (RISAT, NISAR, Drishti) gives day-night, all-weather coverage.
  • NISAR as a model of cooperation: cost- and technology-sharing (NASA's L-band and reflector; ISRO's S-band, bus and GSLV), open data policy, and Indian science priorities (crops, forests, coasts, Himalayan glaciers) built in. Applications: disaster risk (earthquakes, landslides, subsidence), agriculture and food security, forest carbon accounting, water resources, climate science.
  • Private sector in strategic space: Drishti shows Indian start-ups building world-first sensors after the 2020 reforms (IN-SPACe, NSIL support, the Indian Space Policy 2023); SBS-III gives 31 of 52 satellites to private firms. Risks: dependence on foreign launchers (Falcon 9), data security, and the need for assured government demand.
  • Space and national security: SBS-III, the Defence Space Agency and a military space doctrine respond to the lesson that real-time intelligence shortens the decision loop (Operation Sindoor, May 2025, as reported). Persistent watch needs both LEO (resolution) and GEO (continuity, e.g. EOS-05) plus resilience: proliferated constellations, quick replacement, protection of satellites.
  • Data fusion and AI: onboard fusion (OptoSAR) and onboard processing cut the time from image to decision; issues include standards, validation and trained analysts.
  • Way forward: raise launch cadence (PSLV, SSLV, private launchers) so replacements are truly responsive; deepen the national data infrastructure (Bhoonidhi, open data where security allows); combine GEO hyperspectral (GISAT) with LEO SAR for agriculture and disasters; keep transparency about what is approved versus planned.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • 1.5 hours is the period of a full polar orbit, not the pole-to-pole half.
  • Passive is not only optical: thermal infrared and microwave radiometers are passive; lidar is active but optical.
  • Hyperspectral = many narrow, contiguous bands (spectral detail), not necessarily finer pixels.
  • NISAR bands: L-band (24 cm) is NASA's; S-band (9.4 cm) is ISRO's. Both share the 12 m reflector and the 242 km swath.
  • NISAR revisit: 12-day repeat cycle; "about 6 days" is the average when ascending and descending passes are combined.
  • Dawn-dusk orbit is a sun-synchronous polar orbit along the terminator; it is not geostationary.
  • Drishti: one satellite (not a batch); multispectral optical (no hyperspectral reported); launched by SpaceX, not ISRO; built by GalaxEye, not ISRO.
  • SBS-III: 21 ISRO + 31 private = 52; approved 2024 (not 2023); target 2029.
  • GISAT-1 failed (2021); EOS-05 = GISAT-1A (2026). INSAT-3D series = weather satellites; GISAT = imaging satellites.
  • Geostationary (fixed in the sky, equatorial) vs geosynchronous (24 h period, may be inclined).

Substantive changes are marked where they occur. Spelling and typing repairs were made silently and are not listed. Numbering continues from the 2 Nov note (c01-c12).

  1. 1.5 hours is the full orbital period, not pole to pole.
  2. "Optical = passive, microwave = active" is a simplification (thermal IR, radiometers, lidar).
  3. Ionospheric electron content peaks in the early afternoon; it delays and rotates radar signals; dawn-dusk design rationale unconfirmed.
  4. L-band: deeper penetration; S-band: light vegetation, not "horizontal coverage"; swath comes from SweepSAR.
  5. SweepSAR: echo sweeps across the feed array of an array-fed reflector.
  6. GalaxEye launched one satellite (190 kg); GLX-SQ precursor.
  7. Drishti's optical sensor is multispectral only; "AI" unconfirmed.
  8. SBS-III: completion by 2029, approved October 2024.
  9. "Replacement within 60 minutes" not found; SSLV is launch-on-demand.
  10. GISAT-1 failed in 2021; latest is EOS-05 (GISAT-1A), Sep 2026.
  11. 42 m is multispectral only; 30 min = whole India, 5 min = selected areas.
