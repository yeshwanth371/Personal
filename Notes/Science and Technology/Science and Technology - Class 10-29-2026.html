17. Stage 3 Continued: Thorium Resource, Advantages and Challenges

Recap: thorium-232 is fertile, not fissile; it is converted to U-233 after neutron capture (28 Oct note, Section 15). This section adds the class's resource figures, advantages and challenges.

17.1 Resource base

  • Where: thorium occurs in monazite in coastal beach sands, notably Kerala and Odisha (also Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh).
  • How much (official estimates): the Atomic Minerals Directorate for Exploration and Research (AMD, DAE) was reported in Feb 2026 to have estimated 13.15 million tonnes (Mt) of monazite across 8 states (Business Line, Feb 2026, secondary). An earlier Lok Sabha reply (search summary) gave about 11.93 Mt monazite containing about 1.07 Mt thorium (Lok Sabha annexure; the annexure itself could not be opened; whether the figure is thorium metal or thorium oxide was not confirmed). [Verification Required] the form (Th or ThO2) and the current figure.
  • Uranium for comparison: AMD has established 4,42,800 t in-situ U3O8 (PIB, 5 Aug 2026, from the 28 Oct note). [Additional] Arithmetic: U3O8 is about 84.8% uranium by mass (3 x 238.03 / (3 x 238.03 + 8 x 16.00)), so 4,42,800 t U3O8 holds about 3.75 lakh t of uranium; natural uranium is about 0.72% U-235, so the fissile U-235 in it is only about 2,700 t.

[Correction] The class compares "U-235 deposits about 70,000 tons" with "thorium 748,000 tons". U-235 is an isotope (0.72% of natural uranium), not a deposit; resources are quoted for uranium (as U3O8 or U metal) and for thorium or monazite. Neither 70,000 t nor 748,000 t was found in the official sources opened; the AMD figures above are the verified ones. Compare like with like (uranium vs thorium), not "U-235 vs thorium". [Verification Required] the origin of the class numbers.

17.3 Challenges in using thorium

  1. Fertile, so it needs a driver. Th-232 cannot sustain a chain reaction alone; it must be irradiated together with a fissile driver (U-235, Pu-239 or later U-233) to be turned into U-233. In the three-stage programme the driver is Pu-239 from Stage 1 and Stage 2 (28 Oct note, Figure 8).

    [Additional] ANEEL (class example): ANEEL (Advanced Nuclear Energy for Enriched Life) is a thoria-urania mixed-oxide fuel made by the US company Clean Core Thorium Energy (CCTE), using high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) as the driver and a burnable neutron absorber, designed as a drop-in replacement for natural-uranium bundles in PHWR/CANDU reactors (ScienceDirect abstracts; NTPC press release, titled "NTPC and CCTE to advance thorium-based ANEEL fuel for PHWRs"; the Canadian Nuclear Laboratories fuel-fabrication tie-up is reported by The Indian Express; all secondary or company sources, dates not verified). HALEU means uranium enriched above 5% and below 20% U-235 (standard definition); the class says 5-20%; a neutronics paper describes ANEEL with enrichment up to about 16%. It still needs enrichment, so it depends on HALEU supply, unlike the closed-cycle plan of Stages 1-3. It is a private-sector fuel concept, not part of the DAE's three-stage design. [Verification Required] start dates and the status of any Indian demonstration.

  2. Reprocessing difficulty. Thorium dioxide (thoria) fuel is chemically very inert; it dissolves in nitric acid only with a small fluoride (hydrofluoric acid) addition in the THOREX process (standard knowledge, [Verification Required]). The recovered U-233 is accompanied by U-232, whose decay products emit strong gamma rays, so handling needs shielded remote-handled facilities.
  3. Protactinium step. The chain is Th-232 + n → Th-233 → Pa-233 → U-233 (Figure 13). Pa-233 is not fissile, has a half-life of about 27 days, and can absorb another neutron and be lost instead of decaying to U-233, hurting breeding.
  4. Geopolitical. Advanced reprocessing and enrichment technology sits under the NSG's controlled transfers; India's NSG membership bid has been opposed by China and some others (Section 19.3).
  5. Bootstrap problem. Thorium can only be used at scale after enough fast breeder capacity exists to make the starting fissile material (28 Oct note, Section 15.4). At present only one commercial-scale prototype (the 500 MWe PFBR) has reached first criticality (6 Apr 2026); the 40 MWt FBTR is a research reactor.

[Correction] The class says "U-233 does not readily dissolve in acids like hydrofluoric and nitric" and that it "turns into protactinium which is not fissile". Corrected: it is the thoria fuel matrix that resists dissolution (needs nitric acid with fluoride); U-233 itself is a fissile uranium isotope; Pa-233 is an intermediate between Th-233 and U-233, not a decay product of U-233. The radioactivity problem arises mainly from U-232 and its daughters.

[Correction] (i) "China and 9 other countries" opposing the NSG bid: the count of objecting states was not found in the sources opened [Verification Required]. (ii) "Only 1 FBR which has just started the operations": PFBR attained first criticality (start of a sustained chain reaction), not commercial power operation, and FBTR is a separate research FBR (28 Oct note, correction on criticality).

18.1 Accelerator Driven System: principle and the thorium chain

  • Sub-critical means the effective multiplication factor keff is below 1: each generation has fewer neutrons than the last, so the reaction dies unless external neutrons are supplied. The accelerator supplies them through spallation in a heavy-metal target.
  • Uses: burn thorium with a smaller fissile inventory; transmute long-lived waste; inherent shutdown by switching off the accelerator.

[Correction] (i) The class says the core is sub-critical because "the quantity of fissile material is less than the critical mass". Sub-criticality is a property of the whole core (fuel amount, geometry, absorbers and neutron leakage), summarised by keff < 1; a smaller amount of fuel is one way to get there. (ii) The class says ADS were meant "to convert nuclear waste into stable elements". Transmutation turns long-lived minor actinides into shorter-lived products (and some stable ones), not wholly stable elements (28 Oct note, Section 15.3). (iii) The Indian Fermilab partnership is the IIFC; "HISPA" is unverified (table above).

18.2 Hydrogen from the CHTR (class)

  • High-temperature heat (about 1000 C outlet, 28 Oct note: INIS) can drive thermochemical water splitting to make hydrogen, and can also supply heat for desalination (class). The IAEA INIS record describes the CHTR as a compact power pack for remote areas and a technology demonstrator for a hydrogen-focused Indian programme.
  • Target users (class): petroleum refining, fertiliser, and iron and steel industries, which today use hydrogen produced from fossil fuels, releasing CO2.

[Correction] The class says industrial hydrogen today comes from "coal gasification". Most hydrogen worldwide comes from steam reforming of natural gas (called "grey" hydrogen); coal gasification is a route used in some countries including parts of fertiliser production. The point that conventional hydrogen causes CO2 emissions stands. [Verification Required] India's route-wise share (not opened). Do not confuse the 100 kWth CHTR with the HTGCR of the Nuclear Energy Mission (28 Oct note, correction on CHTR versus HTGCR).

18.3 Molten salt reactor (class)

[Correction] The class says that in an accident "the molten material will solidify after coming in contact with air". Molten-salt designs rely on freeze-plug or gravity-drain features: fuel salt drains to a tank where it cools and solidifies, and the salt operates near atmospheric pressure so there is no high-pressure steam to burst a vessel. Contact with air is not the safety mechanism, and such features are design claims for a technology not yet shown at power-plant scale (28 Oct note, correction on molten-salt reactors). [Additional] China's experimental thorium molten-salt reactor TMSR-LF1 (2 MWt, first criticality 11 Oct 2023) is a research prototype (Wikipedia, TMSR-LF1, from the 28 Oct note).

  • What: a group of nuclear supplier countries that controls transfer of nuclear and nuclear-related dual-use material and technology through guidelines; decisions by consensus (class). The count of participating governments is reported as 48 (secondary compilation; the NSG website could not be opened [Verification Required]).

19.4 Doctrine: components and No First Use (NFU)

The National Security Advisory Board, under K. Subrahmanyam, drafted a nuclear doctrine (made public in 1999) which was adopted in January 2003 (class). India states that its weapons are meant to deter, not to attack.

  1. No first use: India will not be the first to use nuclear weapons, but if attacked it will respond with massive retaliation to inflict unacceptable damage. This needs second-strike capability and therefore a triad (land, air and sea), in particular a ballistic-missile submarine. India retains the option of nuclear retaliation against a major biological or chemical attack on India or Indian forces anywhere (class). During drafting there were three schools: rationalists (for NFU), maximalists (first use), rejectionists (decide as the situation demands) (class). In 2010 NSA Shivshankar Menon described the policy as "no first use against non-nuclear-weapon states" (class, [Verification Required]).
  2. Non-use against non-nuclear-weapon states (except as in the biological/chemical clause above).
  3. Self-imposed moratorium on nuclear testing.
  4. Credible minimum deterrence: a minimum number of weapons enough for unacceptable damage; judging "unacceptable" needs an understanding of the adversary's economy, polity, defence and society.
  5. Decision-making: Nuclear Command Authority. The Political Council (chaired by the Prime Minister) takes the final decision; the Executive Council (chaired by the National Security Adviser) supports it; execution is by the Strategic Forces Command (SFC), headed by an officer of Air Marshal or equivalent rank, which holds the weapons, missiles and aircraft. The SFC is one of two tri-service commands (the other is the Andaman and Nicobar Command) (class; [Verification Required] that no other tri-service command has since been created).

[Correction] The class says the triad was completed "when INS Arihant completed its first deterrence patrol in 2018" and mentions only Arighaat (2024). Arihant was commissioned in 2016 and reportedly completed the first patrol in 2018; a third SSBN, INS Aridhaman, was reported as commissioned on 3 Apr 2026 (secondary; no official release opened). Triad status as of 29 Oct 2026 therefore: three SSBNs reported, one unconfirmed officially.

20. Fusion Power

20.1 Principle

  • Nuclear fusion (also called a thermonuclear reaction) is the energy source of the Sun and stars: light nuclei (hydrogen isotopes) combine to form a heavier nucleus (helium), releasing a neutron and energy. The mass of the product is slightly less than the sum of the reactants; the difference is released as energy (E = mc2) (LLNL).
  • Why explored: to address resource and environmental constraints; fusion is a clean, carbon-free option (class; ITER).
  • Fuel: the easiest reaction in the laboratory is deuterium + tritium (D-T), which has the highest energy gain at the "lowest" temperatures (ITER).
  • Three conditions (ITER): very high temperature (of the order of 150,000,000 C); sufficient plasma density; sufficient confinement time.

20.2 Magnetic confinement: plasma and the tokamak

  • Plasma is often called the fourth state of matter: at extreme temperatures electrons are separated from nuclei and the gas becomes charged (ITER). Fusion plasmas are the environment in which light elements can fuse.
  • Principle: a charged particle in a magnetic field is forced to spiral along the field lines, so strong magnetic fields can hold hot plasma away from the vessel walls.
  • Tokamak: a doughnut-shaped (toroidal) vacuum chamber surrounded by magnetic coils. The word is a Russian acronym for "toroidal chamber with magnetic coils"; it was first developed in Soviet research in the late 1950s (ITER). Energy of fusion is absorbed as heat in the walls and, like a conventional plant, can drive steam turbines in a future power plant.
  • Goal: to raise the plasma temperature to about 150 million C so that fusion occurs, and to obtain more fusion power than the heating power put in. The measure is Q = fusion power / heating power: ITER is designed for Q = 10, i.e. 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of heating power; the JET record in 1997 was 16 MW from 24 MW (Q = 0.67) (ITER).

[Visual Representation Recommended: a labelled engineering cross-section of the ITER tokamak (cryostat, toroidal and poloidal field coils, central solenoid, vacuum vessel, blanket modules, divertor) with the ITER Organization's own drawing as source, to complement the simplified schematic above.]

[Correction] The class says plasma is formed "when an atom disintegrates into charged particles" and that a charged particle in a magnetic field "moves clockwise or anticlockwise". Better: at very high temperature the atom is ionised (electrons and nuclei separate; ITER); in a magnetic field the particle gyrates in a spiral along the field line (the sense of rotation depends on the sign of its charge). "Tokamak (cylindrical)" is toroidal (doughnut-shaped).

20.5 Issues in fusion

  • Not yet a power source: no device has produced net electricity; ITER is a plasma-physics and technology experiment.
  • Schedule and cost risk: ITER's first research operation has slipped to 2034 and D-T operation to 2039 (2024 baseline).
  • Tritium supply and breeding; neutron damage to materials; heat exhaust to the divertor; remote maintenance (ITER).
  • Advantages (class and ITER): fuel abundant (deuterium from water), no chain reaction, carbon-free, safety demonstrated through nuclear licensing of ITER in France (2012). Fusion still produces activated structural material and tritium to be managed [Additional].

21. Laser Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF)

Class gap: the rough notes had only the heading "Laser Inertial confinement" with no content. This section is entirely [Additional], written from the LLNL National Ignition Facility (NIF) page. No Indian laser-fusion programme was verified in the sources opened [Verification Required].

21.1 Principle

  • In inertial confinement the fuel is compressed and heated so quickly that fusion occurs before the fuel can fly apart: it is held by its own inertia.
  • In NIF's indirect drive, up to 192 laser beams are fired into a centimetre-sized hollow cylinder called a hohlraum, producing soft X-rays that ablate the surface of a peppercorn-sized capsule of frozen hydrogen isotopes; the rocket-like implosion exceeds 400 km/s (LLNL).
  • Ignition (LLNL definition): the heating by alpha particles from fusion in the central hot spot overcomes the cooling losses, so a burn wave spreads into the surrounding cold fuel; practically, the fusion yield exceeds the laser energy delivered to the target.

21.2 NIF results

  • 5 Dec 2022: ignition: 3.15 MJ of fusion energy from 2.05 MJ of laser energy delivered to the target (target gain about 1.5) (LLNL).
  • 7 Apr 2025: record 8.6 MJ (+/- 0.45 MJ) from 2.08 MJ, target gain 4.13. 20 Jun 2026: eleventh ignition, 7.9 MJ (+/- 0.4 MJ), gain about 3.8.
  • Purpose: LLNL describes NIF as vital for maintaining the safety and effectiveness of the US nuclear arsenal (stockpile stewardship) and as improving the prospects for inertial fusion energy.

[Additional] Important distinction: NIF's "gain" counts only the laser energy that reaches the target, not the much larger electrical energy used to power the lasers, and the facility fires an experiment, not a continuous power plant. It is therefore a scientific breakeven (ignition) and not an energy-producing plant. Likewise, JET's Q = 0.67 and ITER's Q = 10 refer to fusion power versus plasma heating power, not to electricity from the grid.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Thorium: Th-232 is fertile; Th-232 + n → Th-233 → Pa-233 → U-233. Monazite beach sands (Kerala, Odisha). AMD: 13.15 Mt monazite in 8 states (Feb 2026, secondary). U-233 has the best neutron yield in the thermal spectrum.
  • Challenges: fertile (needs a driver: HALEU in ANEEL, Pu-239 in Stage 2); thoria dissolves only with fluoride; U-232 gamma makes handling remote; bootstrap problem (needs FBR capacity); NSG limits technology.
  • ANEEL: thoria-urania MOX with HALEU, by US company Clean Core Thorium Energy; for PHWR/CANDU bundles. HALEU: above 5% and below 20% U-235.
  • ADS: sub-critical (keff < 1); proton accelerator + lead target (spallation); India-Fermilab IIFC. CHTR: 100 kWth, U-233/thorium TRISO fuel, lead-bismuth coolant, BeO moderator, hydrogen focus. AHWR: about 300 MWe, heavy-water moderator, light-water coolant. Molten salt: low pressure, high temperature.
  • Weapons programme: Pokhran-I 18 May 1974 "Smiling Buddha" (plutonium, official yield about 12 kt); Pokhran-II 11 and 13 May 1998 (5 tests, thermonuclear claim about 45 kt); IGMDP 1983 (Prithvi, Agni); doctrine adopted Jan 2003.
  • Doctrine: NFU + massive retaliation; non-use against non-nuclear-weapon states; moratorium on testing; credible minimum deterrence; Nuclear Command Authority: Political Council (PM) and Executive Council (NSA); Strategic Forces Command executes. Retaliation option against major CBW attack.
  • SSBNs: INS Arihant (commissioned 2016; first deterrence patrol 2018; K-15), INS Arighaat (Aug 2024), INS Aridhaman (reported 3 Apr 2026, not officially confirmed in sources opened).
  • NSG: formed 1975 after the 1974 test; consensus; 2008 clean waiver (6 Sep 2008) after India's 5 Sep commitments; 2016 Seoul plenary blocked India's bid (China). NWFZ treaties: Pelindaba (Africa), Semipalatinsk (Central Asia), Bangkok (SE Asia), Tlatelolco (Latin America), Rarotonga (South Pacific).
  • Fusion: D + T → He-4 + n (17.6 MeV); about 150 million C; plasma = ionised gas; tokamak = toroidal chamber with magnetic coils (Russian acronym).
  • ITER: Cadarache, France; 7 Members (China, EU, India, Japan, Korea, Russia, USA; not the UK); Q = 10 (500 MW from 50 MW); no electricity; Start of Research Operation 2034, D-T 2039; India's share 9.1%; Europe 45.6%; all core components on site 2 Oct 2026.
  • Indian tokamaks: ADITYA (1989; ADITYA-U divertor) and SST-1 (superconducting, 2013), both at IPR, Gujarat.
  • Others: JT-60SA (Japan, largest operating, first plasma Oct 2023); JET (UK; retired 2023); KSTAR (Korea; 48 s at 100 million C reported); EAST (China; 1,066 s H-mode reported).
  • NIF: ignition 5 Dec 2022 (3.15 MJ from 2.05 MJ); record 8.6 MJ on 7 Apr 2025; 11 ignition shots to 20 Jun 2026.

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Thorium: resource advantage, time disadvantage. India's thorium resource is large, but its use depends on Stage 2 capacity and on mastering reprocessing and remote fuel handling; options such as HALEU-thorium fuels (ANEEL) could add thorium to existing PHWRs sooner but rely on enrichment and foreign supply.
  • Multiple parallel bets: ADS, CHTR (hydrogen/process heat), AHWR and molten salt are complementary; each should have clear milestones, because several remain at design or R&D stage.
  • Technology denial and diplomacy: the NSG clean waiver (2008) opened trade but not membership; membership would help exports, fuel access and thorium-technology credibility, but progress is hostage to China-Pakistan linkage. A balanced approach: keep bilateral NCAs, build criteria-based claims (export controls, safeguards record) and avoid linkage with Pakistan.
  • Doctrine and stability: NFU and credible minimum deterrence reduce arms-race pressure and support India's "responsible nuclear power" image; a second-strike triad (SSBNs) strengthens the logic of NFU. The debate on revising NFU should weigh China's numbers, Pakistan's posture and the cost of losing the diplomatic gains linked to the doctrine.
  • Fusion: long-horizon science, near-term participation. ITER membership gives India access to the technology and training at a 9.1% share; ADITYA-U and SST-1 build domestic capability; commercial fusion electricity is not expected in the near term (ITER D-T in 2039). Fusion should complement, not replace, fission and renewables in the 2047 and 2070 targets.
  • Science versus engineering: distinguish duration records (EAST, KSTAR), energy-gain records (NIF target gain) and power-plant readiness; media headlines often conflate them.
  • Way forward: (i) complete PFBR commissioning and then commercial FBRs to unlock thorium; (ii) fund thorium fuel demonstrations in PHWRs and AHWR/CHTR prototypes; (iii) strengthen reprocessing and remote-handling capability; (iv) continue NSG outreach and non-proliferation credibility; (v) keep fusion participation tied to technology transfer (superconducting magnets, cryogenics, materials) and a national roadmap; (vi) maintain transparency about doctrine and command and control.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • U-235 is not a "deposit"; compare uranium and thorium resources, not U-235 and thorium.
  • Thorium is fertile, not fissile; Pa-233 is an intermediate; U-233 is the fissile product.
  • ANEEL is a US company's HALEU-thorium fuel, not an Indian Stage 3 design.
  • CHTR (100 kWth, hydrogen/remote power) is not AHWR (300 MWe) and not HTGCR.
  • ADS is sub-critical (keff < 1) and needs an accelerator; it is not a critical reactor.
  • The UK is not an ITER member; the EU is, through Euratom.
  • ITER will not produce electricity; Q = 10 means 10 times the heating power input, not grid output.
  • JET is retired; JT-60SA is the largest operating tokamak.
  • Long-pulse records (EAST, KSTAR) are not energy-gain records; NIF's gain counts laser energy on target only.
  • NSG waiver (2008) is not NSG membership; India still has no membership.
  • NFU is not "no use ever": India reserves massive retaliation if attacked and a nuclear option against a major biological or chemical attack.
  • Pokhran-I (1974) was a plutonium fission device; Pokhran-II (1998) had five tests including a thermonuclear claim.
  • The doctrine was drafted in 1999 and adopted in January 2003; do not mix with 1998.
