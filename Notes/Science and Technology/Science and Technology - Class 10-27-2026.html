5. Nuclear Power Programme: Targets and the Fuel Problem

5.1 Concept

  • Energy security means reliable, affordable energy supply. Nuclear power gives low-carbon baseload electricity, but India has limited uranium and large thorium reserves (see Section 2 of the 26 Oct note), so fuel supply constrains growth.
  • After the 1974 test and the creation of the Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG), India was kept out of global nuclear trade, which hampered fuel imports and plant expansion. The 2008 NSG waiver (Section 6) reopened trade.

5.3 The fuel problem

  • Domestic uranium supply cannot fuel a large fleet; the class says only about 20% of the required fuel could be made available domestically.
  • The thrust to import fuel and reactors led to civil Nuclear Cooperation Agreements (Section 6).

[Verification Required] The "20% domestic fuel" figure was not found in any official source opened today. Treat it as a class statement of the scale of the shortfall, not a verified statistic.

[Additional] Result of the 2008 opening (PIB, 2 Apr 2026): after the NSG waiver and agreements, nuclear electricity generation rose from 16,956 MU (2007-08) to 56,681 MU (2024-25) and capacity from 4,020 MW to 8,780 MW. Sixteen reactors (excluding RAPS-1, 100 MW), with 6,380 MW capacity, are fuelled by imported fuel under IAEA safeguards. (MU = million units, i.e. GWh.)

6. Civil Nuclear Cooperation Agreements (NCAs)

6.1 What and why

  • An NCA is a government-to-government agreement allowing trade in nuclear fuel, equipment, reactors and technology for peaceful uses, with safeguards.
  • For India it became possible only after the IAEA safeguards agreement (1 Aug 2008 Board approval) and the NSG waiver (6 Sep 2008), which exempted India from the NSG rule of full-scope safeguards as a condition of supply.

7. India's Obligations under the NCAs

7.1 Why obligations were needed

India is not a party to the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT). The NSG rules required full-scope IAEA safeguards on all nuclear activity of a recipient. India instead offered a separation of its facilities into civil and military, with only the civil ones safeguarded.

  • NPT (opened 1968): recognises as nuclear-weapon states (NWS) only those that tested before 1 Jan 1967 (US, Russia, UK, France, China). India has not signed.
  • IAEA safeguards: accounting and inspection to verify that nuclear material is not diverted from peaceful use.

[Correction] The first draft says civil reactors "are imported / use imported fuel of both of them can be imported". The clearer rule: civil (safeguarded) reactors may use imported fuel and are open to foreign trade, but are permanently under safeguards; military reactors use only domestic material and stay outside safeguards. Not every civil reactor is itself imported (Indian-designed PHWRs on the civil list use domestic or imported fuel). The 16 imported-fuel reactors in the PIB reply is a count of safeguarded reactors, not all of the 14 or so reactors on the original 2006 list.

[Correction] The class states that India is "recognised as a nuclear weapon state". India is not an NWS under the NPT definition. What changed is a de facto accommodation: India is the only state outside the NPT that is allowed civil nuclear commerce while keeping a military programme outside safeguards. US negotiators stated that no part of the 123 Agreement recognises India as a nuclear-weapon state (secondary summary).

7.3 Obligation 2: Additional Protocol (AP)

  • Drawback of safeguards: the IAEA could inspect only declared facilities. In the early 1990s, Iraq (discovered after the 1991 Gulf War) and North Korea pursued weapons programmes despite being under safeguards.
  • The IAEA's response was the "93+2" programme, culminating in the Model Additional Protocol (1997), to strengthen verification in non-nuclear-weapon states. It gives the IAEA more information and complementary access to both declared and undeclared sites. A state with a safeguards agreement may add a protocol.

7.4 Obligation 3: Self-imposed moratorium on nuclear testing

  • A voluntary halt on nuclear explosive tests, declared after the May 1998 Pokhran-II tests and reiterated in India's statement to the NSG on 5 Sep 2008 (class: "2008 statement"). India has not signed the CTBT.
  • Because it is a unilateral pledge, India says it retains the right to test if its supreme national interest / sovereignty is threatened (class: "unless there is a threat to sovereignty").
  • Analysts note that the 2008 NSG waiver and the Hyde Act both link cooperation to testing, so a test would have costs for supply (see Section 8); this is a policy and legal debate, not a binding ban on India.

[Verification Required] The 5 Sep 2008 date for the NSG statement and the "Paragraph 16" mechanism rest on secondary reporting (Wikipedia summary, which presents differing expert views); the NSG text itself was not opened.

7.5 Obligation 4: Nuclear liability law

To define compensation in a nuclear accident, India passed the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage (CLND) Act, 2010 (popularly the Nuclear Liability Act). It has been repealed and replaced by the SHANTI Act, 2025 - see Section 10.

8. The 123 Agreement (Indo-US Civil Nuclear Agreement, 2008)

8.1 Name and legal basis

  • Named after Section 123 of the US Atomic Energy Act, 1954: the US can share nuclear technology with another country only through an "agreement for cooperation" meeting conditions in US law, including full-scope IAEA safeguards.
  • The Hyde Act (Henry J. Hyde United States-India Peaceful Atomic Energy Cooperation Act, 2006), signed 18 Dec 2006, modified these requirements for India so a 123 agreement could be negotiated. It binds the US, not India; the President called some policy statements "advisory".
  • Signed 10 Oct 2008 (Washington) by India's External Affairs Minister Pranab Mukherjee and US Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice; effective 6 Dec 2008.

8.2 Provisions (class), with refinements

ProvisionPosition
1. Strategic reserve / fuel assuranceThe agreement refers to fuel-supply assurances and a reserve for safeguarded reactors. [Correction] The US side described its fuel assurances as political commitments, not legally binding, and the Hyde Act limits fuel supply to "reasonable operating requirements"; the extent of a "strategic reserve" was disputed in India (secondary summary).
2. Reactor exportsUS companies may sell reactors to India (Westinghouse AP1000 at Kovvada: proposed, not built).

8.3 Significance and criticism

  • Broke India's nuclear isolation (post-1974 sanctions regime) and opened fuel, reactor and technology trade.
  • India retained its military programme and the unsafeguarded reactors; its weapons capability was not capped (class: right to test and enhance arsenal, subject to consequences for cooperation).
  • De-hyphenation of India and Pakistan in US policy.
  • Political cost in India: the Left withdrew support in July 2008; the government won the vote of confidence 275-256.
  • Biggest criticism: for over a decade not one US-designed reactor was built, because of the liability regime and local opposition; the SHANTI Act, 2025 removes the supplier-recourse obstacle (Section 10). [Additional] Westinghouse's 2016 six-reactor plan (AP1000) was to be sited at Kovvada, Andhra Pradesh, after an earlier proposed Gujarat site was dropped; land for Kovvada was reported acquired by end-2024 (secondary).

[Correction] The class note "US will help India build a strategic reserve of nuclear fuel, i.e. lifetime fuel supply assurances" overstates the legal position (see row 1). Say "fuel-supply assurances (political), with advance consent for reprocessing in a safeguarded facility".

9. India-Japan Nuclear Deal

  • Signed 11 Nov 2016 (DAE date); in force 2017 (class). India is the only non-NPT state with which Japan has a nuclear cooperation agreement.

9.1 Why it mattered

  1. Japan, notably Japan Steel Works, is one of very few suppliers of the large steel forgings needed for reactor pressure vessels.
  2. Japanese firms are tied to Western vendors: Toshiba owned Westinghouse until 2018; Hitachi has a nuclear joint venture with General Electric; Mitsubishi Heavy Industries partnered Areva. Without Japan, deals with the US and France could have failed.

9.2 Two sets of documents (first time)

Note on Views and Understanding
Records two Indian commitments: no first use and the self-imposed testing moratorium. India says it is explanatory and non-binding; Japan's position is that cooperation can be suspended if India tests.
Nuclear Cooperation Agreement
Allows Indian and Japanese organisations to form joint ventures to build reactors in India, giving technology transfer.

[Additional] Reading of the dispute (class): Japan is partly reassuring its own public; a test by India would follow a serious threat from China, which is probably a bigger threat to Japan as well. This is an analytical view, not a stated position of either government.

[Additional] NSG angle: the deal makes Japan a stakeholder in India's NSG bid, because some countries opposing India's entry have good relations with Japan (class view; [Verification Required] as interpretation). Japan Steel Works, Toshiba-Westinghouse (until 2018), Hitachi-GE and the MHI-Areva partnership are class statements; the Toshiba sale date is standard knowledge.

[Correction] The rough notes call the Japan deal one of "two sets" of documents "for the first time"; the careful statement is that the Note on Views and Understanding sits alongside the agreement. Whether it is "the first time" Japan has done so for any partner was not verified.

10. Governance Structure, Government Agencies and the Liability Law

10.1 Structure

  • DAE functions directly under the Prime Minister, headed by a Secretary. Parliament replies are given by the Union Minister of State in the Prime Minister's Office (Atomic Energy and Space), Dr Jitendra Singh (PIB).
  • Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) sets policy and targets; the DAE Secretary is its ex-officio Chairman (class).
  • Operators construct, operate and maintain reactors and bear primary liability in an accident: NPCIL (Nuclear Power Corporation of India Ltd) and BHAVINI (Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Ltd, the fast-breeder operator).
  • AERB (Atomic Energy Regulatory Board) was constituted in 1983 under the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 as the safety regulator, administratively under the AEC (class).

10.3 Concerns with the structure

  1. Conflict of interest: the same department controls operators and the safety regulator, whose roles conflict. The IAEA Convention on Nuclear Safety expects the regulator to be effectively separated from promoting bodies (standard knowledge, [Verification Required] article text).
  2. AERB lacked statutory autonomy (functional autonomy claimed by the Government; de jure autonomy demanded).
  3. No mandatory notification of all nuclear incidents and accidents.
  4. Expert committees are drawn largely from the DAE, so the AERB lacks an independent workforce.

[Correction] The class says India has had "no major nuclear accidents like Three Mile Island (1979)". This is a fair statement about severity, but not that India has had no incidents: standard accounts record fires and flooding at Narora (1993) and Kakrapar (1994). [Verification Required] details and any International Nuclear Event Scale rating before quoting.

10.4 Reform history

  • Fukushima (2011) accident, then protests against the Kudankulam reactors.
  • The Nuclear Safety Regulatory Authority (NSRA) Bill, 2011 proposed notifying all nuclear incidents and accidents within 15 days (class figure) and a Council of Nuclear Safety under the Prime Minister. The bill lapsed.
  • The SHANTI Act, 2025 finally gave the AERB statutory status (§17, per secondary summary; PIB confirms statutory status and independent regulation-making).

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Energy security with a safeguards price: the 2008 opening gave fuel and trade (generation up from 16,956 MU to 56,681 MU) at the cost of permanent safeguards on civil facilities. Capacity grew only from 4,020 MW to 8,780 MW in about 18 years, so the opening did not by itself deliver the targets.
  • Strategic autonomy preserved: the Separation Plan kept the military programme outside safeguards and the moratorium stayed a unilateral pledge. This is a model of "de facto" accommodation outside the NPT, but it also leaves India outside NSG membership and the ENR club.
  • Liability as the binding constraint: for a decade the CLND Act's supplier recourse deterred US and French vendors; SHANTI aligns India with the CSC. The test is whether Jaitapur and Kovvada actually move, and whether victims' remedies stay adequate.
  • Regulation and trust: statutory AERB addresses the operator-regulator conflict only if it has independent staff, funding and transparency (incident disclosure, public consultation on the draft Rules).
  • Diversification: only about 46 GW of the 100 GW is projected from non-NPCIL entities; private entry needs clear licence, fuel-cycle and waste rules (enrichment and isotopic separation reserved for the Government).
  • Technology depth: SMRs (BSMR-200, SMR-55), the HTGCR for hydrogen and the thorium path reduce uranium import dependence; indigenous forgings and supply chain (Japan Steel Works dependence in the class example) are a vulnerability.
  • Way forward: (i) ensure supplier contracts for Kudankulam-type projects and early firm commitments for Jaitapur and Kovvada; (ii) build indigenous heavy-forging and component capacity; (iii) strengthen the regulator and waste-management capability; (iv) keep securing fuel through multiple suppliers; (v) pursue NSG membership through diplomacy while avoiding dependence on any single supplier.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Capacity versus generation: 100 GW is installed capacity (GWe) target, not electricity generated.
  • Old versus current targets: 63,000 MWe by 2032 (old) is not the Nuclear Energy Mission (100 GW by 2047).
  • Safeguards agreement is not the Additional Protocol; safeguards cover declared facilities, the AP also covers undeclared ones. India's reactors on the civil list are covered by safeguards, not "by the AP".
  • "Civil" does not mean "imported": civil means safeguarded; imported fuel can be used only in safeguarded reactors.
  • De facto accommodation is not NPT recognition: India is not an NWS under the NPT.
  • NSG waiver (2008) is not NSG membership; India is not a member.
  • NCAs are not reactor purchase orders: Jaitapur and Kovvada are proposals; Kudankulam 3-6 are under construction.
  • Hyde Act is US law; the 123 Agreement is a bilateral treaty-level agreement. Section 123 refers to the US Atomic Energy Act, not an Indian provision.
  • The CLND Act is repealed; SHANTI also repeals the Atomic Energy Act, 1962.
  • Japan's Note on Views is distinct from the NCA, and its status (binding or explanatory) is disputed.
