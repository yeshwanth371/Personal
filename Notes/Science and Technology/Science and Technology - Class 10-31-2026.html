Recap: Section 25 of the 30 Oct note set out the Act's provisions (licensing, liability tiers, recourse, bodies). This section records the class's reasons for the Act and the experts' objections, and checks them.

27. Small Modular Reactors (SMRs): Concept, Advantages and Challenges

27.1 Definition

  • Class: SMRs have a capacity of 30 MW to 300 MW; those below 30 MW are micro-reactors. "Small" means capacity; "modular" means assembled at a central location and transported to the site.
  • IAEA: SMRs are advanced reactors with a power capacity of up to 300 MW(e) per unit, about one-third of a traditional reactor; small = physically a fraction of the size; modular = systems and components can be factory-assembled and transported as a unit; microreactors are a subset designed to generate power typically up to 10 MW(e) (IAEA). PIB gives the range as "less than 30 MWe to 300+ MWe" (PIB, 3 Feb 2025).

[Correction] The class's cut-off (micro below 30 MW) is not the IAEA's: the IAEA puts the SMR ceiling at 300 MW(e) and microreactors at typically up to 10 MW(e). Sources differ in definition (IAEA versus PIB); use the IAEA figure for definitions and note PIB's wording for India. MWe = megawatts of electrical output; MWth = thermal output.

27.3 Challenges (class list, checked)

  1. Technology maturity. Class: the technology will take around two decades to mature. Not verified [Verification Required]. The IAEA notes more than 80 commercial SMR designs under development, and that although SMRs have lower upfront capital cost per unit, their economic competitiveness is still to be proven in practice; the first plants are operating or commissioning (Section 29) (IAEA). Class conclusion: India should therefore consider the 220 MW PHWR. This is what the Bharat Small Reactor (BSR) is: a 220 MW PHWR with a proven safety and performance record, upgraded for a smaller land requirement (PIB, 3 Feb 2025).
  2. Supply chain. Class: not yet established. Globally this is a first-of-a-kind issue; for India, PIB says the necessary technology is available in the country and most equipment is within the manufacturing capability of Indian industry, with the special reactor-vessel alloy ApuRVA and forging technology for the BSMR-200 and SMR-55 developed indigenously (PIB, 12 Mar 2026). Treat the two statements as different scopes (global versus Indian).
  3. Waste distributed across many sites. A plausible concern for many small plants (standard reasoning); PIB says waste management facilities are established with plants at the site (PIB, 3 Dec 2025). Not quantified [Verification Required].
  4. Technology concentrated in the US, Russia, France and China, who may be reluctant to share the "hot section" components. [Correction] "Hot section" is a gas-turbine (jet-engine) term, not a reactor term. For reactors the sensitive items would be the reactor pressure vessel and forgings, fuel, control and protection systems and design codes. India's own designs reduce this dependence: BARC designs the BSMR-200 and SMR-55 and has produced the vessel alloy and control-rod drive mechanism in-house (PIB, 12 Mar 2026). Whether the named countries would refuse transfers was not verified [Verification Required].

28. India's Approach to SMRs

28.1 Budget provisions and targets

  • Union Budget 2024-25 (July 2024): the Government announced it would partner with the private sector to set up Bharat Small Reactors, research and develop the Bharat Small Modular Reactor, and research newer nuclear technologies (WNN; PIB, 3 Feb 2025).
  • Union Budget 2025-26 (1 Feb 2025): the Nuclear Energy Mission, with Rs 20,000 crore for the design, development and deployment of SMRs, aiming at at least five indigenous SMRs operational by 2033 (PIB, 5 Aug 2026).

[Correction] The class says about Rs 25,000 crore was allocated to the Department of Atomic Energy in 2024-25 for SMR research. No SMR-specific Rs 25,000 crore allocation was found; the 2024-25 Budget announcement concerned the BSR partnership and R&D, and the dated, verified SMR provision is the Rs 20,000 crore of 2025-26. The Rs 25,000 crore may be the Department's overall outlay [Verification Required]. The class's "by 2032-33" is read as "by 2033" (PIB). [Additional] PIB says these SMRs need 60 to 72 months (5 to 6 years) to establish after administrative and financial sanction (PIB, 5 Aug 2026), so the 2033 target depends on sanctions coming early; as of 12 Mar 2026 the BSMR-200 proposal had been cleared by the Atomic Energy Commission and was to go to the Cabinet Committee (PIB).

[Correction] The class cites BrahMos as an example of a jet-powered cruise missile. BrahMos is a two-stage missile: a solid-propellant booster takes it to supersonic speed and separates, then a liquid ramjet sustains cruise at about Mach 2.8 (BrahMos Aerospace, search summary). So it uses a rocket stage first and an air-breathing engine afterwards.

30.2 Ramjet versus scramjet

Class comparison checked
Class statementChecked position
Both need atmospheric oxygen.Correct (PIB feature, 5 Sep 2016).
Both need an external push.Correct: neither produces thrust at zero airspeed; a scramjet vehicle needs a rocket boost to the speed where it begins to produce thrust (PIB feature; ISRO used a solid rocket booster, PIB, 28 Aug 2016). A ramjet works the same way (BrahMos's booster).
Both have no moving parts in the engine.Correct: in ramjet and scramjet there is no moving part, unlike turbojets (PIB feature). They compress air by forward motion, without an axial compressor.
The ramjet uses solid fuel and hydrocarbon such as kerosene; the scramjet uses liquid hydrogen and hydrocarbon.[Correction] BrahMos's ramjet is a liquid-fuelled ramjet; the solid propellant belongs to its booster stage (BrahMos Aerospace). Solid-fuel ramjets exist as a type but were not verified here [Verification Required]. For scramjets the fuel differs by programme: ISRO's scramjet used hydrogen as fuel with atmospheric oxygen as oxidiser (PIB, 28 Aug 2016); DRDO's actively cooled scramjet combustor uses an indigenous liquid hydrocarbon endothermic fuel (PIB, 9 May 2026). It is one fuel or the other in an engine, not "hydrogen and hydrocarbon" together.
Ramjet gives supersonic speed; scramjet gives hypersonic speed.Correct in outline. BrahMos cruises at about Mach 2.8 (BrahMos). ISRO's scramjet flight reached Mach 6 (PIB). [Additional] Hypersonic is conventionally above Mach 5.
The ramjet is used in BrahMos Mk-1 and the scramjet in BrahMos Mk-2.[Correction] Mk-1 (the in-service BrahMos) uses the liquid ramjet. BrahMos-II is a planned hypersonic version under development; a secondary report says a test is eyed for 2027-2028 and ties it to access to propulsion technology (Defence Security Asia, search summary). It is not in service [Verification Required].
In a ramjet the air enters supersonic but is slowed to subsonic for combustion; in a scramjet both intake and combustion are supersonic.Correct (PIB feature: inlet exit flow is subsonic in a ramjet and supersonic in a scramjet). [Additional] Holding a flame in supersonic flow is the central difficulty; ISRO named igniting and holding the flame at supersonic speed, the air intake and fuel injection as technologies demonstrated in 2016 (PIB).

  • SMR (IAEA): up to 300 MW(e) per unit; microreactors typically up to 10 MW(e); refuelling every 3-7 years (conventional 1-2); competitiveness still to be proven; more than 80 designs.
  • Nuclear Energy Mission: Budget 2025-26, Rs 20,000 crore for SMRs; at least five indigenous SMRs by 2033; Budget 2024-25 announced the private-sector BSR partnership.
  • BSR-220: 220 MWe PHWR for captive industrial power; NPCIL RFP 31 Dec 2024 (due 31 Mar 2026); user builds, NPCIL operates.
  • BSMR-200: indigenous PWR, slightly enriched uranium, BARC with NPCIL; Tarapur; outlay Rs 5,960 crore. SMR-55: 55 MWe PWR, 2 units, Rs 7,000 crore, Tarapur. HTGCR: up to 5 MWth, hydrogen by thermochemical (copper-chlorine) cycle, BARC Vizag, Rs 320 crore. Time to build: 60-72 months after sanction. All are approved in principle, none under construction (PIB, 5 Aug 2026).
  • ApuRVA: Advanced Purified Reactor Vessel Alloy, developed indigenously for BSMR-200 and SMR-55 reactor vessels.
  • US authorisation: DOE, 10 CFR Part 810, 26 Mar 2025: Holtec may share unclassified SMR-300 technology with Holtec Asia, Tata Consulting Engineers and L&T (summary).
  • World: Akademik Lomonosov (Russia): floating, 2 x 35 MW(e), commercial operation May 2020. Linglong One (China): land-based, Hainan, 125 MWe, commissioning (hot tests Oct 2026), target slipped to 2027.
  • Rocket: carries fuel and oxidiser; non-air-breathing. Jet: carries fuel only; air-breathing. Ramjet and scramjet: no moving parts; need a booster; ramjet burns in subsonic flow, scramjet in supersonic flow.
  • BrahMos (Mk-1): solid booster plus liquid ramjet; cruise about Mach 2.8. BrahMos-II: hypersonic version under development.
  • ISRO scramjet (28 Aug 2016): ATV, hydrogen fuel, Mach 6, about 5 s; fourth country. DRDO: HSTDV flight 7 Sep 2020; full-scale combustor ground test over 1,200 s on 9 May 2026 (liquid hydrocarbon endothermic fuel).

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Liability design: balance of victims, investors and safety. A capped, no-fault regime speeds compensation and lets private firms insure; graded caps (Rs 100-3,000 crore) fit plant size. The weakness is adequacy: even the top tier is a few hundred times smaller than a Fukushima-scale loss, so the real safeguards are the Government's share above the cap, insurance pools, a strong regulator and the retained deliberate-act and contract recourse.
  • Supplier accountability. Removing one recourse ground lowers vendor risk but weakens one quality incentive; offset it with contractual guarantees, NPCIL quality assurance and AERB inspection. Be careful to state exactly which ground was removed.
  • SMRs: promise and proof. Benefits are smaller land footprint, factory build, passive safety, non-electric uses (heat, hydrogen, desalination) and captive supply; limits are unproven economics (IAEA), first-of-a-kind cost (computed Rs 30-64 crore per MWe from PIB outlays), regulatory novelty and distributed waste and emergency planning near industrial users.
  • Self-reliance. India's SMR path leans on its PHWR base (BSR) and BARC designs with indigenous vessel alloy (ApuRVA) and control-rod drives; this lowers dependence on foreign "sensitive" items, while US-linked joint ventures (Holtec with L&T and Tata Consulting Engineers) add technology options.
  • Timelines and delivery risk. 60-72 months after sanction, and no sanction yet for the lead units, make "five SMRs by 2033" a stretch target; the first BSR contracts and the final SHANTI Rules are the near-term tests. A Chinese precedent (Linglong One, 2026 target missed) shows even leaders slip.
  • Strategic and diplomatic value. SMR export to Africa or West Asia is an aspiration; India must first demonstrate an operating plant, settle liability and safeguards rules and navigate export controls (India is outside the NSG).
  • Hypersonic propulsion as strategic technology. Scramjet work spans civil (reusable launchers) and defence (hypersonic cruise missiles); India's evidence is two flight demonstrations and long ground tests, not a fielded system. Dual-use technology means export controls and past technology denial have driven self-reliance (the HSTDV page says materials were developed in-house after denial; secondary).
  • Way forward: (i) notify the Act and finalise Rules with clear insurance, licensing and equity provisions; (ii) publish the liability tiers and how the CSC second tier would operate; (iii) sanction the lead SMRs and report progress against the 2033 target; (iv) run the BSR with transparent contracts and emergency-planning rules for industrial sites; (v) strengthen AERB capacity for novel designs; (vi) continue combustor-to-flight steps for scramjets with clear milestone reporting.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • India is already a CSC party (2016); the Act does not "give" access to CSC funds.
  • Do not quote the Chernobyl and Fukushima rupee figures from the class; use dated, sourced figures.
  • BSR-220 (PHWR, captive, NPCIL) is not BSMR-200 (PWR, BARC). BSMR-200 is also written "220 MWe" in some PIB releases.
  • HTGCR: up to 5 MWth (thermal), helium gas coolant; a hydrogen heat source, not a power plant.
  • "Approved in principle" is not "under construction". No Indian SMR is yet built.
  • SMR size: IAEA ceiling 300 MW(e); microreactors typically up to 10 MW(e), not 30 MW.
  • Refuelling: SMRs 3-7 years against 1-2 years (IAEA), not 5-7 against 2-3.
  • "Hot section" is a jet-engine term, not a reactor term.
  • Holtec's US authorisation (Part 810) is a technology-transfer licence, not an exemption, and pre-dates the SHANTI Act.
  • Linglong One is onshore on Hainan, not floating and not in the disputed sea; Akademik Lomonosov is the floating one.
  • BrahMos has a solid booster and a liquid ramjet. BrahMos-II (scramjet) is under development.
  • Scramjet fuel: ISRO's used hydrogen; DRDO's uses liquid hydrocarbon endothermic fuel.
  • Ground test is not a flight test: the 2025-2026 DRDO scramjet runs were combustor ground tests.
