74. Gaganyaan: India's human spaceflight programme (completes the Section 72 placeholder)

The previous note ended with the heading "Gaganyaan" and orientation points. Today's class fills it in.

74.1 The mission

  • Goal: to demonstrate human spaceflight by launching a crew of three to a 400 km orbit for a three-day mission and bringing them back safely by landing in Indian sea waters (ISRO).
  • Lead centre: ISRO's Human Space Flight Centre (HSFC), Bengaluru (ISRO).
  • Spacecraft: the Orbital Module = Crew Module + Service Module (ISRO).
    • The Crew Module is double-walled: a pressurised metal inner structure, and an unpressurised outer structure with a thermal protection system. It carries the crew, life support and parachutes, and re-enters.
    • The Service Module is unpressurised. It carries propulsion, power and thermal systems, and is discarded before re-entry.
  • Crew: four astronaut-designates, all Indian Air Force test pilots (Britannica):
    • Group Captain Prasanth Balakrishnan Nair, Group Captain Ajit Krishnan, Group Captain Angad Pratap and Shubhanshu Shukla.
    • They were named publicly on 27 February 2024 at VSSC, Thiruvananthapuram.
    • Three will fly on the first crewed mission.

[Correction] "Orbit in 400 km by 170 km": the crewed mission's target is a 400 km orbit (ISRO).
The figure of about 170 x 430 km belongs to the planned insertion orbit of the uncrewed G1 flight, which the Service Module is then to make circular. This was reported from the VSSC director via The Hindu (The Space Road, secondary) [Verification Required].
Duration: ISRO's stated goal is three days. Some secondary sources mention "up to a week" for H1 (Britannica), so use "three days" for the exam.

[Correction] "It will make India the 3rd country": India will be the fourth country to launch humans on its own spacecraft and rocket, after the USSR/Russia, the USA and China (Britannica).
"Crew members will be called vyomnauts": ISRO's official term is "Gaganyatri" (ISRO; PIB). "Vyomanaut" is an informal, media term.

74.2 Requirement 1: a human-rated launch vehicle (HLVM3)

  • What human rating means: every system of the LVM3 has been reconfigured for higher reliability and human-safety margins, and a Crew Escape System (CES) has been added. The result is called the HLVM3 (ISRO, 18 Dec 2024; ISRO).
  • Vehicle facts: a three-stage vehicle, 53 m tall, weighing 640 tonnes, with about 10 tonnes payload capacity to low Earth orbit (ISRO).
  • Stages (PIB, 29 Jul 2026; The Hindu):
    • HS200: two human-rated S200 solid boosters.
    • L110 (L110-G): a liquid core stage with two Vikas engines.
    • C32: a cryogenic upper stage with the CE20 engine (liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen).
  • Escape cover: the CES works from the launch pad until it separates after the atmospheric phase. ISRO says escape provision exists through all phases of ascent until orbit (ISRO).

[Correction] "The launch vehicle should have (A) an environmental control and life support system and (B) redundancy less than 0.2%"; "configuration HS-200, HL110, HC25":
(A) ECLSS location: the ECLSS is part of the Crew Module, not the rocket. Human rating of the rocket means higher reliability, redundant systems and the Crew Escape System (ISRO).
(B) Failure probability, not "redundancy": redundancy means duplicate systems; the target is a low probability of failure. The Hindu reports that HLVM3 must be certified to a failure probability of 1 in 2,000 or lower, i.e. 0.05% (The Hindu, secondary) [Verification Required for the official figure].
Upper stage: the July 2026 Lok Sabha reply names the HLVM3 stages as HS200, L110 and C32. The same reply also mentions a "C25-G" stage-preparation facility (PIB). Use HS200 - L110 - C32.

74.3 Requirement 2: re-entry technology

  • The re-entry corridor: a returning capsule must enter the atmosphere within a narrow range of flight-path angles.
    • Too steep: deceleration and heating become too severe, and the capsule or crew may not survive.
    • Too shallow: the capsule may skip off the upper atmosphere and back into space, like a stone skipping on water. (The class wrote that the atmosphere would "deflect" it.)
    • Specific corridor angles were not checked in this session †.
  • Heat: most of the heat comes from compression of air ahead of the blunt heat shield (shock heating), not mainly from "friction". The thermal protection system absorbs it: ablative materials char and carry heat away, and tiles insulate †.
  • Descent: after re-entry the Crew Module follows a ballistic path, then uses 10 parachutes in sequence (ISRO, IADT-01):
    • 2 apex-cover-separation parachutes (2.5 m);
    • 2 drogues (5.8 m);
    • 3 pilots (3.4 m);
    • 3 mains (25 m).
    These slow it to about 8 m/s for splashdown.

[Correction] "If it gets slightly sharp the friction will burn it down"; "a semi-parabolic path to splash in the sea":
Steep entry: the danger is excessive heating and g-load, and the heating is mostly shock (compression) heating, not friction.
Descent path: the capsule descends on a ballistic re-entry trajectory, then under parachutes. "Semi-parabolic" is not a standard term.

74.4 Requirement 3: crew escape, and the tests that prove it

  • Crew Escape System (CES): in an emergency it pulls the Crew Module, with the crew, away from the rocket to a safe distance. Parachutes then bring the module down (ISRO).
    • It uses five types of quick-acting solid motors (ISRO, TV-D1 brochure; PIB):
      • Low-altitude Escape Motor (LEM);
      • High-altitude Escape Motor (HEM);
      • Low-altitude Pitch Motor (LPM);
      • High-altitude Pitch Motor (HPM);
      • CES Jettisoning Motor (CJM).
  • Pad Abort Test (PAT), 5 July 2018: it simulated an emergency on the launch pad.
    • The CES and a 12.6-tonne simulated crew module flew to about 2.7 km on seven quick-acting solid motors.
    • They came down under parachutes about 2.9 km from Sriharikota; the test lasted 259 seconds (ISRO).
  • TV-D1, 21 October 2023 †: an in-flight abort at Mach 1.2 and an altitude of about 11.7 km, on a single-stage test vehicle (ISRO).
    • The crew module separated from the CES at about 17 km and splashed down about 10 km off Sriharikota.
    • TV-D2 is "in an advanced stage" (PIB, 29 Jul 2026).
  • Integrated Air Drop Test (IADT): a test of the parachutes, not of the escape motors.
    • In IADT-01 (24 Aug 2025), an IAF Chinook dropped a ~4.8-tonne simulated crew module from about 3 km (ISRO).
    • IADT-02 has also been completed (PIB).

[Correction] "In the pad abort the launch is called off by stopping the countdown"; "2 solid motors"; "this is the integrated air drop test":
(1) Pad abort: a pad abort is not a countdown hold. It is the escape system firing on the pad to carry the crew away from a failing rocket. Holding or scrubbing a countdown is a routine launch decision.
(2) Motors: the CES uses five types of solid motors, and PAT used seven. They pull the module away from the rocket and are then jettisoned; parachutes, not the motors, bring the module down.
(3) Which test is which: the in-flight escape test is TV-D1. The IADT tests only the parachute deceleration system.

74.5 Requirement 4: the environmental control and life support system (ECLSS)

  • Definition: a "system of systems" that keeps the cabin habitable. It controls temperature, humidity, pressure and carbon dioxide (PIB). It also supplies oxygen and water, removes trace contaminants and handles waste (The Hindu).
  • Cabin targets (as reported): about 0.8 atm, 22 °C and 45% relative humidity (The Hindu, secondary).
  • Status:
    • The ECLSS is being developed indigenously because foreign technology largely did not come (former ISRO chairman, via The Hindu).
    • An engineering model exists. A reduced version will be demonstrated on the uncrewed flights (PIB, 29 Jul 2026).
  • Example: POEM-3 fuel cell (2024).
    • A 100 W-class polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) fuel cell was tested on POEM-3, launched on PSLV-C58 on 1 January 2024. It generated 180 W from stored hydrogen and oxygen (ISRO).
    • A fuel cell yields electricity, pure water and heat in a single system, which suits crewed missions and space stations (ISRO).

[Additional] Radiation: the class listed "blocking radiation" under ECLSS. Strictly, radiation protection comes from the structure and shielding and from mission design (low orbit, short stay), plus dosimetry. The ECLSS controls the cabin atmosphere. Both are part of human rating.

74.6 Requirement 5: astronaut training

  • Training path (Britannica; PIB; The Hindu):
    • Generic training in Russia (through Glavkosmos) from early 2020.
    • Mission-specific training at ISRO's Astronaut Training Facility, Bengaluru: simulators, parabolic flights, survival training and yoga.
    • Training at NASA facilities for Axiom-4 (Shukla as pilot, Nair as backup).
  • Axiom-4: Shukla launched on 25 June 2025 on a SpaceX Dragon. He returned on 15 July 2025 after an 18-day mission, during which he completed seven Indian microgravity experiments on the ISS (ISRO).

[Correction] "Shubhanshu travelled to the ISS through Axiom in July 2025": he launched on 25 June 2025 and returned on 15 July 2025 (ISRO).
Names: the names are Angad Pratap, Ajit Krishnan and Prasanth Balakrishnan Nair (Britannica).

74.7 The mission sequence and Vyommitra

  • Sequence: three uncrewed orbital flights (G1, G2, G3) come before the first crewed flight, H1 (Britannica; India Today).
  • Expanded scope: in September 2024 the programme grew from three to eight missions. These include more uncrewed flights and the BAS-1 module, with a total cost of Rs 20,193 crore (Wikipedia, secondary; India Today).
  • Vyommitra is a half-humanoid robot that will fly on G1.
    • It can talk to ground control and read instrument panels (Britannica).
    • It monitors the cabin in place of a crew. Data from G1 will feed into the crewed missions.

[Correction] "There will be 3 Gaganyaan missions, the first 2 unmanned": that was the original (2018-19) plan of two uncrewed flights then one crewed. The current plan has three uncrewed flights before the first crewed flight, and the programme now has eight missions (India Today).
Status, 21 Nov 2026: G1 has not flown. The official target is the fourth quarter of 2026, but on 20 Nov 2026 ISRO officials told India Today, anonymously, that it is unlikely before February 2027. H1 is targeted for 2027 (India Today; Britannica).

74.8 Significance of Gaganyaan

  • Stepping stone: the crew module, life support, escape and recovery systems lead on to the BAS (BAS-1 2028, full station 2035) and an Indian on the Moon by 2040 (ISRO; PIB).
  • Space diplomacy: independent crew access, together with a national station, lets India offer flights and partnership on its own terms. Compare the debate over NASA's Moon Base invitation (Section 71.4).
  • Industry and start-ups: human-rated hardware, ECLSS and recovery systems create demand for high-reliability Indian manufacturing. They complement IN-SPACe's role in opening the sector †.
  • Soft power and inspiration: India would become the fourth nation with independent human spaceflight.
  • Science: microgravity research in biology, medicine and materials (see the Prelims 2025 PYQ below).

[Correction] "It will allow India to have its own space collaboration on the lines of the Artemis Accords": the Artemis Accords are a set of principles, not a flight programme, and India already signed them in 2023 (previous note, Section 69.1).
Better phrasing: "Gaganyaan and the BAS would let India lead its own partnerships, offering flights, station access or payload slots. The Prime Minister invited countries in September 2026 to be 'co-travellers' in India's programme" (Lowy).

75. Bharatiya Antariksh Station (BAS)

75.1 Context: the end of the ISS and the rise of Tiangong

  • International Space Station (ISS): a partnership of five agencies: NASA (USA), Roscosmos (Russia), ESA (Europe), JAXA (Japan) and CSA (Canada) (SpaceOdysseyHub, secondary).
    • It has been continuously occupied since 2 November 2000.
  • Retirement plan (SpaceNexus, checked 11 Oct 2026; KeepTrack):
    • NASA's plan of record is a controlled deorbit at the end of 2030 or early 2031, using a SpaceX-built US Deorbit Vehicle (contract worth up to $843 million, June 2024). The final burn is aimed at a remote ocean area, most likely the South Pacific.
    • NASA will decide in 2027 whether to proceed or extend.
    • A Senate bill to fly the ISS until 2032 cleared committee but has not passed.
    • Russia said in July 2026 that it would support operations to 2030.
  • China's Tiangong:
    • The core module was launched on 29 April 2021, and the station has been crewed since 2021.
    • It is the only station in orbit apart from the ISS (OrbitalRadar, 8 Nov 2026, secondary).
  • After the ISS:
    • NASA plans to buy services from commercial stations rather than build a successor. Candidates are Vast's Haven-1 (no earlier than Q1 2027), Axiom (first module 2028) and Starlab (2029).
    • Russia plans ROS (first module 2028), and India plans the BAS (OrbitalRadar; SpaceNexus).

[Correction] "The ISS will be decommissioned by 2030; after that the Chinese space station will be in orbit":
ISS date: the plan is to deorbit at the end of 2030 or early 2031. It is not fixed: NASA reviews it in 2027, and there are moves in Congress to extend it (SpaceNexus).
Tiangong: it is already in orbit and crewed (since 2021). It will not be the only station after 2030, because commercial US stations, Russia's ROS and India's BAS are all planned. Tiangong may, however, be the only government-owned station for a period if the replacements slip.

[Correction] "Space is defined as the 4th domain of warfare": NATO declared space an operational domain in 2019, its fifth after land, sea, air and cyberspace (NATO; NATO Parliamentary Assembly, 2025).
"Whoever controls space controls air, land and water": this is a strategic maxim, not a definition. Write: "space underpins navigation, communications, early warning and surveillance for operations in every other domain" (NATO).

75.3 Why a space station matters: strategic, scientific and economic

  • Strategic:
    • Autonomy: after the ISS retires, a national station avoids dependence on others for long-duration human presence in orbit.
    • Space situational awareness (tracking debris and objects to avoid collisions) is vital for any station's safety. India's main SSA effort is ground-based, through ISRO's tracking network †.
    • Prestige and diplomacy: the station can host partner astronauts and payloads, which links to India's "co-traveller" offer (previous note).
  • Scientific:
    • Microgravity research: life sciences, pharmaceuticals, materials science and advanced manufacturing are among the research priorities named for the BAS (India Today).
    • Human physiology in long stays: bone, muscle and radiation effects. This includes organ-on-chip studies like NASA's AVATAR (previous note).
    • Earth observation from the Lab Module's cupola.
    • Astrophysics: the ISS hosts the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer, which searches for antimatter and dark matter, and X-ray instruments that study black holes and neutron stars †. Whether the BAS will carry such instruments has not been announced.
  • Economic:
    • The commercial low-Earth-orbit economy includes private astronaut missions (Axiom has flown four to the ISS), research services, in-space manufacturing and tourism (OrbitalRadar).
    • NASA spends about $3-4 billion a year on the ISS and plans to buy services from commercial stations instead (SpaceOdysseyHub, secondary).

[Correction] "BAS will help in space control ... it will also be used for space denial": no official source describes a military or space-denial role for the BAS. Official material presents it as a civil human-spaceflight and research platform (India Today; PIB).
Where space denial belongs: it refers to counter-space capabilities such as India's anti-satellite test, Mission Shakti (27 March 2019) †.
Law: the Outer Space Treaty bans nuclear and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit, and military bases on celestial bodies †.
Treat the strategic value of the BAS as autonomy, presence and prestige, not as a weapon [Verification Required for any claim of a military role].

[Correction] "The LEO economy is worth millions of dollars": the scale is billions, not millions. For example, NASA's ISS operations alone cost about $3-4 billion a year (SpaceOdysseyHub, secondary).
No verified figure for the total commercial LEO market was found in this session, so none is quoted [Verification Required].
"PGB": the abbreviation "PGB" in the class notes could not be identified [Verification Required]. It may mean plant growth and biology.

  • Astronaut-designates (IAF pilots, named 27 Feb 2024): Prasanth Balakrishnan Nair, Ajit Krishnan, Angad Pratap and Shubhanshu Shukla.

    • Stages HS200 (solid) - L110 (liquid, 2 Vikas) - C32 (cryogenic, CE20).

  • NATO declared space its fifth operational domain in 2019.

Mains Points

  • Why human spaceflight is harder than robotic missions:
    • It needs human rating: reliability, redundancy and an escape system.
    • It needs life support, safe re-entry and recovery, and crew training and medicine.
    • There is zero tolerance for failure. This explains the step-by-step test ladder (SRE-1, CARE, PAT, TV-D1, IADT, G1-G3) and the delays.
  • Self-reliance:
    • Foreign ECLSS technology largely did not come, so ISRO is building it at home.
    • This slows progress but builds lasting capability for the BAS and the Moon programme.
  • Value of Gaganyaan and the BAS:
    • independent crew access after the ISS;
    • a microgravity laboratory for Indian science and industry;
    • a base for the 2040 Moon goal;
    • space diplomacy, through hosting partners and payloads;
    • soft power and inspiration;
    • demand for high-reliability manufacturing.
  • Costs and risks:
    • Gaganyaan costs Rs 20,193 crore, and only BAS-1 is funded (Rs 1,763 crore).
    • The later modules depend on an NGLV still under development.
    • Schedules have slipped repeatedly (crew flight first promised for 2022).
    • There is a risk to human life, and the opportunity cost against applications satellites must be justified.
  • Strategic angle, in balance:
    • Space is now a recognised operational domain (NATO 2019), and a station adds national presence.
    • But the BAS is a civil platform. Its security value lies in autonomy and space situational awareness, not weapons.
    • India should support norms against weaponising orbit.
  • Way forward:
    • Fly G1-G3 safely before setting crew dates.
    • Fund the later BAS modules alongside the NGLV.
    • Bring in industry to build modules (VSSC has already invited industry for BAS-01).
    • Offer partner slots on Gaganyaan and the BAS.
    • Link the BAS to commercial LEO services and to the 2040 Moon roadmap.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Which country number: India will be the 4th country with independent human spaceflight, not the 3rd.
  • Name of the crew: ISRO's term is Gaganyatri; "vyomanaut" is informal.
  • Where the ECLSS is: it is in the Crew Module, not the rocket. Human rating of the rocket means reliability and crew escape.
  • Upper stage: HLVM3 uses C32 (CE20), not "C25" or "HC25".
  • Pad abort vs countdown hold: a pad abort is the escape system firing on the pad. A countdown hold is a routine stop.
  • TV-D1 vs IADT: TV-D1 tested the escape system in flight; the IADT tests only the parachutes.
  • SRE-1 vs CARE:
    • SRE-1 (2007, PSLV) orbited for about 10-12 days.
    • CARE (2014, LVM3-X) was suborbital (126 km) and did not orbit.
  • Axiom-4 date: launched in June 2025 and returned in July.
  • Number of missions: there are three uncrewed flights before H1, not two. The programme now has eight missions.
  • BAS launcher: BAS-01 goes on the LVM3, and later modules on the NGLV, not "LMLV".
  • Inclination: the BAS is at 51.5° and the ISS at ~51.6°: similar, not identical.
  • Space as a domain: it is the fifth domain (NATO 2019), after land, sea, air and cyber.
  • Tiangong: it is already operating; it does not take over "after 2030".

30-Second Revision

  • Gaganyaan: 3 Gaganyatris; 400 km; 3 days; Indian sea waters.
  • HLVM3: HS200 + L110 + C32, plus the Crew Escape System.
  • Five pillars: human-rated rocket, re-entry, crew escape, ECLSS, training.
  • Tests: SRE-1 (2007), CARE (2014), PAT (2018), TV-D1 (2023), POEM-3 fuel cell (2024), IADT-01 (2025).
  • Crew and flights:
    • Crew: Nair, Krishnan, Pratap, Shukla. Shukla flew on Axiom-4 (Jun-Jul 2025).
    • Flights: G1-G3 uncrewed (Vyommitra), then H1 crewed in 2027. G1 is likely in 2027, not 2026.
  • BAS:
    • 5 modules, ~52 t, 400-450 km, 51.5°, 3-4 crew.
    • BAS-01 in 2028 (LVM3); the full station by 2035 (NGLV).
  • Context: the ISS ends around 2030-31; Tiangong has flown since 2021; space is NATO's 5th domain.
  • Next class: James Webb Space Telescope.

2019 | UPSC CSE Mains | General Studies Paper III | 10 marks, 150 words (per deepmentor; secondary)

"What is India's plan to have its own space station and how will it benefit our space programme?"

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official scheme):

  1. Plan:
    • The BAS: 5 modules, about 52 t, 400-450 km, 51.5°, 3-4 crew.
    • BAS-01 in 2028 on LVM3 (approved Sep 2024), and the full station by 2035 with the NGLV.
    • Docking was proven by SpaDeX (Section 75.2).
  2. Benefits:
    • a microgravity laboratory, and long-duration human-spaceflight experience;
    • autonomy after the ISS retires;
    • a base for the 2040 Moon goal;
    • industry participation, and partnership and diplomacy (Section 75.3).
  3. Challenges: only BAS-1 is funded; it depends on Gaganyaan and the NGLV; the ECLSS must be developed at home.

2017 | UPSC CSE Mains | General Studies Paper III | 10 marks, 150 words (per Anantam IAS; secondary; compilations differ slightly in wording)

"India has achieved remarkable successes in unmanned space missions including the Chandrayaan and Mars Orbitter Mission, but has not ventured into manned space missions. What are the main obstacles to launching a manned space mission, both in terms of technology and logistics? Examine critically."

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official scheme):

  1. Technology: the five pillars of Sections 74.2-74.5: human rating, re-entry, crew escape, ECLSS, and the parachutes and recovery.
  2. Logistics: astronaut selection and training (Russia, Bengaluru, NASA); naval recovery; a ground and tracking network; cost.
  3. Critical update: each obstacle has been tested in turn (SRE-1, CARE, PAT, TV-D1, IADT, POEM-3; Figure 89), but G1 is still pending. Safety-first delays are justified.
