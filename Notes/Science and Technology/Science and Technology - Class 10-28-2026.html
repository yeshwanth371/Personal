11. India's Three-Stage Nuclear Power Programme: Idea and Overview

11.1 Concept

  • The resource problem: India has limited uranium but one of the largest thorium reserves in the world. The programme is designed to multiply the domestic fissile material step by step, so that thorium can eventually become the main fuel (PIB, 7 Apr 2026; PIB, 5 Aug 2026).
  • Closed fuel cycle: spent fuel is reprocessed to recover plutonium (and uranium) and fed into the next stage, instead of being discarded as waste (PIB, 5 Aug 2026). This is different from the "once-through" cycle used in many countries.
  • Sequential: Stage 2 can start on a large scale only after Stage 1 has produced enough plutonium; large-scale Stage 3 follows "only after adequate capacity of FBRs is installed" (PIB, 5 Aug 2026).
  • Origin: conceived by Dr Homi J. Bhabha in the 1950s. The class says it was conceived in 1954 and formally adopted in 1958; the PIB pages say only "first conceived by Bhabha". [Verification Required] the exact 1954 and 1958 milestones (no official page opened today gives them).
  • Experience first came from research reactors (Section 12), not from power reactors.

12. Research Reactors: The Technology Base

Research reactors are nuclear reactors whose primary purpose is not electricity: they supply neutrons for research, radioisotope production, materials testing, training and reactor-physics experiments. BARC (Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, Trombay) operates most of them; IGCAR (Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research, Kalpakkam) operates the fast-reactor research units.

13. Stage 1: Natural Uranium and Light-Water Reactors

13.1 Principle: moderator, coolant and fuel

  • In a thermal reactor a moderator slows fast neutrons so that fission of U-235 is more likely; the coolant carries heat away to make steam.
  • Light water (H2O) absorbs many neutrons, so a light-water reactor needs enriched uranium. Heavy water (D2O) absorbs far fewer neutrons, so a heavy-water reactor can run on natural uranium. This is why India, which lacked enrichment capacity in the 1960s, chose PHWRs for Stage 1 (Wikipedia, BARC: "most efficient reactor design in terms of uranium utilisation").
  • While burning U-235, the U-238 in the fuel captures neutrons and becomes Pu-239, the input for Stage 2 (Figure 2, 26 Oct note).

13.4 PHWR: the backbone

  • PHWR (also called CANDU, CANada Deuterium Uranium): fuel is natural uranium in horizontal pressure tubes; heavy water is moderator and also coolant, kept under pressure to minimise boiling. Natural-uranium fuel and online refuelling (without shutdown) are key advantages.
  • Canada link: early 1960s agreement between the DAE and Atomic Energy of Canada Ltd (AECL); Rajasthan Atomic Power Project construction began in 1963; RAPS-1 was commissioned in 1973 (class: unit installed in 1972). Canada withdrew support after the 1974 Pokhran test; India completed RAPS-2 on its own (1981) with Indian industry (BHEL, L&T), and thereafter built PHWRs indigenously (Wikipedia, RAPS; NPCIL: RAPS-2 on 1 Apr 1981).
  • Indigenous designs (BARC): IPHWR-220 (developed from the CANDU units at Rawatbhata), IPHWR-540 (Tarapur 3 and 4, 2005-06) and IPHWR-700 (Wikipedia, BARC), with the 700 MWe design upgraded with Generation III+ safety features (class: "3+").

13.6 PWR / LWR and Generation III+ reactors

  • PWR (pressurised water reactor) = light-water reactor in which light water acts as moderator and coolant at high pressure so it does not boil; fuel is enriched uranium. These were imported (or proposed) under the Nuclear Cooperation Agreements (Section 6 of the 27 Oct note).
  • Generation III+ designs claim added passive safety (systems that work by gravity, natural circulation or stored energy without needing power or operator action). The "among the safest" description is the vendors' and designers' claim, not an independent ranking.

14. Stage 2: Fast Breeder Reactors (FBR)

14.1 Principle

  • Fast neutrons, no moderator: an FBR sustains the chain reaction with fast (unmoderated) neutrons, so there is no moderator; hence "fast neutron reactor".
  • Fuel: a core of plutonium-uranium mixed oxide (MOX) fuel, using Pu-239 recovered by reprocessing PHWR spent fuel. The core is wrapped in a blanket of fertile U-238, which fast neutrons convert into more Pu-239 (PIB, 5 Aug 2026).
  • Breeding: the reactor produces more fissile material than it consumes. The thorium route: replace (part of) the blanket with Th-232, which becomes U-233, the Stage 3 fuel. The PIB says the PFBR "is designed to eventually use Thorium-232 in the blanket" (not yet).
  • Coolant: liquid sodium, which transfers heat well and does not slow neutrons much. Hence the term liquid-metal fast breeder reactor (LMFBR).

[Correction] The class says the FBR fuel is "a mixed oxide of plutonium-239 and uranium-235". The fuel is a mixed oxide of plutonium and uranium (the uranium is mainly U-238, natural or depleted), not U-235 (PIB, 5 Aug 2026). [Additional] Terminology: sodium is chemically a light alkali metal, so "light metal / liquid metal fast breeder reactor" in the class is understood as liquid-metal (sodium-cooled) fast breeder reactor. A fast reactor is called a "breeder" only when BR > 1; fast reactors can also be run as "burners" (BR < 1) to consume plutonium or long-lived waste.

14.3 Strategic significance

  • Fuel for Stage 3: only FBRs can breed enough U-233 from thorium; hence utilisation of thorium depends on the number of FBRs built (class and PIB 5 Aug 2026).
  • Closing the fuel cycle: spent PFBR fuel will be reprocessed and recycled back into the reactor (PIB 7 Apr 2026).
  • Nuclear deterrence link (class): the class says FBRs can yield plutonium that helps maintain a minimum number of nuclear weapons, and that FBRs may in future be split into civil (safeguarded) and military lists, as PHWRs were in 2008. This is an analyst interpretation: the DAE presents the PFBR as an energy-security programme. Plutonium from the blanket (lightly irradiated U-238) can be of weapons grade; plutonium from fuel burnt to high burn-up in the core is reactor grade. [Verification Required] whether and how any FBR will be placed under IAEA safeguards; India's declared position was not found in the pages opened. Do not treat any statement on military capability as an official specification.

[Correction] The class says "the plutonium obtained is weapon grade" and that FBRs are "the source of weapon-grade plutonium" for the nuclear arsenal. This is too absolute. Plutonium grade depends on irradiation time, and the official position is civil. The first draft's statement that the FBR was "kept outside safeguards" is a class statement about the current list; see the [Verification Required] note above.

14.4 Issues

  • Technology and cost risk: sodium fires (sodium burns with air and reacts with water), complex fuel-handling, decades-long delay before criticality.
  • Plutonium supply: Stage 2 needs enough reprocessed plutonium, which depends on the size of the PHWR fleet and reprocessing capacity.
  • Proliferation and safeguards perception because of plutonium handling (see the 27 Oct note, Section 7, on safeguards and the separation plan).
  • Waste and reprocessing: closed fuel cycle needs safe handling of high-level liquid waste (vitrification); BARC has indigenous vitrification technology (Wikipedia, BARC).

15. Stage 3: Thorium-Based Nuclear Power

15.1 Why thorium

  • Source: thorium occurs in monazite, a mineral of the beach sands of the southern and eastern coast, especially Kerala (also Odisha, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh). Monazite is reported to contain about 8-10% thorium (Wikipedia, BARC, secondary). India is often cited as having about 25% of the world's known thorium reserves (secondary, estimates vary; [Verification Required]).
  • Not fissile itself: Th-232 is fertile; it must be converted to fissile U-233 (Figure 2 of the 26 Oct note). Therefore thorium cannot start a chain reaction alone; it needs a starter fissile driver (U-235 or Pu-239 now; U-233 later).

15.3 Accelerator Driven System (ADS)

  • Spallation: a high-energy proton striking a heavy nucleus (such as lead) knocks out many neutrons.
  • Uses: (i) burn thorium without needing a large fissile inventory; (ii) transmute long-lived waste (minor actinides) into shorter-lived products, reducing the time for which waste must be isolated (BARC, via Wikipedia); (iii) inherent safety, because the reaction is switched off with the accelerator.
  • Limits: requires a very reliable, high-power accelerator, a robust target and special materials; costly and unproven at power-plant scale.

15.4 Issues and way forward for Stage 3

  • Dependence on Stage 2: thorium can be used at scale only after enough FBR capacity exists to supply fissile material; Stage 3 is therefore a long-term option, not a near-term source. Within the 100 GW-by-2047 mission (27 Oct note), thorium is unlikely to be a major share [analytical view; Additional].
  • Technology maturity: AHWR, IMSBR and ADS are at design or R&D stages; fuel fabrication and reprocessing for U-233 (which carries strongly penetrating gamma radiation from decay products, standard knowledge) need shielded facilities.
  • Way forward: complete PFBR power operations and learn from them; build FBR capacity; demonstrate thorium irradiation in operating reactors (already done in research and some power reactors, Wikipedia BARC); fund and demonstrate AHWR/IMSBR; international collaboration (IIFC; ITER-type fusion work is separate and not part of this programme).

16. Timeline of the Indian Programme

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Three stages: Stage 1 PHWR (natural U, heavy water) → Pu-239; Stage 2 FBR (Pu-U MOX, U-238 blanket, liquid sodium) → more Pu-239 and U-233; Stage 3 thorium (U-233) → AHWR, CHTR, molten salt, ADS. Closed fuel cycle.
  • Fleet (NPCIL, Aug 2026): 24 units, 8,780 MWe = 20 PHWR (6,460 MW) + 2 PWR (2,000 MW) + 2 BWR (320 MW). Capacity factor Apr-Aug 2026: 89%.
  • PFBR: 500 MWe, Kalpakkam, BHAVINI, IGCAR design; first criticality 6 Apr 2026; MOX fuel; U-238 blanket; sodium coolant; India would be second country after Russia with a commercial FBR once fully operational.
  • Tarapur 1 and 2: GE BWR, 1969, 160 MWe each; 1963 agreement; shut 2020 and restarted 2026 after refurbishment.
  • 700 MWe PHWR fleet: 10 units, 7,000 MW (Kaiga 5-6 construction started; Mahi Banswara 1-4 implemented by ASHVINI, NPCIL-NTPC JV). Operating 700 MWe units: Kakrapar 3, 4; Rawatbhata 7.
  • Capacity projection: 8,780 MW to 22,480 MW by 2031-32 (Lok Sabha, 5 Aug 2026).
  • Research reactors: APSARA (1956), CIRUS (1960), ZERLINA (1961), PURNIMA I-III, DHRUVA (1985), KAMINI (U-233), FBTR (1985, Pu-U carbide).
  • Thorium: fertile, not fissile; Th-232 → U-233; monazite beach sands; AHWR: heavy-water moderator, boiling light-water coolant, about 300 MWe, no unit under construction.
  • ADS: sub-critical; proton accelerator + lead target (spallation); IIFC with Fermilab; switching off the accelerator stops the reaction.
  • Uranium resource: AMD has established 4,42,800 t in-situ U3O8 (PIB, 5 Aug 2026).

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Logic of the programme: a resource-driven strategy (limited uranium, large thorium) built on a closed fuel cycle; its merit is long-term energy sovereignty; its weakness is time: each stage waits for the previous one.
  • Stage 1 delivered, with a nuance: PHWRs are indigenous, standardised (fleet mode) and ran at about 89% capacity factor in Apr-Aug 2026; but nuclear power is still only about 3% of electricity (3.1% in 2024-25), and fleet projects are only now moving to construction.
  • Stage 2 is the bottleneck: PFBR reached first criticality only in April 2026, roughly two decades after the project started; success will be judged by power operation, availability, sodium-system safety and the reprocessing-fabrication chain. Plutonium supply and doubling time decide how many FBRs are possible.
  • Thorium is a long game: AHWR, IMSBR and ADS are at design/R&D stage; they should be pursued as options, but near-term capacity to 2047 depends on PHWRs, LWRs and SMRs (27 Oct note).
  • Self-reliance versus imports: PHWR and PFBR are indigenous; fuel and LWR technology depend on imports and cooperation. A closed cycle reduces future uranium dependence but not the near-term need.
  • Safety, waste and public acceptance: sodium hazards, reprocessing waste (vitrification), regulatory independence (AERB statutory status under the SHANTI Act, 27 Oct note) and local consent at project sites.
  • Strategic dimension: FBR plutonium has deterrence implications attributed by analysts; maintaining transparency about civil-military classification of FBRs will affect safeguards and trade (cross-reference: 27 Oct note).
  • Way forward: (i) complete PFBR commissioning and publish operating data; (ii) sanction and build commercial FBRs on the PFBR learning; (iii) expand reprocessing and MOX fabrication capacity; (iv) keep AHWR/IMSBR/ADS R&D funded with clear milestones; (v) pursue fuel security through multiple suppliers while the closed cycle matures; (vi) strengthen regulation, waste management and public engagement.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Stage 1 fuel is natural uranium (PHWR), not "uranium-235"; enriched uranium is used only in the BWR/PWR light-water units.
  • FBR fuel is Pu-U MOX, not Pu-239 + U-235; the blanket is U-238 now, thorium later.
  • Criticality is not electricity: PFBR reached first criticality on 6 Apr 2026; grid power is a later step.
  • Thorium is fertile, not fissile; it needs U-233 (or a Pu/U-235 driver).
  • Breeder is not "perpetual motion": BR > 1 only creates more fissile material; it still consumes fuel and needs reprocessing.
  • Moderator is absent in an FBR; in PHWR heavy water is both moderator and coolant; in PWR light water is both; in AHWR heavy water moderates and boiling light water cools.
  • Fleet mode (10 reactors) is not the 16 approved 700 MWe units; two earlier pairs (Kakrapar 3-4, Rawatbhata 7-8) are separate.
  • Tarapur 1-2 are old BWRs (1969) restarted in 2026, not "defunct"; Tarapur 3-4 are PHWRs.
  • CHTR is not HTGCR; ADS is sub-critical, not critical.
  • Jaitapur and Kovvada are proposed, not under construction; Kudankulam 1-2 operate, 3-6 are under construction.

30-Second Revision

Three-stage programme (Bhabha, 1950s): uranium scarce, thorium abundant; closed fuel cycle. Research reactors first (APSARA 1956, CIRUS 1960, DHRUVA 1985; IGCAR: KAMINI U-233, FBTR 1985). Stage 1: 24 units, 8,780 MWe = 20 PHWR + 2 PWR (Kudankulam) + 2 BWR (Tarapur); 700 MWe PHWR fleet of 10 (7,000 MW); 22,480 MW by 2031-32. Stage 2: FBR, Pu-U MOX, U-238 blanket, sodium coolant, BR > 1; PFBR 500 MWe, BHAVINI, first criticality 6 Apr 2026. Stage 3: Th-232 to U-233; AHWR (300 MWe, heavy-water moderator, boiling light-water coolant), CHTR, molten salt (IMSBR), ADS (sub-critical, accelerator + lead target, IIFC with Fermilab). Thorium use depends on FBR capacity.
