78. James Webb Space Telescope (completes the Section 76 placeholder)

The previous note ended with the heading "James Webb Telescope" and orientation points. Today's class fills it in.

78.1 The mission

  • Agencies: the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is an international programme led by NASA with ESA and the Canadian Space Agency (CSA) (NASA).
    • ESA provided the Ariane 5 launch, the NIRSpec spectrograph and half of MIRI (ESA/Webb); CSA provided the guidance sensor and the NIRISS instrument (NASA).
  • Launch: 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 from Kourou, French Guiana (NSSDCA). It reached its orbit around L2 on 24 January 2022 and began science in July 2022 (Orbit Codex, secondary).
  • Telescope: 18 hexagonal gold-coated beryllium segments form a 6.5 m (flat-to-flat about 6.6 m) primary mirror with about 25 m2 of collecting area, against Hubble's 2.4 m mirror and 4.5 m2 (NASA).
  • Wavelengths: 0.6 to 28.5 μm, from visible red to mid-infrared (NASA).
  • Location: it orbits the Sun about 1.5 million km from Earth around the second Lagrange point, L2 (NASA).
[Correction] c152 - Distance to L2. The rough notes say "approx 1.4 Mil KM". NASA gives about 1.5 million km (about 1 million miles) for both L1 and L2 (NASA). That is about 1% of the Sun-Earth distance †.

78.2 Lagrange points and halo orbits

Principle: at a Lagrange point, the gravitational pull of two large masses precisely equals the centripetal force required for a small object to move with them (NASA).

  • A three-body problem has five such solutions, named after the 18th-century mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange (NASA).
  • Geometry: L1, L2 and L3 lie on the Sun-Earth line; L4 and L5 form equilateral triangles with the Sun and Earth (NASA). L4 leads Earth by 60° in its orbit and L5 trails by 60° †.
  • Stability:
    • L4 and L5 are stable: objects there orbit them without assistance, and some small asteroids are known to orbit the Sun-Earth L4 and L5 points (NASA).
    • L1, L2 and L3 are only metastable. Objects drift away unless they keep station with small periodic rocket thrust (NASA).
  • Halo orbits: a spacecraft circles the point rather than sitting on it.
    • Webb's orbit around L2 takes about 6 months and is similar in size to the Moon's orbit around Earth. It keeps the telescope out of the shadows of both Earth and Moon (NASA).
    • Aditya-L1 is in a halo orbit around L1 (ISRO).

78.3 Why infrared, and how Webb is kept cold

  • Why infrared:
    • Light from the most distant galaxies is stretched by the expansion of the universe. Visible light emitted by very distant galaxies reaches us as infrared (NASA).
    • Infrared also penetrates dense dust clouds that block visible light, where stars and planets form (NASA).
  • The problem with infrared is heat: any warm object glows in infrared, including the telescope itself, so the mirror and instruments must be very cold (Orbit Codex).
  • Passive cooling: a five-layer, tennis-court-sized sunshield (about 22 m x 12 m) always keeps the telescope in shade (NSSDCA; Northrop Grumman).
    • The telescope operates at about −225 °C (NASA), about 45 K (Northrop Grumman).
    • The near-infrared instruments sit at about 39 K by passive cooling alone (Orbit Codex, secondary).
  • Active cooling (one instrument): the Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) needs about 7 K. A closed-cycle helium cryocooler brings it down to that temperature (Orbit Codex, secondary; NSSDCA confirms cryocoolers).
  • Lifetime: designed for 5 years with a 10-year goal. NASA now states that Webb carries enough propellant for more than 20 years of science (Orbit Codex, secondary). Propellant is needed for station-keeping at metastable L2 (NASA).
[Correction] c153 - Cooling and lifetime. The rough notes say that infrared "causes heating", that coolant is circulated to keep the mirrors sensitive, and that a coolant leak puts the telescope's life in question. The corrected position:
  • The telescope must be cold because its own warmth would emit infrared and swamp faint signals; observing infrared does not heat it.
  • The mirrors are cooled passively by the sunshield. No coolant flows through them.
  • Only MIRI is actively cooled, by a closed-cycle cryocooler, not by an expendable coolant that can run out.
  • Webb's working life is set mainly by station-keeping propellant (now enough for more than 20 years) and hardware wear, since it cannot be serviced.
  • The concern in the notes fits earlier infrared telescopes that used liquid helium, such as Spitzer, whose cryogen ran out in 2009 †.

78.6 Searching for life: habitable zones and biosignatures

  • Habitable ("Goldilocks") zone: the range of distances from a star where a planet's surface could hold liquid water (UPSC Prelims 2015, Q45 explanation). Liquid water is the first requirement of life as we know it.
  • What JWST measures: the gases in an exoplanet's atmosphere, through transmission spectra (NASA). It cannot see organisms. A gas that life produces on Earth is a candidate biosignature, not proof of life.
  • Biosignature gases named in class:
    • methane;
    • phosphine;
    • dimethyl sulfide (DMS): on Earth, it is produced overwhelmingly by marine life (SpaceDaily), mainly phytoplankton and microbes.

79.1 The mission

  • What it is: India's first space-based mission to study the Sun (ISRO), described in PIB's 3 Nov 2026 release as India's first space-based solar observatory (PIB).
  • Launch and orbit:

  • Why L1: a satellite in a halo orbit around L1 has the major advantage of continuously viewing the Sun without any occultation or eclipses (ISRO).
  • Status (3 Nov 2026): operational at L1, providing continuous observations (PIB).
  • Classification: Aditya-L1 is a solar physics mission, not a space-weather mission. Its data nonetheless help India build towards space-weather prediction (PIB, 23 Jul 2026).
[Correction] c160 - "Solar observability" and "placed at L1". Aditya-L1 is India's first space-based solar observatory. It is in a halo orbit around L1, not parked exactly at the point, because L1 is metastable and needs station-keeping (NASA).

79.2 The temperature anomaly (coronal heating problem)

  • Core: about 15 million °C, hot enough to sustain nuclear fusion (NASA).
  • Outward to the surface: temperature falls to about 5,500 °C at the photosphere, the visible "surface" (NASA).
  • Above the surface: temperature rises again, through the thin chromosphere, up to about 2 million °C in the corona (NASA).
  • The puzzle: the source of coronal heating has been a scientific mystery for more than 50 years (NASA). Explaining it is a core Aditya-L1 objective (ISRO).
  • Aditya-L1's VELC observes coronal plasma at about 1.8 million K in the green iron emission line at 530.3 nm (PIB).
[Correction] c161 - Coronal temperature. The rough notes give "3-4 million °C" for the corona. NASA gives up to about 2 million °C (NASA), and VELC's green line samples plasma at about 1.8 million K (PIB). Higher values occur locally in active regions and flares †. The rise begins just above the photosphere, in the chromosphere and transition region.

79.3 The Sun's magnetism and the sunspot cycle

  • Solar dynamo: the Sun's magnetic fields are generated by a dynamo below its surface (NOAA). The Sun is made of plasma, an electrically charged gas, so moving plasma creates magnetic fields.
  • Differential rotation: plasma rotates at different speeds: once in about 25 Earth days at the equator and about 36 days at the poles (NASA).
  • Sunspots: regions where the magnetic field is so strong that it blocks convective heat flow to the visible surface (NOAA). Being cooler, they look dark.
  • The cycle: a new solar cycle comes roughly every 11 years. Over each cycle, the Sun goes from calm to active and stormy, and its magnetic poles flip at the peak (NASA).
  • Solar Cycle 25:
    • It began in December 2019 (NASA).
    • NASA, NOAA and the Solar Cycle Prediction Panel announced on 15 Oct 2024 that the Sun had reached its solar maximum period (NASA).
    • SIDC later placed the smoothed peak in October 2024, at a sunspot number of about 161 (Specialty Digest, secondary).
[Correction] c162 - The magnetic cycle.
  • The sunspot number rises and falls over about 11 years, and the polarity flips once per cycle (NASA). So the field returns to its original orientation only after two cycles, about 22 years (the magnetic or Hale cycle) †.
  • Sunspots are cooler because strong magnetic fields block convection (NOAA). They appear in varying numbers at irregular places, not at a "regular interval".
[Correction] c163 - Solar maximum is not the cause of extreme summers. The rough notes link solar maximum to recent extreme summer temperatures. This is not supported:
  • Over a strong cycle, the Sun's total brightness changes by only about 0.1%, which affects global average temperature by 0.1 °C or less (NOAA).
  • Since 1750, greenhouse-gas warming has been over 270 times greater than the slight extra warming from the Sun (NASA, citing IPCC AR6).
  • Recent heat extremes are driven mainly by human-caused greenhouse warming, with natural variability such as El Niño adding to it †.

79.4 CMEs, geomagnetic storms, flares and the solar wind

  • Coronal mass ejection (CME): a large eruption from the Sun in which huge amounts of magnetised plasma are thrown into interplanetary space (ISRO).
    • Differential rotation twists and stresses magnetic field lines over active regions. When they suddenly reconnect, stored magnetic energy is released as a flare and/or a CME †.
    • Solar flares are understood to result from the release of magnetic energy (ISRO).
  • Aurora: particles released by the Sun interact with Earth's magnetic field (CSA). Earth's magnetic field diverts charged particles towards the poles, where they excite oxygen and nitrogen atoms, which glow †.
  • Geomagnetic storm: a CME's magnetised plasma disturbs Earth's magnetosphere. The changing field induces currents in long conductors †. The effects include:
    • GPS and navigation failures;
    • damage to power grids;
    • intense auroras at low latitudes;
    • disturbed satellite orbits through atmospheric drag;
    • interrupted shortwave radio on polar flights.

    UPSC's 2022 key lists these five and excludes tsunamis and forest fires (Prelims 2022 Q40).

  • Recent storms: the two most intense storms of Solar Cycle 25 came on 10 May and 10 Oct 2024. Aditya-L1 data, combined with ground magnetometers, helped explain them (PIB). Strong flares cause radio blackouts on Earth's sunlit side, and storms produce low-latitude auroras (NASA).
  • Solar flare: a sudden, intense burst of electromagnetic radiation. Large flares can disrupt radio communication and navigation, damage satellites and raise radiation hazards for astronauts (ISRO).
    • Flare classes, weakest to strongest: A, B, C, M, X (Specialty Digest).
    • The largest flare as of 15 Oct 2024 was X9.0, on 3 Oct 2024 (NASA SVS).
  • Solar wind: a continuous stream of corpuscular radiation, mainly protons and electrons, which shapes the space environment throughout the solar system (SPL/VSSC).
[Correction] c164 - Auroras and storm effects.
  • Earth's field does not simply "deflect" the plasma to make auroras. It channels charged particles along field lines towards the polar regions, where collisions make upper-atmosphere gases glow.
  • Induced currents damage transformers and grids and speed up corrosion of pipelines †. They do not make pipelines "burst".
  • Forest fires are not an effect of solar storms (UPSC Prelims 2022 key).
  • A CME is not always accompanied by a flare, and a flare not always by a CME. In the Aug 2026 study, small pre-flare brightenings appeared hours before major flares (ISRO).
  • The solar wind is continuous but not uniform: it has fast and slow streams †.

80. Biotechnology: AlphaFold and the protein-folding problem

This is the first class of syllabus Unit 3 (Biotech, Health, IPR); see the syllabus map. Space (Unit 2) concluded with Aditya-L1.

80.1 Principle: sequence decides shape, shape decides function

  • Proteins control and drive the chemical reactions of life. They also act as hormones, signal substances, antibodies and the building blocks of tissues (Nobel press release).
  • Proteins generally consist of 20 different amino acids, linked in long strings that fold into a three-dimensional structure, which is decisive for the protein's function (Nobel).
  • Levels of structure †:
    • primary: the amino-acid sequence;
    • secondary: local alpha-helices and beta-sheets;
    • tertiary: the full 3D fold of one chain;
    • quaternary: several chains together.
  • The folding problem: since the 1970s, researchers had tried to predict protein structures from amino-acid sequences, but this was notoriously difficult (Nobel). The Nobel Committee calls it a 50-year-old problem.
  • Why it matters: misfolded proteins can trigger severe diseases (Legacy IAS, secondary). Drug design needs the shape of the target protein, since most drug targets are proteins (Learnerz IAS, summarising The Hindu, secondary).
[Correction] c166 - "Infinite" shapes, and what a sequence change does.
  • The possible shapes are astronomically many, not infinite. A chain of n amino acids has 20n possible sequences, and each chain could in principle take a vast number of conformations (Levinthal's paradox) †. Yet a natural protein reliably adopts one precise shape.
  • Not every change in sequence changes the fold. Many substitutions are tolerated, while some single changes cause misfolding and disease, for example sickle-cell haemoglobin †.
  • The folding problem belongs to structural biology. "Proteomics" is the large-scale study of all the proteins of an organism †.

80.2 How AlphaFold works and what it achieved

  • Developer: Google DeepMind. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper presented AlphaFold2 in 2020 (Nobel).
  • Method: a deep neural network is trained on experimentally determined structures in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). It then predicts the structures of proteins that are not in the PDB (Legacy IAS, secondary).
  • Accuracy: AlphaFold regularly achieves accuracy competitive with experiment. In CASP14 it was the top-ranked method by a large margin, though it "still has some limitations" (EMBL-EBI).
  • Scale:
    • AlphaFold2 has predicted the structure of virtually all the 200 million proteins that researchers have identified, and more than two million people in 190 countries have used it (Nobel, Oct 2024).
    • The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Google DeepMind with EMBL-EBI) now offers open access to over 260 million predictions under a CC-BY-4.0 licence (EMBL-EBI, Oct 2026).
  • Speed: laboratory methods (X-ray crystallography, NMR, cryo-electron microscopy) are slow and expensive. Working out one shape could take a doctoral student years; AlphaFold does it in hours (Anantam IAS, secondary).
  • Successor: AlphaFold 3 (May 2024, with Isomorphic Labs) predicts structures and interactions of proteins with DNA, RNA, small molecules and ions. Its code was not released at first, which limited independent verification (Learnerz IAS, summarising The Hindu, secondary).
[Correction] c167 - Numbers and times.
  • Sequences: "more than 200 million" agrees with the Nobel press release, which says about 200 million proteins had been identified (Nobel).
  • Experimental structures: "1.7 lakh" is the approximate number of PDB structures available when AlphaFold2 was trained † [Verification Required]. The current PDB count is higher.
  • Time per protein: "around 2 decades for 1 protein" overstates it. Laboratory determination typically took months to years per protein (Anantam IAS, secondary). The problem, not each protein, was about 50 years old (Nobel).
  • Prediction time: AlphaFold takes minutes to hours per protein, depending on size and hardware †, rather than literally "seconds".
[Correction] c168 - What "solved" means. AlphaFold solved structure prediction for most single proteins, to near-experimental accuracy, but it "still has some limitations" (EMBL-EBI). Several things it does not do:
  • It does not explain why or how a protein folds (Legacy IAS, secondary).
  • Flexible and disordered regions, protein motion and complex interactions remain harder †.
  • Its effect on drug success is uncertain. Many candidates fail in clinical trials for reasons beyond structure (Legacy IAS).
"Reducing the cost of medicines" is therefore an expected benefit, not yet a demonstrated one.
[Correction] c169 - The prize split. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went one half to David Baker "for computational protein design" and the other half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper "for protein structure prediction" (Nobel). Hassabis and Jumper therefore shared one half; they did not receive the whole prize.

80.3 Implications, and Baker's protein design

  • Science: AlphaFold turns structure determination from a bottleneck into a lookup for most proteins. Researchers can now better understand antibiotic resistance and create images of enzymes that can decompose plastic (Nobel).
  • Disease and drugs: structures reveal how mutations disrupt function and where drugs can bind. AI helps mainly in target discovery and drug-target interaction; pre-clinical and clinical phases are still needed (Learnerz IAS, secondary).
  • Protein design (Baker): in 2003, Baker designed a protein unlike any other (Top7). His group has since produced proteins usable as pharmaceuticals, vaccines, nanomaterials and tiny sensors (Nobel). Designed enzymes that break down pollutants are a research goal †.
  • Dual use (ethics): the same tools could, in principle, help design harmful proteins or toxins. This calls for biosecurity screening and responsible-use norms †. The point is kept conceptual here.
  • India's position [Additional]: India needs large-scale GPU computing and more skilled AI scientists. Despite a strong history in protein X-ray crystallography and structural biology, it has not gained a first-mover advantage in AI tools for drug development (Learnerz IAS, summarising The Hindu, secondary).

Key Facts for Prelims

  • JWST:
    • NASA with ESA and CSA; launched 25 Dec 2021 on Ariane 5 from Kourou.
    • 18 gold-coated beryllium segments form a 6.5 m mirror (Hubble: 2.4 m).
    • Observes 0.6-28.5 μm; orbits around Sun-Earth L2, ~1.5 million km from Earth.
  • Cooling: a five-layer sunshield keeps the telescope at about −225 °C (~45 K). Only MIRI uses a closed-cycle cryocooler (~7 K). Propellant lasts more than 20 years.
  • Lagrange points: 5 in all. L1, L2 and L3 are collinear and metastable; L4 and L5 are stable, 60° ahead of and behind Earth, and collect asteroids.
    • L1 hosts SOHO, ACE, DSCOVR and Aditya-L1.
    • L2 hosts JWST, Planck and WMAP.
  • Webb's four themes: Early Universe, Galaxies Over Time, Star Lifecycle, Other Worlds.
  • Timeline: the universe is 13.8 billion years old; neutral hydrogen formed at ~378,000 years; Cosmic Dawn ran from ~50 Myr to 1 Gyr. The record galaxy is MoM-z14 (z = 14.44, 280 Myr after the Big Bang).
  • First Deep Field: SMACS 0723, seen as it was 4.6 billion years ago; it acts as a gravitational lens.
  • WASP-96 b: water vapour, clouds and haze; a hot gas giant ~1,150 light-years away. K2-18 b: methane robust; DMS disputed.
  • Aditya-L1:
    • PSLV-C57, 2 Sep 2023; halo orbit around L1 on 6 Jan 2024.
    • 7 payloads: 4 remote (VELC, SUIT, SoLEXS, HEL1OS) and 3 in situ (ASPEX, PAPA, MAG).
    • VELC is the primary payload, built by IIA.
  • Sun:
    • Temperatures: core ~15 million °C; photosphere ~5,500 °C; corona up to ~2 million °C.
    • Rotation: ~25 days at the equator, ~36 days at the poles.
    • The cycle lasts ~11 years and the poles flip at maximum. Cycle 25 peaked in Oct 2024.
  • Flare classes: A, B, C, M, X. The largest of Cycle 25 as of Oct 2024 was X9.0 (3 Oct 2024).
  • Nobel Chemistry 2024: David Baker (one half, computational protein design); Demis Hassabis and John Jumper (one half, protein structure prediction: AlphaFold2, 2020).
  • AlphaFold: ~200 million proteins predicted (2024). AlphaFold DB has over 260 million entries (EMBL-EBI with Google DeepMind). Proteins use 20 amino acids; Top7 (2003) was the first designed protein.

Mains Points

  • Why JWST matters:
    • Infrared sensitivity lets it see redshifted first galaxies and dust-hidden star formation.
    • Early galaxies brighter than predicted are forcing a revision of galaxy-formation theory, an example of observation correcting models.
    • Exoplanet spectroscopy turns "is there life?" into a testable question. The K2-18 b dispute shows why extraordinary claims need independent reanalysis.
  • Big science as diplomacy: a NASA-ESA-CSA partnership whose data are open after any exclusive period. Indian scientists can compete for time. This model is relevant to Indian proposals (for example, joining large telescopes †).
  • Aditya-L1 and space-weather security:
    • Solar storms threaten satellites, GNSS (including NavIC †), HF communication and power grids.
    • Aditya-L1 is a science mission, but it builds capability towards national space-weather forecasting (PIB). This links to protecting India's growing satellite constellation and grid.
  • Self-reliance in science payloads: all seven payloads were built in India by ISRO centres and academic institutions (IIA, IUCAA †, PRL †). Its results are in peer-reviewed journals, and joint work with ESA's Proba-3 shows a shift from technology demonstration to frontier science.
  • Climate literacy: solar variability (~0.1%) cannot explain recent warming. Attributing heatwaves to the Sun misdirects policy away from mitigation and adaptation.
  • AlphaFold, benefits: faster target discovery, understanding of antimicrobial resistance, enzyme design (plastic degradation), open data (CC-BY) that democratises research, and the first Nobel for AI-driven science.
  • AlphaFold, limits and risks:
    • It predicts static structures, not mechanism.
    • Clinical attrition is unchanged.
    • Restricted access to newer versions raises equity issues.
    • Dual-use biodesign risks require biosecurity screening and norms.
  • Way forward for India: invest in GPU compute and structural-biology data, train researchers across AI and biology, link this to the BioE3 policy † and India's pharmaceutical base, and develop guidelines for responsible AI in biology.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • L1 and L2 are both ~1.5 million km from Earth, not 1.4 million, and not 1.5 million km from the Sun.
  • L4 and L5 are stable; L1, L2 and L3 are not. Spacecraft fly halo orbits around L1 and L2.
  • JWST is passively cooled; there is no coolant to "leak". Spitzer was the cryogen-limited infrared telescope.
  • "4.6 billion years" is the age of the light from the foreground cluster in the First Deep Field, not the deepest view.
  • WASP-96 b's water is not a sign of life; it is a hot gas giant. K2-18 b's DMS is not confirmed.
  • JWST uses gravitational lensing; it did not discover it.
  • Black dwarfs are theoretical; white dwarfs and planetary nebulae are observed.
  • A constellation is not a galaxy.
  • The corona (~1-2 million °C) is hotter than the photosphere (~5,500 °C) but much cooler than the core (~15 million °C).
  • The Sun's poles flip every ~11 years, so the full magnetic cycle is ~22 years †.
  • Solar storms do not cause tsunamis or forest fires (UPSC 2022). Auroras come from particles channelled to the poles.
  • The Aditya-L1 magnetometer measures the interplanetary field at L1. SUIT is a near-UV imager, not a "holographic" instrument.
  • Hassabis and Jumper shared one half of the 2024 Chemistry Nobel; Baker received the other half.
  • AlphaFold predicts shape; Baker designs new proteins (the reverse direction).

Corrections Made

Substantive changes are marked where they occur and listed here. Spelling repairs are listed in the header.

  1. c152 - The L2 distance is ~1.5 million km, not 1.4 million.
  2. c153 - Infrared telescopes must be cold because they would glow themselves. JWST is passively cooled, and only MIRI uses a closed-cycle cryocooler. There is no coolant leak risk; propellant sets the lifetime (>20 years).
  3. c154 - The age is 13.8 billion years. Order: plasma, then recombination (~378,000 years), then the Dark Ages, first stars and galaxies. "Reorg to form pla." is unclear. Galaxies are not formed from constellations.
  4. c155 - "Phisphene" is phosphine. "Biofill" is unidentified (possibly chlorophyll). Biosignature gases are candidates and can be abiotic.
  5. c156 - 4.6 billion years is the foreground cluster in the First Deep Field. Background galaxies reach within ~1 billion years of the Big Bang, and the record is now 280 Myr.
  6. c157 - DMS on K2-18 b is not confirmed; it is disputed, and multiple reanalyses find no reliable evidence. Methane is robust.
  7. c158 - JWST uses, rather than confirms, gravitational lensing, which was established decades ago. Lensing supports dark matter and is consistent with general relativity.
  8. c159 - Sun-like stars end as white dwarfs with planetary nebulae. Black dwarfs are theoretical and not yet existing. A nebula is gas and dust, only partly plasma.
  9. c160 - Aditya-L1 is a solar observatory in a halo orbit around L1, not fixed at the point.
  10. c161 - The corona is up to ~2 million °C (VELC: ~1.8 MK), not typically 3-4 million. The rise begins in the chromosphere.
  11. c162 - The polarity flips each ~11-year cycle, so the full magnetic cycle is ~22 years. Sunspots are cool because magnetic fields block convection, and they are not regularly spaced.
  12. c163 - Solar maximum is not a significant cause of extreme summers: a ~0.1% brightness change gives ≤0.1 °C. Greenhouse warming dominates.
  13. c164 - Auroras come from particles channelled to the poles. Induced currents damage grids and corrode pipelines; they do not burst them. Forest fires are not an effect. Flares and CMEs can occur independently. The solar wind is not uniform.
  14. c165 - SUIT is a near-UV imager, not holographic. MAG measures the interplanetary field at L1. HEL1OS studies hard X-rays from flares. ASPEX studies solar-wind particles.
  15. c166 - The possible shapes are astronomically many, not infinite. Not every sequence change alters the fold. The folding problem belongs to structural biology.
  16. c167 - Laboratory structures took months to years each, not two decades; the problem was 50 years old. Prediction takes minutes to hours. The 1.7 lakh figure is marked for verification.
  17. c168 - AlphaFold solved structure prediction, not the folding mechanism. Its limits remain, and lower drug costs are an expectation, not a result.
  18. c169 - Hassabis and Jumper shared one half of the 2024 Chemistry Nobel; Baker received the other half.
