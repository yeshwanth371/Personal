66.1 Origin of the Moon: the giant-impact hypothesis

  • The Solar System formed about 4.5 billion years ago (4.57 billion for the oldest solids †).
  • The leading explanation for the Moon is the giant-impact hypothesis. A Mars-sized protoplanet named Theia struck the young Earth and flung molten and vaporised rock into orbit, and this debris gathered to form the Moon (NASA).
  • Evidence (NASA; Planetary Science textbook):
    • Apollo rocks have oxygen isotopes that match Earth's too closely to be a coincidence.
    • The Moon has a small core and a lower overall density than Earth.
    • Its rocks are poor in volatile elements, which fits a high-energy event.
    • Anorthosite covers the highlands. This light rock floats to the top of molten rock, so the young Moon must have had a deep magma ocean.
  • Speed of formation: simulations published in September 2026 suggest the Moon could have gathered within hours if both bodies were hot and soft. This is one model outcome, not a measurement (Live Science).

[Correction] "Thea" is Theia, a protoplanet rather than a full planet. "165-180 million years after the Solar System": the timing is disputed, and this figure sits at the late end of the range. NASA says Apollo rock ages point to about 60 million years after the Solar System began to form (NASA). Orbital models give 37-62 million years, and hafnium-tungsten dating gives about 50 million years. However, the oldest lunar crustal rocks crystallised only at about 200 million years (LPSC 2024 summary). For the exam, write "about 4.5 billion years ago, within roughly the first 100-150 million years".

  • The Moon and the origin of life (class point). The Moon may have helped life arise by creating conditions for prebiotic chemical reactions.

[Correction] This is a hypothesis, not established fact. The ideas usually cited are listed below †. None is proven, and life could have arisen without them. Write it in an answer as "may have contributed".
(1) A closer young Moon raised strong tides, and the wet-dry cycles of tidal pools may have concentrated organic molecules.
(2) The Moon steadies the tilt of Earth's axis, which keeps the climate relatively stable.

66.2 Why the focus has shifted to the lunar south pole

  1. Water ice in polar cold traps.
    • Crater floors near the poles never see the Sun and can be colder than about -200 °C (NASA, cited by Reuters). They trap water ice and other volatiles.
    • The ice could supply drinking water, oxygen for breathing (by splitting water) and hydrogen-oxygen rocket propellant (Reuters).
    • Making these on the Moon is called in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU). It avoids lifting every kilogram out of Earth's deep gravity well.
  2. The Moon as a fuel depot and launch pad.
    • Lunar gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's, and escape velocity is about 2.4 km/s against 11.2 km/s on Earth †.
    • So a rocket fuelled on the Moon needs much less thrust to leave. This is the logic of the Moon as a "gas station" for missions to Mars.
  3. Near-constant sunlight on high ground.
    • The Sun stays close to the horizon at the poles. Some crater rims and peaks are therefore lit for most of the year (Reuters). They are popularly called "peaks of eternal light".
    • Solar panels there could run almost continuously and power electrolysis of ice-water into H2 and O2.
    • The polar day-night cycle is milder than at the equator, where a night lasts about 14 Earth days (Reuters).
  4. Science.
    • Polar ice may record water delivered by comets and asteroids, and early volcanic gases (Al Jazeera).
    • The South Pole-Aitken basin may expose deep crust and possibly mantle (Section 66.3).
    • Such samples help date the Solar System's early bombardment, show how rocky planets differentiated into core, mantle and crust, and show which commercially useful elements are present.

[Correction] "Billions of tons of frozen water": the quantity is not known. Researchers commenting on China's Chang'e-7 mission in August 2026 noted that we know some form of water exists. What we still do not know is how much accessible ice the shadowed craters hold, how deep it lies or in what form (Phys.org, R. de Grijs, 21 Aug 2026). Older radar-based estimates, such as "at least 600 million tonnes" in north-polar craters from Chandrayaan-1's Mini-SAR †, are model-dependent. Write "large but unquantified deposits".

[Correction] "Peaks of eternal light" is a popular name. Reuters describes the rims as receiving "near-constant" sunlight (Reuters). The best-lit sites are illuminated for most, but not all, of the year †, so no confirmed spot is lit 100% of the time.

[Correction] "PSRs with frozen water could be used as human settlements": permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) are the resource sites, not the living sites. They are pitch-dark, colder than about -200 °C and hard to reach (Phys.org). Bases are planned on the sunlit rims and ridges next to PSRs, which offer a relatively stable thermal climate and steady solar power (Reuters). Robots or crews would make short trips into the craters to mine ice.

66.3 The South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin

  • The SPA basin is the Moon's largest impact feature, more than 2,500 km across and covering nearly a quarter of the Moon. It is among the largest known impact craters in the Solar System (NASA).
  • Location: the far side, stretching from Aitken crater to the south pole. It is more than 8 km deep (NASA gives about 10 km on average) and is the deepest and oldest lunar basin, formed about 4 billion years ago or earlier (NASA; NASA/LOLA).
  • Because it is so deep, the lower crust may be exposed there, something seen nowhere else on the Moon (NASA). Some mantle material may also have been dug up.
  • Link to Chandrayaan-3: PRL scientists propose that the extra sulphur at the Chandrayaan-3 site came from primitive mantle material. In their view, the SPA impact excavated this material about 4.3 billion years ago (ISRO, 30 Apr 2025; Section 66.10).
  • Why sample it: SPA rocks could date the Moon's oldest large impact and the early bombardment, which bears on when Earth became habitable (NASA). They also show how the Moon differentiated, help pin down the timeline of the early Solar System and reveal its chemistry, including potentially useful elements.

[Correction] "Aitken basin, one of the deepest craters in the entire Solar System, where crust and some mantle are exposed":
Name: the correct name is the South Pole-Aitken basin. It is the largest, deepest and oldest basin on the Moon and one of the largest in the Solar System.
Mantle exposure: this is inferred, not directly confirmed (NASA).
Samples: China's Chang'e-6 (2024) has already returned the first far-side samples, collected inside the SPA basin (AP). Chandrayaan-4 aims to bring back samples from the south polar region, the first from there (Section 66.11).

66.5 Chandrayaan-1 (2008): orbiter and Moon Impact Probe

  • Agency: ISRO. Launch: 22 October 2008 on PSLV-C11 (the XL version) from Sriharikota. Orbit: about 100 km polar lunar orbit (NASA).
  • Payloads: 11 instruments, 5 Indian and 6 from the USA, UK, Germany, Sweden and Bulgaria. They included NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3) and Mini-SAR (NASA).
  • Moon Impact Probe (MIP): a 29 kg probe released on 14 November 2008. It crashed near Shackleton crater at the south pole (NASA).
    • Its mass spectrometer CHACE, from SPL/VSSC, gave direct evidence of water (H2O) in the sunlit lunar exosphere (VSSC).
    • The probe carried the Indian flag to the surface.
  • Most important result: evidence of water and hydroxyl (OH) on the lunar surface.
    • M3 found it across much of the surface, with the strongest signals at the poles (NASA). It was announced in September 2009.
    • Chandrayaan-1 data were later used to map polar surface ice.
  • End: contact was lost on 28-29 August 2009, likely from power-supply failure caused by overheating. This was short of the planned two years, but ISRO said about 95% of objectives were met (NASA).

[Correction] "Discovery of water on Moon" needs nuance.
(1) The surface detection came mainly from NASA's M3 instrument flying on Chandrayaan-1. India's own CHACE detected water in the thin exosphere during the MIP's descent (VSSC).
(2) What was found was mostly water and hydroxyl bound in or adsorbed on the soil, in small amounts, plus signs of ice in polar cold traps. It was not lakes or free water.
(3) "Helped in the quantification of water at the poles" overstates it. Chandrayaan-1 mapped and indicated polar water, and reliable quantities are still unknown (see above).

66.6 Chandrayaan-2 (2019): orbiter, lander and rover

  • Launch: 22 July 2019 on GSLV Mk III-M1 (now LVM3). Elements: orbiter, Vikram lander and Pragyan rover (ISRO).
  • What went wrong: Vikram's descent was normal down to about 2.1 km altitude. Communication was then lost, and the lander crash-landed on 6-7 September 2019. Pragyan was still inside Vikram, so the rover never deployed (ISRO).
  • What went right: ISRO said 90-95% of mission objectives were accomplished.
    • Precise launch gave the orbiter fuel for about 7 years instead of the planned one (ISRO).
    • Its Orbiter High Resolution Camera (OHRC) is among the sharpest lunar cameras, at about 0.25-0.32 m per pixel. It helped choose the Chandrayaan-3 landing site (TOI, 11 Sep 2026).
    • The orbiter served as a backup relay for Chandrayaan-3 (TOI).

[Correction] "The lander and the rover crashed": the lander crashed with the rover still inside it. The rover was never deployed. Chandrayaan-2 is better described as a partial success. Its orbiter is still working in 2026 (TOI), and that is why Chandrayaan-3 needed no new orbiter.

66.7 Chandrayaan-3 (2023): soft landing near the south pole

  • Launch: 14 July 2023 on LVM3-M4 from SDSC-SHAR, Sriharikota (ISRO).
  • Objectives:
    • demonstrate a safe and soft landing;
    • demonstrate a rover moving on the Moon;
    • conduct in-situ experiments (ISRO).
    ISRO says all were "completely met" (ISRO).
  • Elements: a Propulsion Module (PM) plus a Lander Module (Vikram), with the 26 kg Pragyan rover inside the lander. Total mass was 3,900 kg (ISRO).
  • Landing: Vikram touched down on 23 August 2023 at about 6:04 pm IST (TOI; exact second †).
    • The site is about 69.37° S, 32.3° E (ISRO), named Shiv Shakti point.
    • 23 August is now National Space Day (CosmosLens).
  • Firsts: India became the fourth country to soft-land on the Moon, after the USSR, the USA and China. It was also the first to land in the lunar south polar region (ISRO).
  • [Additional] Engineering extras (the hop and the PM return were rehearsals for Chandrayaan-4):
    • Vikram did a short "hop": it re-fired its engines, rose about 40 cm and landed again nearby †.
    • The PM was later flown back from lunar orbit to an Earth orbit (ISRO).
  • [Additional] Compared with Chandrayaan-2, the lander was made "failure-tolerant": stronger legs, more fuel, a larger landing zone (4 km × 2.4 km) and extra sensors such as a laser Doppler velocimeter (ISRO).

[Correction] "Landed in the south pole region on 23 Aug at 3 past 6 pm, making India the first country to land at the south pole; the second was Japan". Four points need fixing:
Time: about 6:04 pm IST.
Place: Vikram landed at about 69.4° S, in the south polar region (southern high latitudes), about 600 km from the pole itself. It did not land at the pole.
Japan: Japan's SLIM (19 January 2024) made Japan the fifth country to soft-land on the Moon. Its site was about 13.3° S near Shioli crater, not the south pole (NASA).
Second south-polar landing: this was the US company Intuitive Machines' IM-1 "Odysseus" (22 February 2024). It landed at 80.13° S, closer to the pole than Vikram, but tipped over (NASA).

[Correction] "One lunar day is equal to 14 days on Earth": a full lunar day (sunrise to sunrise) lasts about 29.5 Earth days †. That is roughly 14 Earth days of daylight followed by about 14 days of night. Vikram and Pragyan were designed for one lunar daytime, about 14 Earth days (ISRO). They were put to sleep in early September 2023 and did not wake after the lunar night (CosmosLens).

66.9 Why the lander and rover stopped: solar versus nuclear power

  • Solar power and the lunar night.
    • Vikram (738 W) and Pragyan (50 W) ran on solar panels and batteries (ISRO).
    • During the ~14-day lunar night they get no sunlight. Near-surface temperatures at night fall below about 100 K (about -173 °C) at the equator (MNRAS), and lower in polar shadows.
    • Electronics that are not kept warm can crack or fail, which is why the mission was planned for one lunar day.
  • Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (RTG): converts the heat released by radioactive decay, usually of plutonium-238, into electricity using thermocouples. It works day and night for decades †. Examples are the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers and the Voyagers †.
  • Radioisotope Heating Unit (RHU): gives heat only, about 1 W each from a Pu-238 pellet, to keep hardware warm (TOI).
    • Chandrayaan-3's propulsion module carried two RHUs built by BARC. ISRO officials called it the first major joint ISRO-BARC project.
    • They were not fitted on Vikram or Pragyan because of the extra mass (TOI).
  • [Additional] Way forward: work is under way on systems that let landers survive the 14-day night (TOI). LUPEX's rover reportedly plans special insulation rather than RHUs (Wikipedia, secondary).

[Correction] "RTGs produce current by using the radioactivity during lunar night": an RTG does not use radiation directly. It turns the heat of decay into electricity through the thermoelectric (Seebeck) effect, and it does so continuously, not only at night. An RHU gives heat, not electricity. "Temperature below -100 °C" is true but understated: at night it drops to about -170 °C or lower (MNRAS), and below -200 °C in shadowed craters (Reuters).

[Correction] SHAPE "is looking to discover life on exoplanets": SHAPE observes Earth from lunar orbit (ISRO). Earth is the only planet known to host life. Its light, especially polarised light, acts as a reference signature of a habitable planet †. Future telescopes could compare distant exoplanets against that signature. SHAPE itself does not observe exoplanets. The PM's move back to Earth orbit was made partly to keep SHAPE observing Earth and partly to rehearse return manoeuvres for a sample-return mission (ISRO).

[Correction] "ChaSTE drilled the surface; at 8 cm depth the temperature dropped by 60 °C":
Method: ChaSTE pushed a probe in with a controlled penetration mechanism. It did not drill.
Values: ISRO's first graph (27 Aug 2023), as reported, showed about 50 °C at the surface and about -10 °C at 80 mm depth (TNIE). That is a fall of roughly 60 °C within 8 cm, so the class point holds in substance.
Caution: the readings were affected by heat from the lander itself (MNRAS).
Conclusion: lunar regolith has very low thermal conductivity and insulates the layers below, so the class conclusion stands.

[Correction] The LRA is a NASA instrument. It is passive, needs no power and lasts for decades (ISRO). It does more than mark Vikram's location. It acts as a fiducial (reference) point that helps refine the lunar coordinate system and pin down orbiter positions. It also gives clues to the Moon's dynamics and interior (ISRO).

[Correction] ILSA "finds out how stable the Moon is compared to Earth": ILSA is a seismometer. It measures ground vibrations from moonquakes, meteoroid impacts and artificial sources (Mint). The data help work out the structure of the crust and mantle. Seismic activity does say something about how "quiet" the Moon is, but the instrument's purpose is lunar seismicity.

[Correction] "Discovered sulphur on Moon, which supports volcanism":
(1) Sulphur was already known from Apollo samples. Chandrayaan-3 made the first in-situ measurement near the south pole and found it at higher levels than expected (ISRO).
(2) Its origin is debated. Volcanic outgassing is one possibility. Others are meteorite impacts and frost condensing from the thin atmosphere in cold polar soil.
(3) PRL's 2025 analysis favours a different source. It concluded that sulphur-rich primitive mantle material (troilite, FeS) was dug up by the SPA basin impact. It ruled out meteorites alone and cold-trapping as sufficient (ISRO).
Write "sulphur confirmed in situ; source under study (mantle, volcanic or impact)".

66.11 Chandrayaan-4: lunar sample return

  • Approval: Union Cabinet, 18 September 2024; cost Rs 2,104.06 crore. The cost covers the spacecraft, two LVM3 launches, deep-space network support and special tests. The mission was to be completed within 36 months of approval (PIB).
  • Purpose: demonstrate technologies for docking and undocking, landing, lunar ascent, sample collection and safe return to Earth. These are building blocks for an Indian landing on the Moon by 2040 and fit the goal of the Bharatiya Antariksh Station by 2035 (PIB).
  • Five modules (India Today):
    • Propulsion Module (PM), Descender Module (DM) and Ascender Module (AM);
    • Transfer Module (TM) and Re-entry Module (RM).
    They are launched as two stacks on two LVM3 rockets and docked in Earth orbit. A single LVM3 cannot lift the roughly 9,200-9,600 kg spacecraft (SpaceNexus; mass per secondary sources).
  • Sequence (class description, consistent with public reports; see Figure 81):
    1. The two stacks dock in Earth orbit. The PM raises the combined craft towards the Moon and then separates.
    2. In lunar orbit, the DM carrying the AM lands in the south polar region. The TM and RM stay in orbit.
    3. The DM drills and scoops regolith (reportedly up to about 3 kg) into a vacuum-sealed container in the AM (India Today).
    4. The AM lifts off and docks with the TM in lunar orbit. The container moves to the RM, and the AM is discarded.
    5. The TM and RM return to Earth. The RM separates, re-enters and lands with the samples.
  • Precursor: SpaDeX, launched 30 December 2024, demonstrated docking in Earth orbit before Chandrayaan-4 relies on it (SpaceNexus; docking achieved January 2025 †). The Chandrayaan-3 PM's return and Vikram's hop were earlier rehearsals.
  • Advantages of sample return (class points, expanded):
    1. Preservation: samples can be curated for decades and re-studied with future instruments. Apollo samples are still being analysed today.
    2. Sharing: they can be shared with other countries' laboratories, which builds scientific diplomacy.
    3. Better analysis: large lab instruments on Earth, such as electron microscopes, mass spectrometers and isotope-dating labs, can do much deeper analysis than anything that can be flown (secondary summary).
    4. [Additional] Infrastructure: the mission will set up Indian sample-curation facilities as "national assets" (PIB).

[Verification Required] These details are not in the PIB release, which names neither the modules nor the stacks:
(1) Stack split. The class says stack 1 is PM + TM + RM and stack 2 is AM + DM. A launch tracker lists the first launch as DM + AM and the second as TM + RM + PM (Space Launch Schedule). The groupings agree, but the launch order and the exact point where the PM separates (Earth orbit or lunar orbit) are not settled in official sources.
(2) Landing site. A fan site reports that OHRC imagery was used in February 2026 to choose a site on Mons Mouton; this is unconfirmed.

[Correction / Status] As of 20 November 2026, Chandrayaan-4 is approved but not launched.
Target dates: a Rajya Sabha committee report (March 2026) gives October 2027 (India Today). The ISRO chairman said in December 2025 that it would fly "by 2028" (SpaceNexus).
Spending: the committee noted slow spending, with Rs 34.60 crore of Rs 150 crore spent by January 2026 (India Today).
False claim: some exam websites claim Chandrayaan-4 "was launched on 12 August 2026". No official source supports this. Treat it as false.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Moon's origin: giant impact of the Mars-sized protoplanet Theia with proto-Earth, about 4.5 billion years ago. Evidence: matching oxygen isotopes, small core, magma-ocean anorthosite crust.
  • South pole draw: water ice in PSRs (colder than about -200 °C); rims with near-constant sunlight; ISRU (water, O2, H2 propellant); escape velocity ~2.4 km/s versus 11.2 km/s.
  • South Pole-Aitken basin: ~2,500 km across, more than 8 km deep; the largest, deepest and oldest lunar basin; on the far side. Chang'e-6 (2024) returned samples from it.
  • Chandrayaan-1:
    • Launched 22 Oct 2008 on PSLV-C11; 11 payloads.
    • Moon Impact Probe hit near Shackleton crater on 14 Nov 2008; its CHACE detected water in the exosphere.
    • NASA's M3 found water and OH on the surface. Contact lost Aug 2009.
  • Chandrayaan-2: launched 22 Jul 2019 on GSLV Mk III-M1. Vikram crashed (communication lost at about 2.1 km altitude), with Pragyan still inside. The orbiter still works; its OHRC gives about 0.25 m imagery.
  • Chandrayaan-3:
    • Launched 14 Jul 2023 on LVM3-M4. Landed 23 Aug 2023, about 6:04 pm IST, at 69.37° S (Shiv Shakti point).
    • India was the 4th country to soft-land and the first in the south polar region. 23 August is National Space Day.
  • Chandrayaan-3 payloads (7):
    • PM: SHAPE.
    • Lander: ChaSTE, ILSA, RAMBHA-LP, LRA (NASA).
    • Rover: APXS, LIBS.
    The PM carried 2 RHUs from BARC.
  • Chandrayaan-3 findings:
    • Sulphur confirmed in situ (LIBS).
    • Soil supports the magma-ocean hypothesis (APXS).
    • ~50 °C at the surface but ~-10 °C at 8 cm (ChaSTE).
    • First MEMS seismometer on the Moon (ILSA).
    • LRA pinged by NASA's LRO on 12 Dec 2023.
  • Chandrayaan-4:
    • Sample return. Approved 18 Sep 2024; Rs 2,104.06 crore; two LVM3 launches.
    • 5 modules: Propulsion, Descender, Ascender, Transfer, Re-entry. Docking in Earth orbit and in lunar orbit.
    • Not launched; target 2027-28.
  • Chandrayaan-5 = LUPEX: ISRO lander + JAXA rover, Japanese H3 rocket, about FY2028.
  • Other landers:
    • Japan's SLIM (Jan 2024) made Japan the 5th country to soft-land, at 13.3° S, not the pole.
    • IM-1 (Feb 2024) was the first commercial landing, at 80.13° S.
  • Artemis II (1-10 Apr 2026): the first crewed lunar flyby since 1972.

Mains Points

  • Why the south pole matters strategically:
    • Water ice enables ISRU and lowers the cost of long-term presence; the Moon becomes a refuelling stop on the way to Mars.
    • The few well-lit, ice-adjacent sites will be contested (US Artemis versus the China-Russia ILRS).
    • Being early (Chandrayaan-3, LUPEX) gives India data, prestige and a voice in shaping norms.
  • Chandrayaan as a model of learning from failure:
    • Chandrayaan-2's crash led to Chandrayaan-3's "failure-tolerant" design: stronger legs, more fuel, a wider landing zone, extra sensors.
    • The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter carried on and helped Chandrayaan-3. This frugal, step-by-step approach is the way forward.
  • Technology ladder:
    • Orbiting (Chandrayaan-1), then landing and roving (Chandrayaan-3).
    • Docking, ascent and re-entry (SpaDeX, then Chandrayaan-4).
    • Long-duration polar operations (LUPEX), then a crewed landing by 2040.
    Each step de-risks the next. The capabilities also serve Gaganyaan and the Bharatiya Antariksh Station.
  • Science gains: water in the exosphere and on the surface; a polar thermal profile; near-surface plasma; sulphur and mantle clues from the SPA impact. These are first-of-their-kind data from the south polar region, published in Nature, MNRAS and other journals.
  • Constraints:
    • Lunar-night survival, which needs RHUs/RTGs and nuclear-safety and regulatory capacity.
    • Heavy-lift limits: two LVM3 launches for Chandrayaan-4; NGLV is still years away.
    • Slow spending and delays.
    • Reliance on foreign partners for some instruments (M3, LRA, the LUPEX rover).
  • Governance:
    • The Outer Space Treaty (1967) bars sovereignty claims, but rules on resource use are unsettled.
    • India is in the Artemis Accords and works with JAXA and NASA. It should press for transparent norms on safety zones, debris and protecting pristine ice.
  • Way forward:
    • Fund Chandrayaan-4 on time.
    • Build sample-curation labs and nuclear power and heating units for space.
    • Involve industry and start-ups (the PIB stresses industry participation).
    • Diversify partnerships while keeping core technologies indigenous.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • "Landed at the south pole": Chandrayaan-3 landed at about 69.4° S, in the south polar region, about 600 km from the pole. IM-1 landed closer (80.13° S) in 2024.
  • "Second was Japan": SLIM made Japan the fifth country to soft-land, at 13.3° S. It was not a south-pole landing.
  • India's rank: India was the 4th country to soft-land (after the USSR, USA, China) but the first near the south pole.
  • Chandrayaan-1 water: Chandrayaan-1 did not "land" (the MIP was a deliberate crash). The surface detection was by NASA's M3; ISRO's CHACE found water in the exosphere.
  • Orbiter vs PM: Chandrayaan-3 had no new orbiter. Its propulsion module was not a relay; the Chandrayaan-2 orbiter served as backup.
  • SHAPE: it looks at Earth, not at exoplanets.
  • RTG vs RHU: an RTG gives electricity, an RHU only heat. Chandrayaan-3 carried RHUs only, on the PM.
  • LRA: it is NASA's and passive. It is not an Indian instrument.
  • Lunar day: about 29.5 Earth days in full; the mission lasted one lunar daytime of about 14 Earth days.
  • Chandrayaan-4 status: not launched as of Nov 2026. Claims of an August 2026 launch are false.
  • Chandrayaan-5: it is LUPEX, launched on Japan's H3, not on an ISRO rocket.
  • SPA basin: it is on the far side. Chang'e-6, not Chandrayaan, returned the first SPA samples.

30-Second Revision

  • Moon: formed after Theia hit Earth, about 4.5 billion years ago. The young Moon had a magma ocean.
  • South pole:
    • Permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) hold ice and are colder than about -200 °C. Lit rims beside them suit solar power and bases.
    • ISRU turns ice into water, O2 and propellant.
    • The SPA basin is the largest, deepest and oldest lunar basin.
  • Chandrayaan-1 (2008, PSLV-C11): an orbiter plus the Moon Impact Probe, and evidence of water.
  • Chandrayaan-2 (2019, GSLV Mk III): the lander crashed, but the orbiter still works.
  • Chandrayaan-3 (2023, LVM3):
    • Landed 23 Aug 2023 at 69.37° S. India was the 4th country to soft-land and the first in the south polar region.
    • Seven payloads: SHAPE; ChaSTE, ILSA, RAMBHA-LP, LRA; APXS, LIBS.
    • Found sulphur; the surface is ~50 °C but ~-10 °C at 8 cm.
    • The PM carried 2 BARC RHUs and went back to Earth orbit.
  • Chandrayaan-4: sample return; 5 modules, 2 LVM3 launches, docking; Rs 2,104 crore. Not yet launched; target 2027-28.
  • Chandrayaan-5: LUPEX with JAXA on an H3 rocket, about 2028.
  • Goals: the Bharatiya Antariksh Station by 2035 and an Indian on the Moon by 2040.

2023 | UPSC CSE Mains | General Studies Paper III | 15 marks (marks per IAS Board and SuperKalam; both secondary)

"What is the main task of India's third moon mission which could not be achieved in its earlier mission? List the countries that have achieved this task. Introduce the subsystems in the spacecraft launched and explain the role of the 'Virtual Launch Control Centre' at the Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre which contributed to the successful launch from Sriharikota."

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official scheme):

  1. Main task: a safe soft landing and roving. Chandrayaan-2's Vikram crashed in 2019 (Section 66.6).
  2. Countries: USSR/Russia, USA, China, then India (23 Aug 2023), and later Japan (Jan 2024). India was the first in the south polar region (Section 66.7).
  3. Subsystems: the propulsion module (SHAPE), the Vikram lander with its sensors (laser Doppler velocimeter, hazard-avoidance camera, throttleable 800 N engines) and four payloads, and the Pragyan rover with two payloads (Section 66.8; Section 66.10; Figure 79).
  4. Virtual Launch Control Centre (VLCC): located at VSSC, Thiruvananthapuram. It let launch-vehicle teams carry out checkouts of the LVM3 and monitor telemetry remotely, in step with Sriharikota. This adds redundancy and saves time (secondary model answers; not covered in class) †.

  1. c109 - Theia (a protoplanet), not "Thea"; the impact timing is disputed (about 50-60 million years after the Solar System began on most estimates); 165-180 million years is at the late end.

  9. c117 - Chandrayaan-3 landed at about 6:04 pm IST at about 69.4 deg S (south polar region, not the pole); Japan's SLIM (13.3 deg S) was the 5th country to soft-land; IM-1 (80.13 deg S) was the next south-polar landing.

  11. c119 - Chandrayaan-3 chain: rover to lander to IDSN directly, with the Chandrayaan-2 orbiter as backup; the PM had no relay role.
  12. c120 - An RTG converts decay heat to electricity (Seebeck effect) continuously; an RHU gives heat only; nights fall to about -170 deg C or lower.
  13. c121 - SHAPE observes Earth as a reference habitable planet; it does not search exoplanets for life.

  15. c123 - The LRA is a NASA passive fiducial marker for laser ranging and geodesy.
  16. c124 - ILSA is a MEMS seismometer for moonquakes and impacts, not a measure of "stability".
  17. c125 - Sulphur confirmed in situ; its origin is debated; PRL (2025) favours mantle material dug up by the SPA impact, not simply volcanism.
  18. c126 - Chandrayaan-4 is approved but not launched (target October 2027 / "by 2028"); the claim of a 12 August 2026 launch is false.
