37. Reusable Launch Vehicles (RLVs)

37.1 Concept

A reusable launch vehicle is one whose major parts (a stage, a booster, a winged orbiter or the fairing) are recovered and flown again after inspection, minor repair and refurbishment, instead of being discarded after one flight as in an expendable launch vehicle (ELV) such as PSLV, GSLV or LVM3.

[Additional] Reusability may be partial or full. Falcon 9 reuses its first stage (and fairings) but its second stage is expendable; NASA's Space Shuttle reused the orbiter and the solid boosters but discarded the external tank. No fully reusable orbital vehicle is in regular service as of 3 Nov 2026 (Pragnya IAS summary).

37.2 Advantages

  1. Lower launch cost. The class figure is a cut of 80-90% (c01).
  2. Higher launch frequency - the same hardware can be turned around and flown again instead of building a new vehicle.
  3. Less space debris - recovered stages are not left in orbit (c02).
  4. Lower ecological footprint - vehicles are manufactured less often (fewer materials and less manufacturing energy). [Additional] Launch emissions per flight (soot, alumina from solid motors) are not removed by reuse.
  5. Enabler for the next phase of exploration - human spaceflight, building and servicing a space station, carrying crew and cargo to a station, interplanetary missions and sample-return missions (c03).
  6. Research and development - drives work on re-entry, thermal protection, autonomous guidance and landing, and engine restart and throttling.
  7. Democratisation of space - lower cost opens space to universities, start-ups and smaller countries.

[Correction] "They do not result in the formation of space debris" overstates the case. Reuse removes the recovered stage from the debris problem, but partly reusable vehicles still leave expendable upper stages (Falcon 9's second stage, for example), and most new debris comes from satellites, collisions and break-ups. Write "reduce space debris". [Additional] Expendable stages can also be de-orbited: the C25 upper stage of LVM3-M5 (launched 2 Nov 2025) re-entered on 30 Jul 2026, within ISRO's Debris Free Space Missions (DFSM) guideline (India Today, 14 Aug 2026).

[Correction] "They are required for human space flight..." - RLVs are desirable, not strictly required. Apollo used the expendable Saturn V, and India's Gaganyaan uses the expendable human-rated LVM3 (HLVM3). [Correction] "VTVL can only 1 can be used for landing the places where the atmosphere is extremely thin or non-existent" means: only VTVL (propulsive landing) can be used where the atmosphere is very thin or absent (the Moon, Mars), because wings need air to produce lift. 39. Commercial Viability of RLVs

  • The commercial viability of an RLV depends on the number of launches per year: the design, recovery fleet and refurbishment facilities are fixed costs that pay off only if the same hardware flies often.
  • Class benchmark: for economic success the launch rate should be more than about 100 a year, as with Falcon 9. Checked: Falcon 9 flew 134 orbital launches in 2024 and 165 in 2025, a record; boosters landed safely on all but three flights in 2025, and 123 of the 165 carried SpaceX's own Starlink satellites (Space.com via Yahoo, 31 Dec 2025). The "100 a year" threshold is a rule of thumb, not an economic constant (c11).
  • ISRO manages only about 4-5 launches a year (class). [Additional] Worldwide, 2025 saw 328 launch attempts, 315 successful (ISRO's ISSAR-2025, released 8 Apr 2026); India had 144 spacecraft launched by the end of 2025 and GSLV-F15/NVS-02 was the 100th launch from Sriharikota (ISRO ISSAR-2025; Vision IAS summary). The Hindu's editorial of 2 Nov 2026 calls GSLV-F18 only ISRO's second successful launch of 2026 (see the 2 Nov note, Section 36).
  • Therefore ISRO needs: a higher budget; a rapidly growing private sector (launchers, satellites, constellations that create demand); and global cooperation so that foreign customers fill the manifest.
  • The RLV programme should be fast-tracked, because it underpins future exploration - Gaganyaan, the Bharatiya Antariksh Station (BAS) and an Indian on the Moon by 2040 (c12).

40. Types of Satellites

Classification by function: Earth observation, communication, navigation, scientific/space science and experimental/technology demonstration. Today's class covered Earth observation only.

40.1 Earth observation (EO) satellites - uses

  • Weather and meteorological information - cloud images, temperature and humidity profiles, cyclone tracking.
  • Resource monitoring - land use, forests, water bodies, minerals, ocean colour and fisheries.
  • Agro-climatic information - crop acreage and yield estimates, drought and soil moisture.
  • Intelligence gathering (surveillance and reconnaissance).
  • Cartography - mapping, urban planning, infrastructure.
  • Pollution monitoring - aerosols, gases, oil spills; also [Additional] disaster management (floods, landslides) and climate studies.

40.2 Orbits used by EO satellites

EO satellites are placed in polar (usually sun-synchronous) orbits and in the geostationary orbit.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • RLV = recovered and reflown launch vehicle; partial (Falcon 9 first stage) vs full reuse. ISRO's RLV-TD target: cut cost to LEO by about 80% (about $20,000/kg to $4,000/kg) - a target.
  • Winged: glides back, runway landing, no return fuel, more dead mass, needs atmosphere. VTVL: propulsive landing, fuel penalty, restartable and throttleable engines, works on airless bodies, faster turnaround.
  • Pushpak (RLV-TD): 6.5 m, 1.75 t; HEX-01 23 May 2016 (Sriharikota, about Mach 5); LEX-01 2 Apr 2023, LEX-02 22 Mar 2024, LEX-03 23 Jun 2024 at the ATR, Chitradurga, dropped from an IAF Chinook at 4.5 km; landing speed above 320 km/h; next: Orbital Re-entry Experiment (no date).
  • NGLV: Cabinet 18 Sep 2024; 30 t to LEO; reusable first stage; 3x LVM3 payload at 1.5x cost; Rs 8,240 crore; D1-D3 within 96 months; called "Soorya"; LOX-methane lower stages, cryogenic upper (ISRO chief, 2024).
  • LMLV: announced 23 Aug 2025; 80 t to LEO, 27 t to the Moon; 3 stages (liquid, liquid, cryogenic); 27 first-stage engines; ready by 2035 for a crewed Moon landing by 2040.
  • SE2000 semi-cryogenic engine (LOX + isrosene), 2,000 kN class, for the SC120 stage replacing LVM3's L110; power head at full 200 t thrust on 5 Sep 2026.
  • AVATAR: concept of an air-breathing, single-stage, runway take-off spaceplane (DRDO-linked); no hardware.
  • Falcon 9: 134 launches (2024), 165 (2025); 123 of 165 for Starlink. Global 2025: 315 successful of 328 attempts (ISSAR-2025).
  • EO satellites: weather, resources, agriculture, intelligence, cartography, pollution; orbits: polar/sun-synchronous (high resolution, global coverage) and geostationary (35,786 km; continuous view).

Mains Points

  • Why RLVs matter for India: cost per kg decides competitiveness in the commercial launch market; reuse supports BAS logistics, Gaganyaan follow-ons and lunar missions, and aligns with ISRO's Debris Free Space Missions (DFSM) by 2030 commitment.
  • Technology choice: winged RLVs (Pushpak) build re-entry and thermal-protection skills useful for crew return and spaceplanes; VTVL (NGLV) is the proven commercial route and the only route for Moon and Mars landers. India pursuing both hedges risk but splits scarce funds.
  • Economics: reuse pays only at high flight rates; ISRO's cadence (a handful a year, with PSLV failures in 2025-26) is the binding constraint, not technology alone. Demand creation - constellations, export launches, private payloads - must precede or accompany reuse.
  • Institutions and self-reliance: IN-SPACe, NSIL, technology transfer (SSLV to HAL, 2025) and private launchers (Skyroot, Agnikul) can raise cadence; NGLV's design of "maximal participation from Indian industry" shows the intended model.
  • Risks: long gestation (NGLV 96 months; LMLV 2035), shifting plans (NGLV vs LMLV), refurbishment cost uncertainty, payload penalty of recovery, and dependence on a few test ranges and launch pads.
  • Way forward: stable multi-year funding linked to milestones; complete the Orbital Re-entry Experiment and a sub-scale VTVL hop test; anchor demand through national constellations; open ISRO test facilities to private firms; partner internationally on recovery hardware and launch services; publish clear programme status to avoid confusion between approved and proposed vehicles.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Pushpak is not an orbital vehicle: it is a scaled technology demonstrator; it has not reached orbit.
  • ATR Chitradurga is a DRDO Aeronautical Test Range, not an Air Force base; the IAF supplied the Chinook.
  • Semi-cryogenic = LOX + refined kerosene (SE2000). LOX-methane (methalox) is a different combination, planned for NGLV. Cryogenic = LOX + LH2 (CE-20).
  • 80 t (LMLV) is to LEO; 27 t is to the Moon; NGLV's 30 t is to LEO.
  • Approved vs announced: NGLV has a Cabinet approval (2024); LMLV was announced (2025); TSTO and AVATAR are concepts.
  • RLV-TD vs AVATAR: separate projects.
  • Reuse does not end debris: expendable upper stages and satellites still create it.
  • Polar vs geostationary: PSLV places EO satellites in polar/SSO orbits (not "fixed in the sky"); geostationary satellites appear fixed (the 2018 PYQ).
  • Falcon 9 is partly reusable (second stage expendable); Starship aims at full reuse.

30-Second Revision

RLVs recover and refly hardware: lower cost (ISRO target about 80%), higher cadence, less debris, smaller footprint, wider access. Winged (Pushpak): glide, runway, no return fuel, more dead mass, needs air. VTVL (Falcon 9): powered landing, fuel penalty, restart/throttle engines, works on the Moon and Mars, faster turnaround. India: HEX-01 (2016) and LEX-01/02/03 (2023-24, ATR Chitradurga) done; Orbital Re-entry Experiment next. NGLV approved 2024 (30 t LEO, reusable first stage, Rs 8,240 crore); LMLV announced 2025 (80 t LEO, 27 t Moon, 2035); SE2000 semi-cryogenic power head at full thrust (Sep 2026) for LVM3. Viability needs cadence: Falcon 9 165 launches in 2025 vs ISRO about 5. EO satellites (weather, resources, crops, maps, intelligence, pollution) use polar/SSO and GEO orbits.
