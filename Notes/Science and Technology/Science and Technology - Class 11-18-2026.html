68. Chandrayaan-5 / LUPEX (completes the 66.12 placeholder)

The previous note ended with the heading "Chandrayaan-5" and a few orientation points. Today's class fills it. Time-sensitive facts are stated as of 21 November 2026.

68.1 What the mission is

  • Name: Chandrayaan-5 is the Lunar Polar Exploration (LUPEX) mission, a joint ISRO-JAXA mission and the fifth in the Chandrayaan series (ISRO, 15 May 2025).
  • Who builds what (ISRO; JAXA):
    • India (ISRO): the lander, plus a few science instruments.
    • Japan (JAXA): the launch vehicle (H3-24L) and the rover, which Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) is making.
    • Instruments also come from NASA and ESA, all aimed at volatiles in the polar region.
  • Target area: the lunar south polar region, near a permanently shadowed region (PSR) (ISRO).
  • Approval: the Government of India gave its financial sanction on 10 March 2025 (ISRO).
  • Launch: JAXA lists Japan's fiscal year 2028 on an H3 rocket from Tanegashima (JAXA special site). Its English page says "no earlier than 2028" (JAXA).
  • Duration: about 100 days on the surface, up to one year (JAXA). JAXA's English page gives "3.5 months after landing (max. one year)" (JAXA).

[Additional] Origins and recent milestones:
(1) The ISRO-JAXA study began with an Implementing Arrangement in December 2017 (Wikipedia, secondary; JAXA's timeline also starts in 2017).
(2) The implementing arrangement for the joint mission was signed in August 2025 (Wikipedia, secondary).
(3) The fifth Technical Interface Meeting (TIM-5) was held in Japan, as JAXA noted on 26 December 2025 (JAXA).
(4) On 6 November 2026, JAXA announced that NASA's neutron spectrometer (NS) will fly on the rover (JAXA).

68.2 Objectives: sub-surface water, not splitting it

  • Main objective: to measure the quantity, quality (form) and distribution of water in the south polar region. The data will show how far lunar water can be used for future sustainable exploration (JAXA leaflet).
  • How: the rover will land where ice is thought likely. It will drill regolith from the surface down to about 1.5 m and analyse it on board (JAXA).
  • Technology demonstrations: roving, surviving the lunar night and excavation. These feed into Japan's crewed pressurised rover (JAXA leaflet).
  • Why water matters: water can be electrolysed into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel. Using local water avoids carrying large quantities from Earth (JAXA leaflet; see Section 66.2 on ISRU).

[Correction] "Chandrayaan-5 will split water molecules and produce H and O2": LUPEX is a prospecting mission. It will find, measure and characterise water, not produce hydrogen or oxygen.
Splitting water by electrolysis (2H2O → 2H2 + O2) is the future use that JAXA gives as the reason the data matter (JAXA leaflet).
Likewise, "exploring the possibility of human settlement" is the long-term purpose: the results are meant to support future crewed activity and lunar bases (JAXA). The mission itself carries no habitat.

68.4 Why it matters for India

  • Step on the ladder: Chandrayaan-3 proved landing; Chandrayaan-4 (target 2027-28) is to prove sample return. LUPEX adds long-duration polar operations and a heavier Indian lander. [Additional] ISRO's 2025-26 annual report mentions a hot-fired 3.1 kN prototype engine for this lander (Wikipedia, secondary) †.
  • Science: one of the first direct, in-situ measurements of how much ice lies below the polar surface. Orbital data (such as Chandrayaan-1's M3) can only infer it. China's Chang'e-7 has similar goals but was postponed in August 2026 (Section 66.13).
  • Partnership model: a bilateral mission with a non-US partner. Japan is also a key Artemis partner: JAXA's pressurised rover sits in Phase 2 of NASA's Moon Base plan (NASA fact sheet). This links LUPEX to the Artemis discussion below.
  • Goal: ISRO calls Chandrayaan-5 one of the short-term milestones towards Indian astronauts landing on the Moon by 2040 (ISRO).

[Additional] Power: the rover will reportedly carry no radioisotope heater units (RHUs). It will instead use insulation to retain heat at night (Wikipedia, secondary). Compare Chandrayaan-3, whose lander and rover died in the first lunar night (Section 66.9).

69. The Artemis Accords and the space-law framework

69.1 What the Artemis Accords are

  • Definition: a non-binding set of principles to guide civil space exploration and use, grounded in the Outer Space Treaty of 1967 (OST) (US State Department).
  • Launched: on 13 October 2020. The Accords are co-led for the US by the State Department and NASA (State Department).
    • There were eight founding signatories: Australia, Canada, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, the UAE, the UK and the USA.
  • Legal character:
    • The text calls itself a "political commitment" (Section 1).
    • It is not eligible for registration as a treaty under Article 102 of the UN Charter (Section 13) (Cambridge ILM, 2023).
    • Questions of liability, intellectual property and technology transfer are left to separate bilateral or multilateral agreements (Cambridge ILM).
  • Scope (Section 1):
    • the Moon, Mars, comets and asteroids, including their surfaces and sub-surfaces;
    • the orbits of the Moon and Mars, and the Earth-Moon Lagrange points;
    • transit between these places (OrbitalIntel; Cambridge ILM).
  • How to join: a country submits its signature to the Government of the United States. The text has 13 sections (OrbitalIntel).
  • Membership: 78 signatories as of 28 October 2026. Vietnam is the newest (NASA).
    • Russia and China have not signed.
    • China's accession is in any case blocked by US domestic law (Cambridge ILM).
  • India: signed on 21 June 2023 as the 27th signatory (NASA list via OrbitalIntel; Hindustan Times).
  • Working method: under the Signatories Group Method of Operations (2024), the group meets twice a year. One meeting is a technical workshop; the other is a Principals' Meeting at the International Astronautical Congress (NASA, 28 Sep 2026).

[Correction] "An international framework modelled on the International Space Station (ISS)": the Accords are not modelled on the ISS.
Different instruments: the ISS is run under a legally binding Intergovernmental Agreement (1998 †), in which each partner contributes hardware. The Accords are a non-binding political commitment based on the OST (State Department; Cambridge ILM).
Where the ISS comparison fits: the multinational, hardware-sharing model resembles the Artemis programme, not the Accords. Examples are ESA's service module for Orion, Canada's robotics and Japan's pressurised rover (Section 70).

[Correction] "More than 70 countries have decided to join" and "Moon, Mars and other astronomical objects":
Count: the figure is 78 signatories as of 28 October 2026. Uzbekistan was 77th (19 Oct) and Vietnam 78th (28 Oct) (State Department; NASA).
Signing is not flying: signing the Accords is a statement of principles. It does not by itself mean taking part in Artemis missions.
Scope: the Accords cover the Moon, Mars, comets and asteroids (plus related orbits, Lagrange points and transit), not all "astronomical objects".

69.3 Where the Accords sit in space law

  • The five UN space treaties were concluded between 1967 and 1979: the OST, the Rescue Agreement, the Liability Convention, the Registration Convention and the Moon Agreement (McGill IASL).
  • OST, Article II: outer space, including the Moon, is not subject to national appropriation "by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means" (quoted in Kruckel, 2025).
    • It does not say clearly whether extracting resources is allowed. That is the gap the Accords and national laws try to fill.
  • Moon Agreement (1979):
    • Its Article 11 makes the Moon and its resources the "common heritage of mankind".
    • It bars any state, organisation or person from owning the surface, sub-surface or resources in place.
    • Parties promise an international regime to govern exploitation (Space Law Journal, 25 Sep 2026).

[Correction] "Moon treaty 1979": the formal name is the Agreement Governing the Activities of States on the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies, or Moon Agreement. The UN General Assembly adopted it on 5 December 1979, and it entered into force on 11 July 1984 (UNOOSA; UN treaty record).
Parties: it has only 17 parties. No state with its own human spaceflight capability is a party (Space Law Journal).
India: India signed it on 18 January 1982 but has not ratified it (UN treaty record).
Saudi Arabia: it withdrew with effect from January 2024, the first withdrawal from any UN space treaty (McGill IASL; Space Law Journal).
So the class point that existing mechanisms are "inadequate" is fair, but for a precise reason: the OST is silent on resources, and the Moon Agreement lacks the major space powers.

70. NASA's Artemis programme

70.1 What it is

  • Artemis is NASA's programme to return humans to the Moon, this time to the south polar region, and to build up to an enduring presence and later crewed Mars missions (NASA).
  • Core NASA systems (NASA):
    • the SLS (Space Launch System) super heavy-lift rocket;
    • the Orion crew spacecraft;
    • Exploration Ground Systems at Kennedy Space Center.
  • Commercial landers: astronauts go from Orion to the surface in commercial human landing systems. These are SpaceX's Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon (NASA, 23 Oct 2026).
  • Partners: ESA builds Orion's European Service Module, which supplies propulsion, power, water and air (ESA; Britannica). Japan and Canada are to contribute surface systems (Section 70.5).

[Additional] Precision: the class wrote "the spacecraft will be Orion; the vehicle will be SLS". This is correct, and the distinction matters. The SLS is the launch vehicle (rocket), Orion is the crew capsule, and the lander is a separate, commercially built vehicle. Artemis is US-led, but it is not a purely national effort.

70.3 Artemis I and II: the flight tests

  • Artemis I (2022):
    • It launched at 1:47 a.m. EST on 16 November 2022 from Launch Complex 39B. It splashed down off San Diego on 11 December 2022 (NASA).
    • Orion flew 268,563 miles (about 432,000 km) from Earth (NASA).
    • It met 161 test objectives. Unexpected char loss on the heat shield led to changes for later flights (NASA).
  • Artemis II (2026):
    • It launched on 1 April 2026 on an approximately 10-day lunar flyby with four astronauts, without landing (Wyss Institute).
    • As the class noted, it tested life support with a crew on board.
  • AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response) (NASA; NASA, Sep 2025):
    • It uses organ-on-a-chip devices about the size of a USB drive.
    • Each chip holds bone-marrow tissue grown from an individual astronaut's own cells.
    • The chips study how deep-space radiation and microgravity affect blood-forming cells.
    • It was the first time personalised organ chips flew beyond low Earth orbit.

[Additional] Why bone marrow, and what AVATAR is not:
Bone marrow is among the organs most sensitive to radiation and is the source of all adult blood cells. In low Earth orbit, Earth's magnetosphere shields astronauts from most cosmic and solar radiation; on a lunar trip it does not (NASA).
After the flight, the chips are being compared with ground-control chips and with the crew's blood samples. Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals; none is final yet (NASA, What's Next).
AVATAR is experimental research, not a medical product. Its long-term aim is personalised medical kits for astronauts.

70.4 Artemis III, IV and V

  • Artemis III (2027): a docking rehearsal in low Earth orbit.
    • Four astronauts on SLS/Orion will spend about 14 days in Earth orbit. They will test rendezvous and docking with lander test articles from Blue Origin (Blue Moon) and SpaceX (Starship) (NASA).
    • It needs three launches: New Glenn, SLS and Super Heavy-Starship (CBS).
    • NASA compares it to Apollo 9, which tested the lunar module in Earth orbit (Britannica).
    • Crew: NASA's Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas, and ESA's Luca Parmitano as pilot (ESA).
  • Artemis IV (2028): the first landing.
    • Two of the four crew will land while two stay in Orion (NASA).
    • NASA will decide as late as possible whether SpaceX or Blue Origin provides the lander (CBS).
    • Landers will no longer meet Orion in a near-rectilinear halo orbit, because the Gateway is paused.
      • Starship refuels in Earth orbit, docks with Orion there and pushes the stack to low lunar orbit.
      • Blue Moon uses three transfer stages and meets Orion in lunar orbit (NASA).
  • Artemis V (late 2028 target): the second landing. NASA's fact sheet lists "Artemis IV and V (2028): completion of lunar landings". After that, it plans more commercial, reusable hardware, initially aiming for landings every six months (NASA fact sheet).

[Correction] "Artemis III will dock Orion with the spacecraft of Blue Origin and Starship": Starship is SpaceX's vehicle, so the two partners are Blue Origin (Blue Moon lander test article) and SpaceX (Starship V3 test article) (NASA).
History: Artemis III was originally the first landing mission. NASA changed it into an Earth-orbit test in February 2026, and the landing moved to Artemis IV (Britannica; ESA).
Blue Moon version: NASA and ESA describe the Blue Moon test article as based on the Mark 2 crew lander. CBS calls it "Blue Moon Mark 1", so treat the version name with care.

[Correction] "Artemis 5 will have a robotic and crewed station at the south pole": Artemis V is a single crewed landing mission (the second), targeted for late 2028.
The robotic-then-crewed outpost is the separate, phased Moon Base programme (Section 70.5). NASA expects construction to begin around Artemis V (T-Minus Zero, citing NASA, 3 Mar 2026).
The Moon Base is a surface base. The planned lunar-orbit station, the Gateway, was paused on 24 March 2026 (Reuters; NASASpaceFlight).

70.5 NASA's Moon Base programme

  • Announced at NASA's "Ignition" event on 24 March 2026 (Times of India; Reuters).
    • The aim is a semi-permanent, expandable outpost at the lunar south pole, with habitats, rovers, power systems and landers (NASASpaceFlight).
  • Gateway paused: NASA paused the lunar-orbit station "in its current form". It is shifting resources to surface infrastructure and repurposing equipment and partner commitments (NASA fact sheet).
  • Three phases (NASA fact sheet; years from NASASpaceFlight and Hindustan Times):
    1. Build, test and learn (to about 2028-29):
      • commercial robotic landings through CLPS, up to 21 according to NSF;
      • Lunar Terrain Vehicles and propulsive "Moon drones" (hoppers);
      • radioisotope heater units for the lunar night, and communication satellites.
    2. Establish early infrastructure (2029-2032): semi-habitable infrastructure and regular logistics, including JAXA's pressurised rover.
    3. Enable continuous human presence (2032 onward):
      • heavier cargo;
      • Italy's (ASI) Multipurpose Habitats and Canada's (CSA) Lunar Utility Vehicle.
  • Cost: sources differ.
    • NASASpaceFlight reports "at least $30 billion" over the three phases (NSF).
    • Reuters reported a "$20 billion base" (Reuters).
    • Quote the figure with its source, or say "tens of billions of dollars".
  • Open to all signatories: NASA says it has invited every Artemis Accords signatory to take part through science payloads, technology demonstrations, CubeSats and other capabilities (NASA, 28 Sep 2026).

Context: NASA frames the urgency against China's plan to land astronauts by 2030 and the China-Russia ILRS (Reuters; CBS). The race is for the same few ice-rich, well-lit polar sites (Section 66.2).

71. India and NASA's Moon Base invitation: to join or not?

71.1 The invitation

  • When and where: at the 9th India-US Civil Space Joint Working Group (CSJWG), held at ISRO headquarters in Bengaluru on 5-6 August 2026. The statement was issued on 11 August 2026 (BusinessLine; Hindustan Times).
    • The CSJWG has existed since 2005 (Space.com).
  • Words of the statement: "NASA invited ISRO to join its Moon Base program, building on the two countries' partnership under the Artemis Accords" (joint statement of the US Mission and MEA, via BusinessLine).
    • The two sides also agreed to advance talks on open scientific data sharing under the Accords.
    • They reaffirmed the UN COPUOS guidelines on the long-term sustainability of outer space activities.
  • Framework: the meeting advanced cooperation under the India-US TRUST initiative (Transforming the Relationship Utilizing Strategic Technology) of February 2025 (Hindustan Times).
    • It builds on NISAR (launched July 2025) and Axiom-4, which took Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla to the ISS in June 2025 (India Today).
  • Follow-up: on 7 September 2026, US Ambassador Sergio Gor said at the Bengaluru Space Expo: "we want India by our side on the lunar surface" (Business Standard).
  • India's position: as of 22 September 2026, India had not accepted the invitation. It had not earmarked funding or named a hardware contribution (Lowy Institute).
    • No formal decision was found in the sources searched up to 21 November 2026.

[Correction] "India has received the invitation to join this initiative": India already joined the Artemis Accords in 2023, so the pending invitation is a different one. It is to join NASA's Moon Base programme, the surface-outpost part of Artemis (BusinessLine).
Not unique to India: NASA says it has invited every Accords signatory to take part (NASA, 28 Sep 2026). India's invitation came bilaterally and publicly, but it is not an exclusive offer.
No seat offered: no specific role has been made public, and in particular no seat for an Indian astronaut on an Artemis landing.

  • Australia: deep-space communication (the Canberra station of NASA's Deep Space Network †).

[Correction] "It might facilitate transfer of technology from other members": neither the Accords nor an invitation transfers technology by itself.

Write instead: "joining could create opportunities for co-development and learning, if India negotiates them into implementing arrangements". NISAR is an example of genuine co-development (Lowy).

71.3 Concerns (class points, with evidence)

The case for caution
ConcernReasoning and evidence
1. No detailed Indian lunar blueprintWithout a clear plan of its own, India would be fitting into a plan drawn up elsewhere (see c135 for what India has declared).
2. US interests take primacyPartners could be "expected to spend heavily while remaining junior partners in an architecture designed elsewhere" (Lowy). Evidence of the risk: when NASA paused the Gateway, the roles of Japan, Canada and ESA, which had built parts for it, became uncertain (Reuters).
3. Interoperability and technology lock-inSignatories commit to "reasonable efforts" to use current interoperability standards (NASA). If ISRO designs docking ports, power or communications to NASA standards, its hardware becomes tied to the US system, and later withdrawal grows costly. This is analysis, not a treaty obligation: the Accords are non-binding, but engineering dependence is real.
4. Strategic autonomyIndia prefers a multipolar order. Lunar exploration is splitting into two camps: the US-led Artemis Accords (78) and the China-Russia ILRS (SpaceNexus). Joining one camp closely may limit options with others, such as BRICS space cooperation (Lowy).
5. Safety zones as de facto claimsCritics argue that safety zones could become de facto territorial or resource claims, against the non-appropriation principle of OST Article II (Kruckel, 2025; ANZSIL). See c136.

[Correction] "India does not have a clear blueprint of its lunar programme": this needs qualifying. India has declared goals and a mission sequence:
(1) Missions: Chandrayaan-4 (sample return, approved 2024) and Chandrayaan-5/LUPEX (approved 2025).
(2) Stations and landing: BAS-1 by 2028 and the full BAS by 2035; an Indian landing on the Moon by 2040 under Space Vision 2047 (India Today; ISRO).
(3) Further goals: reports mention a moon base and a lunar-orbit station in the 2040s (Space.com).
What is missing is a published, costed architecture for the crewed landing: a heavy-lift launcher, a lander and a surface plan. Defensible phrasing: "India has goals but no detailed public architecture".

[Correction] "The Artemis Accords have a provision of buffer zones, a violation of the Outer Space Treaty and Moon Treaty":
(1) Term: the term is "safety zones" (Section 11). They are areas where signatories notify and coordinate their activities to avoid harmful interference (State Department).

(2) The criticism: that they could be used as de facto claims is a criticism, not an established violation. Scholars are divided:

  • some say the OST and Moon Agreement can be read as allowing such zones;
  • others say there is no independent body to set a zone's size or duration (ANZSIL; Kruckel).

(3) Moon Agreement: it binds only its 17 parties. The US and India are not parties, so "violation of the Moon Treaty" cannot apply to them.

[Additional] "Lunar exploration is becoming bipolar": a nuance.
The blocs are not closed: three countries (Senegal, Serbia, Thailand) appear on both the Artemis Accords and ILRS official lists (SpaceNexus).
India itself is keeping several tracks open. In September 2026 the Prime Minister invited other countries to be "co-travellers" in India's own programme (Lowy). Bilateral missions such as LUPEX with Japan show that there is room between the blocs.

  • Use the Accords forum and the UN together. Press within the Accords for transparent, time-limited, notified safety zones. Also support COPUOS work on a multilateral regime for space resources, which India and the US have reaffirmed (BusinessLine).
  • Bridge the timeline gap by delivering Gaganyaan, Chandrayaan-4 and LUPEX on schedule. A partner with proven hardware negotiates better terms.
  • Diversify partners: Japan (LUPEX), France and Australia (Lowy), keeping India's own Moon-landing goal for 2040.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Chandrayaan-5 = LUPEX (Lunar Polar Exploration):
    • ISRO lander; JAXA rover (built by MHI); JAXA H3 launch from Tanegashima.
    • Target Japan's FY2028; sanctioned 10 Mar 2025.
    • About 100 days (up to 1 year) near a south-polar PSR.
    • Drills to ~1.5 m; measures water quantity, form and distribution.
    • Instruments from ISRO, JAXA, NASA (neutron spectrometer) and ESA (EMS-L).
  • Artemis Accords:
    • 13 Oct 2020; NASA + US State Department; 8 founders.
    • Non-binding; grounded in the OST 1967; cover the Moon, Mars, comets and asteroids.
    • 78 signatories (Vietnam, 28 Oct 2026). India was 27th, on 21 Jun 2023. Russia and China are not signatories.
  • Accords principles (10): peaceful purposes, transparency, interoperability, emergency assistance, registration, release of scientific data, heritage, space resources, deconfliction (safety zones), orbital debris.
  • Moon Agreement 1979:
    • "Common heritage of mankind"; in force 1984.
    • 17 parties. India signed (1982), not ratified.
    • Saudi Arabia left in Jan 2024, the first exit from a UN space treaty.
  • OST Article II: no national appropriation of outer space, the Moon or celestial bodies.
  • Artemis I: 16 Nov - 11 Dec 2022; uncrewed SLS + Orion; distant retrograde orbit.
  • Artemis II: 1-10 Apr 2026; crewed flyby. AVATAR flew bone-marrow organ chips made from the astronauts' own cells.
  • Artemis III: 2027; low Earth orbit; Orion docks with Blue Origin's Blue Moon and SpaceX's Starship test articles; no landing.
  • Artemis IV: 2028, the first Artemis landing. Artemis V: late 2028, the second landing.
  • Moon Base programme:
    • Announced 24 Mar 2026 ("Ignition"); Gateway paused.
    • Three phases. JAXA's pressurised rover is in Phase 2.
  • NASA's invitation to ISRO: made at the 9th India-US CSJWG, Bengaluru, 5-6 Aug 2026. It is to join the Moon Base programme; India is already in the Accords.
  • Gaganyaan G1: uncrewed, carrying Vyommitra on HLVM3. Target Q4 2026; reported (20 Nov 2026) as unlikely before Feb 2027.

Mains Points

  • Why lunar governance is unsettled:
    • The OST bars sovereignty but is silent on resource extraction.
    • The Moon Agreement fills the gap but lacks every major space power (17 parties).
    • The Accords spread rapidly as non-binding "soft law" outside the UN. They take a US reading of resource use (extraction is not appropriation) and add safety zones.
  • The case for joining the Moon Base programme:
    • cost-sharing and multilateral exploration;
    • early influence over standards, data and safety norms;
    • access to partners' strengths (Japanese sample return, Australian deep-space communication, Canadian robotics);
    • supply-chain openings for Indian start-ups (start-ups already earn about a third of their orders abroad);
    • a possible shortcut while Gaganyaan and the BAS proceed.
  • The case for caution:
    • India could become a junior partner in a US-designed architecture. The Gateway pause showed that partner roles can change unilaterally.
    • Engineering lock-in through interoperability standards.
    • Strategic autonomy in a two-camp contest (Artemis versus ILRS).
    • The unresolved legality of safety zones.
    • No automatic technology transfer: it must be negotiated, and export controls apply.
  • Science and self-reliance:
    • LUPEX shows a third path: a bilateral partnership with clear division of work, where India builds the lander and Japan the rover.
    • It builds indigenous capability (heavier lander, long-duration operations) while sharing cost.
  • Ethics and the commons:
    • "First come, first served" at the few ice-rich, well-lit polar sites risks inequity for late-comers.
    • Equitable benefit-sharing, protection of pristine ice and heritage, and debris mitigation should be built into any regime.
  • Way forward:
    • Issue-based alignment: join selectively (payloads, data, instruments) and keep the sovereign core (launchers, lander, human-rating, BAS) indigenous.
    • Negotiate IP and technology terms in implementing arrangements.
    • Press for transparent, time-limited safety zones inside the Accords, and for a multilateral resource regime at UN COPUOS.
    • Deliver Gaganyaan, Chandrayaan-4 and LUPEX on time to strengthen India's bargaining position.
    • Diversify partners (Japan, France, Australia).

Common Traps / Confusions

  • LUPEX splits water? No. It measures water. Electrolysis is the future use the data support.
  • LUPEX launch vehicle: it is Japan's H3, not an ISRO rocket. India builds the lander, not the rover.
  • Artemis Accords vs Artemis programme:
    • The Accords are a non-binding set of principles (78 signatories).
    • The programme is NASA's set of missions.
    • Signing the Accords does not put a country on a mission.
  • Accords vs ISS: the ISS works under a binding intergovernmental agreement; the Accords are non-binding. The Accords are not "modelled on the ISS".
  • India and the Moon Agreement: India signed but did not ratify, so India is not a party.
  • India and the Accords: India has signed (2023). What is pending is the Moon Base invitation (2026).
  • "Buffer zones": the term is safety zones (Section 11). They are about notification and coordination, and are not legally territorial claims.
  • Artemis III lands on the Moon? No, not since the February 2026 restructuring: it is an Earth-orbit docking test. The first landing is Artemis IV (2028).
  • Starship belongs to Blue Origin? No. Starship is SpaceX's; Blue Origin's lander is Blue Moon.
  • SLS vs Orion: SLS is the rocket; Orion is the crew capsule; the landers are commercial.
  • Gateway: it was paused (Mar 2026), not completed. The Moon Base is on the surface.
  • AVATAR: an organ-chip experiment (bone marrow), not a robot or digital twin. Its results are still being analysed.
  • Prelims 2025 trap: SpaDeX is a docking technology demonstrator. The microgravity experiments on the same PSLV flight rode on POEM-4.

Suggested answer approach [Additional] (study guidance, not an official scheme):
