49. Communication Satellites (completes Section 47)

49.1 What they do and where they are placed

  • Communication satellites relay signals between distant points on Earth: TV broadcasting, DTH (Direct-to-Home), internet, telephony, VSAT networks (banks, ATMs, remote offices), mobile-tower backhaul, and connectivity for ships and aircraft.
  • Class: they are placed in polar and geostationary (GEO) orbits. Checked: communication satellites use three orbit classes - LEO, MEO and GEO - and most LEO broadband satellites fly in inclined, not polar, orbits (c24).

[Correction] "Communication satellites are placed into polar and GEO": write "LEO, MEO and GEO". Examples: Starlink's shells are at about 43, 53, 70 and 97 degrees inclination - only the 97-degree shell is near-polar (Upside Curve, Nov 2026; KeepTrack); Eutelsat OneWeb is near-polar (about 87-88 degrees) (eoPortal; Orbital Radar); SES's O3b mPOWER (used by Jio-SES) is in MEO at about 8,000 km (Wikipedia); TV/DTH satellites (ISRO's GSAT series, for example) are in GEO.

50.4 Significance of satellite-based internet

  • Less need for last-mile fibre: fibre is needed only up to the gateways, not to every end user (c27).
  • Instant connectivity in remote areas: crucial for security operations, disaster management, and search and rescue when ground networks are absent or destroyed.
  • Digital economy: promotes digitisation of services (e-governance, payments, e-commerce).
  • Inclusive growth: telemedicine, tele-education, agriculture advisories.
  • Extending 5G (and future 6G) to remote areas: satellites can provide backhaul for mobile towers [Additional: Jio has said it will use O3b capacity for mobile backhaul in remote areas (ET)]. [Additional] 5G standards now include non-terrestrial networks, and "direct-to-cell" satellites can reach ordinary phones (Starlink V2 Mini carries a direct-to-cell payload: KeepTrack).
  • Beyond homes: international aviation and shipping connectivity is a very large market - so the stakes and the competition are high.

[Correction] "It has eliminated to a great extent the need of fibre optics": it reduces the need for last-mile fibre in remote areas; it does not replace fibre or mobile networks at scale. Capacity per area is limited: TRAI estimated that a single constellation could serve only about 10,000-20,000 connections in an area like Delhi against a need of about 50 lakh (BusinessLine, Sep 2026). Reported Starlink prices (about Rs 3,000-4,200 a month plus a kit of about Rs 33,000) are several times entry fibre plans [reported, not official] (Communications Today). The service has not started in India (Section 55).

50.5 Concerns

  1. Geopolitical tool: Starlink has been used in conflict zones, including Ukraine; in June 2026 Indian security agencies reportedly withheld Starlink's final clearances after reports that its terminals were in use in Iran (where it is not licensed), raising doubts about India's ability to control a US-based operator during a crisis (Bloomberg via Straits Times; Business Standard).
  2. Cyber and internal security: lawful interception, the ability to restrict service in specific areas or to specific users in emergencies, and the risk that laser inter-satellite links could let Indian traffic bypass domestic gateways are licence issues under review; grey-market terminals have also been flagged (Business Standard; Satellite Internet Guide, secondary).
  3. Impact on the telecom sector: class figure - 83% of subscribers are with Airtel and Jio, who might lose them to Starlink or Kuiper. Checked: Jio and Airtel hold about 77% of wireless subscribers and about 84% of broadband subscribers (c28). Note that both are also partners in satellite broadband (Jio-SES; Bharti in Eutelsat OneWeb; both signed Starlink distribution deals), and satellite capacity and price limit mass substitution.
  4. Data privacy and data localisation: licence conditions require data localisation and lawful interception; Starlink says it meets local data-storage requirements (Business Standard; Straits Times).
  5. [Additional] Space sustainability: thousands of LEO satellites raise collision and debris risk and affect astronomy; Starlink is lowering its main shell (about 550 to 480 km) partly so failed satellites re-enter faster (Orbital Radar, secondary).
  6. [Additional] Affordability and capacity (see c27), and dependence on foreign operators for critical connectivity.

[Correction] "83% of subscribers are with Airtel and Jio": depends on the base. Wireless (mobile) subscribers, Aug 2026: Jio 39.31% + Airtel 38.01% = 77.32% (TRAI monthly data as reported by Bharatnet, 30 Sep 2026). 51. The Satcom Spectrum Controversy

51.1 What is telecom spectrum?

  • Telecom spectrum is the range of radio frequencies (part of the electromagnetic spectrum) used to carry signals. The Central Government, "being the owner of the spectrum on behalf of the people", assigns the right to use specific frequency blocks to operators so that they can run their networks (Telecommunications Act 2023, Section 4(1)) (Act, s. 4) (c29).
  • Frequencies are grouped into bands. Class list: below 1 GHz, L-band, S-band, Ka-band. The checked ranges are in the table (c30).
  • For mobile services India is divided into 22 telecom circles (licensed service areas); spectrum is assigned circle by circle. [Not re-checked in this session against a DoT document.]

[Correction] "Spectrum is the allocation of electromagnetic waves by the central govt": spectrum is the resource (a range of frequencies); what the government does is allocate bands to services (through the National Frequency Allocation Plan, in line with ITU Radio Regulations) and assign frequencies to particular users. The 2023 Act uses the word "assignment" (s. 4).

51.3 Why the controversy, and where it stands

  • The dispute: mobile operators argued that satellite broadband competes with their auctioned spectrum and should also be auctioned; satellite operators argued for administrative assignment because the spectrum is shared. The 2023 Act settled the method (administrative); the fight moved to the price.
  • TRAI (May 2025): recommended a spectrum charge of 4% of AGR for both GSO and NGSO operators, a minimum of Rs 3,500 per MHz a year, an extra Rs 500 per urban subscriber a year, and a subsidy for user terminals in rural areas (BusinessLine; ETLegalWorld/PTI).
  • DoT (Digital Communications Commission, 3 Sep 2026): approved most TRAI recommendations but a 5% of AGR spectrum usage charge, reduced to 4% in notified rural/remote, hilly, island and border areas; administrative assignment for 5 years, extendable by 2 (Voice&Data, 9 Sep 2026). Operators also pay an 8% licence fee, so the total regulatory outgo would be about 12-13% of AGR (Communications Today, 12 Oct 2026).
  • Status (4 Nov 2026): a Cabinet note was being prepared in October 2026; no Cabinet approval was found in the sources read [Verification Required if a decision has since been announced]. All three licensed operators hold only provisional spectrum and await MHA security clearance (Communications Today).

52. Communication Satellites in GEO

  • Characteristics (as in Section 46.1 of the 3 Nov note): altitude about 36,000 km (exactly 35,786 km); period about 24 hours (23 h 56 min, one sidereal day) (c33); always above the equator; appears fixed over one place, so a dish can be pointed once and left.
  • Three satellites spaced around the equator can cover the whole planet except the polar regions (see Figure 45 of the 3 Nov note).
  • Uses: DTH television (Ku-band), TV distribution, VSAT and satellite internet (e.g. ISRO/NSIL's GSAT series).
  • [Additional] Trade-off: wide, fixed coverage and cheap receive-only dishes, but about 0.48 s round-trip delay (Figure 48) and higher rain fade at Ku/Ka frequencies.

[Correction] "36K km, time period almost 24 hrs": acceptable round figures; for the exam use 35,786 km and 23 h 56 min (the PIB NVS-03 backgrounder also gives about 35,786 km for the GEO/GTO apogee) (PIB, 1 Nov 2026).

53. Satellite-Based Navigation

53.1 Principle: ranging and trilateration

  • A navigation system is a constellation of satellites carrying atomic clocks. Each satellite continuously broadcasts its position and the exact time of transmission.
  • Class: the satellites are placed in GEO and GSO. Checked: the global systems (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou's core) use MEO at about 19,100-23,200 km; the regional systems use GEO + inclined geosynchronous (IGSO) orbits - NavIC: 3 GEO + 4 IGSO (c34).
  • The receiver measures the signal's travel time and converts it to a distance (range): d = c × (treceive - ttransmit), where c = 299,792,458 m/s. One range puts the receiver on a sphere around that satellite; the receiver is where the spheres intersect - trilateration (distances), not triangulation (angles).
  • Class: 3 satellites give a 2D fix and the 4th adds altitude for 3D. Checked: a phone's clock is not an atomic clock, so the receiver's clock error is a fourth unknown. With 4 satellites the receiver solves x, y, z and clock error - a 3D position plus precise time. With 3 satellites only a 2D fix is possible, and only if the altitude is assumed (c35).
  • Why clocks matter: a timing error of 1 microsecond = c × 10-6 s ≈ 300 m of range error.

[Correction] "A constellation of sats placed in GEO and GSO": true only for regional systems. NavIC: 3 satellites in GEO (32.5, 83 and 129.5 degrees E) and 4 in inclined geosynchronous orbit (29 degrees inclination, crossing the equator at 55 and 111.75 degrees E) (ISRO). GPS: MEO, about 20,200 km (GPS.gov); GLONASS: about 19,100 km (eoPortal); Galileo: 23,222 km (ESA); BeiDou-3: 24 MEO + 3 GEO + 3 IGSO (Wikipedia). QZSS: quasi-zenith (inclined, elliptical geosynchronous) orbits plus GEO (The Register).

[Correction] "3 satellites give 2D; the 4th satellite adds altitude": the 4th satellite is needed mainly to solve the receiver clock error. Four ranges give four equations for four unknowns (x, y, z, clock bias) and hence 3D position and time. A 2D fix from 3 satellites works only when altitude is assumed (for example, a ship at sea level).

53.2 Signals

  • Signals are broadcast continuously in real time. Class: in the L and S bands, using CDMA. Checked: all systems use the L-band; the S-band signal is a NavIC feature (c36).
  • NavIC: L5 at 1176.45 MHz and S-band at 2492.028 MHz (NovAtel; ISRO's page prints "2498.028 MHz", which appears to be a typographical slip [Verification Required]) (ISRO). The new NVS series adds an L1 signal (common in phones) and carries an indigenous atomic clock (ISRO, GSLV-F18).
  • [Additional] Shared centre frequencies help multi-system receivers: 1176.45 MHz is used by GPS L5, Galileo E5a, BeiDou B2a and NavIC L5 (Sonia Satellite, secondary).

[Correction] "Signals are transmitted in the L and S band using CDMA": correct for NavIC (L5 + S; the RS and SPS share both). GPS, Galileo, BeiDou and GLONASS broadcast in the L-band only (Galileo 1,200-1,600 MHz: ESA; GLONASS L1-L3 about 1.2-1.6 GHz: eoPortal). CDMA is used by GPS, Galileo, BeiDou and NavIC; legacy GLONASS signals use FDMA (newer GLONASS-K satellites add CDMA signals) [Additional; general reference, not re-read in this session].

  • Communication satellites use LEO (about 480-1,200 km), MEO (O3b mPOWER, about 8,000 km) and GEO (35,786 km). Minimum one-way delay: LEO 3-8 ms, MEO about 53 ms, GEO about 239 ms.
  • Starlink (SpaceX): about 10,900 active (Nov 2026), shells at 43/53/70/97 degrees, laser links. Eutelsat OneWeb: 648 satellites, 1,200 km, near-polar, bent-pipe, Ku user / Ka gateway. Amazon Leo = former Project Kuiper (renamed Nov 2025), 3,232 authorised, 590-630 km. Jio-SES = SES's MEO O3b mPOWER.
  • Satellite internet chain: user device - Wi-Fi router - user terminal - satellite - gateway - optical fibre - PoP/internet exchange.
  • Telecommunications Act 2023, s. 4(4): spectrum by auction by default; administrative for First Schedule entries (entry 16: satellite services incl. DTH, VSAT, GMPCS, MSS in L and S bands).
  • 2G judgment, 2 Feb 2012: 122 licences quashed; auction directed. Presidential Reference 2012: auction not a constitutional mandate for every resource.
  • Satcom spectrum charge: TRAI 4% of AGR (May 2025); DCC 5% (4% in notified remote areas), 5 + 2 years (Sep 2026); Cabinet decision pending (as of 4 Nov 2026).
  • IEEE bands: L 1-2 GHz, S 2-4, C 4-8, X 8-12, Ku 12-18, K 18-27, Ka 27-40 GHz. DTH = Ku-band.
  • Navigation: ranging by signal travel time; 4 satellites for 3D position + time (the 4th removes receiver clock error). 1 microsecond = about 300 m.
  • Orbits: GPS MEO 20,200 km (24 baseline, about 31 flown); GLONASS 19,100 km (24); Galileo 23,222 km (30 = 24 + 6); BeiDou-3 30 (24 MEO + 3 GEO + 3 IGSO); NavIC 7 (3 GEO + 4 IGSO, 29 degrees); QZSS 7 planned (quasi-zenith + GEO).
  • NavIC: L5 1176.45 MHz + S 2492.028 MHz; NVS adds L1 and an indigenous atomic clock; SPS (civil, better than 20 m design) and RS (encrypted); only 3 working in 2026; NVS-03 launched 30 Oct 2026 on GSLV-F18.

Mains Points

  • Connectivity and inclusion: satellite broadband can reach the last villages, islands, border posts, ships and aircraft faster than fibre; it supports disaster resilience, telemedicine and tele-education and 5G backhaul. But per-area capacity (TRAI: 10,000-20,000 connections in a Delhi-sized area vs 50 lakh needed) and terminal cost mean it complements, not replaces, fibre and mobile.
  • Sovereignty and security: foreign-controlled constellations raise questions of lawful interception, emergency control, data localisation and gateway bypass by laser links; Starlink's reported use in Ukraine and Iran shows connectivity can become a geopolitical lever. India's response: GMPCS licence conditions, Indian gateways, MHA security clearance.
  • Spectrum policy: administrative assignment (shared spectrum, level playing field, faster roll-out) vs auction (transparency, revenue, telecom operators' parity argument). The 2023 Act chose administrative assignment; the remaining question is fair pricing (TRAI 4% vs DoT 5% of AGR) and periodic review as satellite and mobile markets converge (direct-to-cell).
  • Competition: Jio and Airtel dominate mobile and broadband; they are also satcom partners and owners, so regulators must watch for both foreign dominance and domestic incumbency.
  • Self-reliance in PNT: NavIC's fall to 3 working satellites (2026) - after clock failures, the PSLV-C39 (2017) and NVS-02 (2025) setbacks - shows the need for indigenous clocks, faster replacement launches and spare satellites; NVS-03 (Oct 2026) with an indigenous clock and L1 signal is the recovery path. Multi-GNSS receivers with NavIC support reduce single-system dependence (the 1999 Kargil GPS-denial lesson).
  • Way forward: a time-bound, transparent satcom authorisation and pricing framework; security-by-design conditions applied equally to all operators; terminal subsidies for unserved areas through USOF-type support (TRAI's suggestion); domestic constellations and manufacturing under IN-SPACe; space-sustainability norms for mega-constellations; and an accelerated NVS launch schedule with in-orbit spares.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Broadband constellations are LEO - mostly inclined, not polar. Jio-SES is MEO, not LEO. Kuiper = Amazon Leo.
  • A gateway is a ground station; coverage comes from satellite beams.
  • Satellite internet reduces last-mile fibre needs; gateways still need fibre.
  • Allocation (band to a service) vs assignment (frequencies to an operator). The 2023 Act says "assign".
  • Ka-band is 27-40 GHz (IEEE), not "24 kHz"; 26 GHz is the 5G mmWave band.
  • Administrative satellite spectrum is a national choice (India: First Schedule of the 2023 Act), not a global treaty rule.
  • 4 satellites for a 3D fix because of the receiver clock error, not only for altitude. Trilateration uses distances; triangulation uses angles.
  • S-band navigation signal = NavIC; GPS/Galileo/GLONASS/BeiDou are L-band. GLONASS (legacy) = FDMA.
  • India's encrypted service = RS (Restricted Service); GPS's = PPS. Both SPS and RS give 3D fixes.
  • Design numbers vs working numbers: NavIC 7 designed, 3 working (2026); BeiDou-3 30 (35 was BeiDou-2); Galileo 30 includes 6 spares.
  • QZSS and NavIC are regional; GAGAN is augmentation, not a constellation.
  • Geostationary period is 23 h 56 min (sidereal day), altitude 35,786 km.

30-Second Revision

Communication satellites: TV, DTH, internet, VSAT, backhaul; in LEO (low delay, thousands of satellites), MEO (O3b mPOWER, 8,000 km) and GEO (35,786 km, fixed, 3 cover all but poles, DTH in Ku-band, about 0.48 s round trip). Satellite internet: device - router - terminal - satellite (laser links) - gateway - fibre - exchange; Starlink (about 10,900 active), OneWeb (648, 1,200 km, near-polar), Amazon Leo (ex-Kuiper), Jio-SES (MEO). Benefits: remote, disaster, security, digital economy, telemedicine, 5G backhaul, aviation and shipping. Concerns: geopolitical lever (Ukraine, Iran), lawful interception and gateway bypass, data localisation, Jio + Airtel (77% wireless, about 84% broadband), debris, cost and capacity. Spectrum: 2023 Act s. 4 - auction default, satellite services administrative (First Schedule); TRAI 4% vs DCC 5% of AGR; Cabinet decision pending; 2G judgment 2012. Navigation: ranging, 4 satellites (x, y, z, clock); SPS (open) vs PPS/RS (encrypted); GPS 24 (31 flown), GLONASS 24, Galileo 30, BeiDou-3 30, NavIC 7 (3 working; NVS-03 launched 30 Oct 2026), QZSS 7 planned (5 + QZS-7 testing).
