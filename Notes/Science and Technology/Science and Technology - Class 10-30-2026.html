23. Laser Inertial Confinement Fusion: the Class Account Checked

Recap: the principle, the NIF indirect-drive steps and the ignition results were set out in the 29 Oct note, Section 21 (Figures 17 and 18). This section records the class's version, corrects it, and adds the LIFE power-plant idea.

23.1 What the class described

  • At the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) the world's most powerful laser, with 192 lasers, forms the National Ignition Facility (NIF).
  • All the lasers were directed on a capsule with an outer layer of lead, then a layer of plastic or diamond or beryllium, and an innermost frozen pellet of deuterium and tritium. The lasers hit the beryllium layer, causing ablation, which creates inward pressure compressing the frozen hydrogen isotopes to about 1000 times the liquid density at a temperature of about 1000 C, so that fusion gives helium and neutrons.
  • The helium "changed into alpha particles" to heat the remaining hydrogen isotopes and create more plasma; the neutrons struck a lead shell to heat it, and that heat was used to make electricity; tritium is also produced and can be used as fuel.
  • Electricity by this approach is called LIFE (Laser Inertial Fusion Energy).
  • In one experiment the input-output ratio was 1 : 1.59; the whole experiment lasts a few picoseconds and can be repeated several times a day.

[Additional] Size of the remaining gap: the same secondary article says a laser-driven power plant would need a target gain of about 25 to 50 to recover generation losses and the large driver power, against the best NIF target gain of 4.13 (8.6 MJ from 2.08 MJ, 7 Apr 2025; LLNL). Also, NIF's first priority is stockpile stewardship (29 Oct note). Ignition is a science result; a power plant also needs efficient lasers, cheap mass-produced targets, a repetition rate of several shots a second and a durable chamber.

24.2 Challenges to commercial fusion power (class list, checked)

  1. Tritium supply. Tritium is scarce and expensive (class; no price was verified, [Verification Required]) and radioactive, so a stockpile decays. Its half-life is about 12.3 years (class: about 13 years; standard nuclear data, [Verification Required]). ITER will test breeding blankets because supply would not cover future plants (ITER).
  2. Alternative fuel: helium-3. The class suggests helium-3 (He-3), reported to be abundant on the Moon, and links this to space-agency programmes to bring back lunar soil, giving Chandrayaan-4 as an example. See correction.
  3. Industrial base. A tokamak needs about one million components (ITER), and very few countries have the industrial base to build at that scale. The class asks how fusion power plants would then be installed across the globe; this is an open question, and ITER's own in-kind delivery model (about nine-tenths of project value delivered as components, buildings or systems) is how the Members shared the work (29 Oct note, Section 20.4).
  4. Sustained plasma. To produce power the plasma must be held at about 150 million C for up to 300 s (class), which is yet to be demonstrated. See correction.

[Correction] (i) Chandrayaan-4 is ISRO's lunar sample-return mission: Cabinet approval on 18 Sep 2024 at Rs 2,104.06 crore, two LVM3 launches with in-orbit docking, to bring 2-3 kg of south-polar material to Earth (secondary report); the ISRO page describes it as "planned for launch in the 2027-2028 timeframe" (ISRO National Science Meet page). It has not launched as of 30 Oct 2026. Its stated purposes are sample-return technology, support to future crewed missions and scientific study of lunar samples (PIB, Sep 2024, search summary); helium-3 supply is not stated as an objective in the sources opened. (ii) That the Moon holds He-3 usable as fusion fuel is the class's claim and was not verified; D-He3 fusion is also much harder than D-T because it needs far higher temperatures (standard physics, [Verification Required]). (iii) "All major space agencies have a programme" for this purpose was not verified.

[Correction] The class combines two ideas. For KSTAR, the stated goal after its 2023 tungsten divertor was 100 million C for 300 s by 2026, against a reported 48 s at 100 million C (ITER; 29 Oct note). For ITER the design aim is Q = 10, 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of heating (ITER); the 300 s figure for ITER's pulses was not confirmed in the sources opened [Verification Required]. What has not been shown by any device is net fusion power gain (Q > 1) in a tokamak: JET's best was Q = 0.67, and long-pulse results at lower temperature (EAST, 1,066 s, secondary) do not change this.

25. The SHANTI Act, 2025 (revision with the class's details)

Recap: the 27 Oct note (Sections 7-10) compared the CLND Act, 2010 with the SHANTI Act and could not verify the liability amounts. The PRS Legislative Research summary of the Bill now confirms the main numbers and the new bodies; the full Act text was still not opened.

25.1 Name, background and why the Act was needed

  • Full name: the Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Act, 2025 (Act No. 39 of 2025). The class spells it "SHAANTI" and abbreviates the name; the Act's own spelling is SHANTI.
  • Timeline: introduced in Lok Sabha 15 Dec 2025; passed Lok Sabha 17 Dec; passed Rajya Sabha 18 Dec; assent 20 Dec 2025 (PRS; Wikipedia). A law-firm note says it came into effect on 21 Dec 2025 (Norton Rose Fulbright, search summary). The Department of Atomic Energy released the draft SHANTI Rules and Regulations for public consultation from 14 Aug to 4 Sep 2026 (Wikipedia, secondary; DAE page not opened).
  • Target (class): the Nuclear Energy Mission, announced in the Union Budget 2025-26 (the class writes "20-26 budget"), aims at 100 GW of nuclear capacity by 2047. Present capacity is 8.8 GW (8.78 GW, NPCIL; PIB, 23 Jul 2026; 27 Oct note).
  • Problem (class): nuclear power was a monopoly of central public-sector entities (NPCIL, ASHVINI, BHAVINI); even in the best case they could reach only part of the target, so private operators were to be allowed.
  • Repeals: the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 (under which licences for the main activities could go only to a central-government entity or government companies) and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010 (PRS).

[Correction] The class says "in the best case scenario they can achieve 46% of the target". The PIB roadmap (23 Jul 2026, 27 Oct note) says NPCIL would add capacity to about 54 GW by 2047, i.e. about 54% of 100 GW, and the balance of about 46 GW must come from other agencies (other public-sector enterprises, State governments, the private sector and joint ventures). Arithmetic: 22 + 32 = 54; 100 - 54 = 46. So 46% is the gap, not what the PSUs can reach.

25.2 Private participation and reserved activities

  • Who may be licensed (PRS): for specified activities the Government may now also grant licences to (i) any other company except a company incorporated outside India, (ii) joint ventures between government entities and private companies, and (iii) any other person expressly permitted by the Government. These activities include building, owning or operating a nuclear power plant or reactor and fabrication, transport, trade or storage of nuclear fuel. Any activity involving radiation exposure needs a safety authorisation from the AERB.
  • Foreign and private equity (class): private equity capped at 49%, the FDI limit also 49%, and the limit for both together also 49%, the rest with central-government PSUs; private operators to be notified separately.
  • Exclusive to the Central Government (class): (a) uranium enrichment, (b) reprocessing of spent fuel, (c) production and upgradation of heavy water, (d) disposal of waste, (e) separation of isotopes; in exceptional circumstances a private operator might be allowed to enrich uranium.

25.3 Liability: from a flat cap to a graded cap

  • CLND Act, 2010 (class and PRS): a flat Rs 1,500 crore on the operator, which the class says deterred private participation.
  • SHANTI Act (class and PRS): a graded liability in proportion to the capacity of the reactor, Rs 100 crore to Rs 3,000 crore. The no-fault principle, the operator's insurance requirement and the Claims Commissioner or Commission are retained (PRS). The Bill also extends cover to nuclear damage in a foreign state from incidents in India, subject to conditions (PRS) [Additional].

25.4 Right of recourse against suppliers

  • Under the 2010 Act (PRS and class): the operator has a right of recourse (to recover some or all compensation) when (i) the contract provides for it, (ii) the incident arises from supply of defective equipment or materials (the class: a latent or patent defect), or (iii) it is caused by a deliberate act intended to cause damage (the class: an act of omission or commission by the supplier's employee). The class gives the sum as Rs 1,500 crore.
  • Why suppliers objected (class): US, French and Japanese suppliers saw open-ended liability, and the clause conflicted with the international nuclear liability regime, including the Convention on Supplementary Compensation (CSC) that India joined in 2016. The CSC ratification year was not re-verified [Verification Required]; a commentary site also says India ratified in 2016 (Vajiram and Ravi, search summary).
  • SHANTI Act (PRS): the Bill removes the right of recourse on the ground of supply of defective equipment or materials.

[Correction] The class says the SHANTI Act "removed the right of recourse". Per PRS, only one ground (supply of defective equipment or materials) is removed; the contract-based and deliberate-act grounds are not reported as removed. This reading rests on the PRS summary of the Bill; the Act text was not opened [Verification Required]. This refines the 27 Oct note, which left the recourse treatment open.

25.7 Significance (the class left this heading empty) [Additional]

  • Capacity: opens nuclear power to Indian companies and joint ventures to help close the roughly 46 GW gap in the 100 GW target.
  • International alignment and supplier confidence: graded liability, removal of the defective-equipment recourse ground and alignment with the CSC address the obstacle that, per PIB, kept foreign vendors away (27 Oct note; Jaitapur and Kovvada remain only proposed, 28 Oct note).
  • Regulation: statutory status for the AERB and a dedicated appeal route (AERAC, then the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity).
  • Concerns: commentators argue that the Rs 3,000 crore ceiling is small against potential damage and that reduced supplier accountability weakens the safety incentive (for example a Takshashila paper titled "Shanti Act advances reform, streamlines and dilutes liability", cited on Wikipedia; The Hindu explainer). The Supreme Court was reported in May 2026 to be examining the Rs 3,000 crore cap and whether courts are precluded from fixing suitable compensation (Rediff, 19 May 2026, search summary; outcome [Verification Required]).
  • Still to come: final Rules and Regulations (draft consultation closed 4 Sep 2026) will fix licensing, safety authorisation and many details.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • NIF: LLNL; up to 192 beams; indirect drive: laser → hohlraum (gold, high-Z) → X-rays → ablation of capsule; ablators: plastic, diamond (HDC), beryllium; capsule has D-T ice. Ignition 5 Dec 2022 (3.15 MJ from 2.05 MJ, gain about 1.5); record 8.6 MJ (7 Apr 2025, gain 4.13).
  • Alpha particle = helium-4 nucleus; D-T fusion gives alpha (3.5 MeV) and neutron (14.1 MeV).
  • LIFE: Laser Inertial Fusion Energy, LLNL 2008-2013, cancelled 2013; a concept, not a plant; blanket of liquid lithium or FLiBe breeds tritium. NIF fires less than once a day; a plant needs several shots a second.
  • Fusion vs fission: no chain reaction, no long-lived high-level spent fuel, deuterium from water, tritium bred from lithium; but neutron activation waste, tritium scarcity (half-life about 12.3 years), no net electricity yet. Proliferation risk is lower, not nil.
  • Helium-3: a possible alternative fuel (class); needs much higher temperatures than D-T; its lunar supply is unverified.
  • Chandrayaan-4: ISRO lunar sample-return mission; Cabinet approval 18 Sep 2024 (Rs 2,104.06 crore, secondary); two LVM3 launches; 2-3 kg from the south-polar region; ISRO page: planned 2027-2028; not launched as of 30 Oct 2026.
  • SHANTI Act, 2025 (Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India): Act 39 of 2025; LS 17 Dec, RS 18 Dec, assent 20 Dec 2025; repeals the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the CLND Act, 2010.
  • Licences: Government plus Indian companies and joint ventures for building and operating plants and for nuclear fuel fabrication, transport, trade and storage; companies incorporated outside India excluded; enrichment and isotopic separation reserved to the Centre "unless otherwise notified"; AERB safety authorisation for radiation activities.
  • Liability: CLND flat Rs 1,500 crore (reactors of 10 MWth and above); SHANTI Rs 100-3,000 crore by capacity; no-fault and Claims Commissioner retained; recourse ground of defective equipment removed; the class's SDR 300 million first tier and CSC second tier are unverified.
  • Bodies: AERB statutory (chair, one whole-time and up to seven part-time members; three-year term extendable by up to three years); AERAC (chair: AEC chairperson) hears appeals; appeal to the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity.
  • Nuclear Energy Mission: Budget 2025-26; 100 GW by 2047; NPCIL path about 54 GW and the balance about 46 GW from others; capacity 8.78 GW.
  • Space block: headings only (ELV, RLV; EOS, CMS, Nav; lunar and Mars exploration, Gaganyaan, BAS, GW and LIGO, telescope; private sector; space pollution).

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Laser fusion: a real science milestone, a long road to power. NIF's ignition and the 4.13 target gain show that fusion can release more energy than reaches the target; but a plant needs a gain of the order of 25 to 50, lasers that are efficient, several shots a second and cheap targets. Keep the scientific claim (ignition) apart from the engineering claim (electricity).
  • Fusion in the energy mix: its merits are fuel abundance, no chain reaction and carbon-free operation; its limits are tritium supply, neutron-damaged materials, scale of the industrial base and long timelines (ITER D-T in 2039). India's sensible position is to stay engaged (ITER membership, ADITYA-U, SST-1) while relying on fission, renewables and SMRs for the 2047 and 2070 goals.
  • Space resources hype versus evidence: helium-3 on the Moon is frequently cited but unproven as a practical fusion fuel; Chandrayaan-4's declared objectives are sample-return technology and science. Policy should not be justified by unverified resource claims.
  • SHANTI Act: capacity, capital and consent. Opening the sector to Indian private companies and joint ventures can mobilise capital and delivery capacity for the 46 GW gap; graded liability and the removal of one recourse ground make supplier participation easier. The trade-off is lower supplier accountability and a liability ceiling that critics find low, so victims' remedies, insurance and a strong, independent regulator are the safeguards that matter.
  • Federal and institutional design: a statutory AERB with government-appointed members, an advisory appeal council chaired by the Atomic Energy Commission's chairperson, and appeals to a power-sector tribunal raise questions of regulatory independence and expertise. The final Rules should address them.
  • Strategic control: keeping enrichment and isotopic separation with the Centre protects the strategic and safeguards interests (27 and 29 Oct notes on safeguards and the NSG); how exceptions are notified will matter.
  • Way forward: (i) finalise the SHANTI Rules with clear licensing, equity, insurance and waste provisions; (ii) publish the Act's liability tiers and the working of the CSC tier; (iii) keep the regulator transparent and well resourced; (iv) pursue fusion through ITER and domestic tokamaks with a technology-transfer plan; (v) state mission objectives and resource claims (such as helium-3) only with evidence.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • NIF's capsule has no lead layer; the hohlraum wall is gold (high-Z), the capsule has an ablator, D-T ice and D-T gas.
  • Indirect versus direct drive: NIF's beams hit the hohlraum wall, not the capsule.
  • Alpha particle = helium-4 nucleus; alphas heat the fuel, neutrons carry the heat out.
  • 1000 C is not a fusion temperature; fusion needs about 150 million C.
  • NIF gain is target gain, not wall-plug efficiency, and NIF fires less than once a day.
  • LIFE was a cancelled concept, not an operating plant.
  • Tritium is bred, not harvested from seawater; deuterium is the one in water.
  • Fusion is not waste-free or proliferation-proof; it is far lower on both counts than fission.
  • Chandrayaan-4 has not launched and is not stated to be a helium-3 mission.
  • SHANTI, not "SHAANTI"; it repeals both the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the CLND Act, 2010.
  • 46 GW is the gap, not the PSUs' share; NPCIL's path is about 54 GW.
  • Recourse: only the "defective equipment" ground is reported as removed, not recourse as a whole.
  • AERAC hears appeals; victims' claims go to the Claims Commissioner.
  • Do not state a 49% limit as law until the Act text or rules are checked.

30-Second Revision

Laser fusion: NIF (LLNL) fires up to 192 beams into a gold hohlraum; X-rays ablate a capsule (ablator: plastic, diamond or beryllium; D-T ice inside); implosion; alpha (helium-4) heating; ignition 5 Dec 2022 (3.15 MJ from 2.05 MJ), record 8.6 MJ (gain 4.13); LIFE (2008-2013) was the plant concept (lithium or FLiBe blanket, tritium breeding), cancelled; a plant needs gain 25-50 and several shots a second. Fusion evaluation: no long-lived spent fuel, deuterium plentiful, carbon-free; but tritium scarce (half-life about 12.3 years), activation waste, huge component base, 150 million C for long pulses unproven; helium-3 and Chandrayaan-4 (planned 2027-28) are unverified as a fuel route. SHANTI Act 2025: repeals the Atomic Energy Act 1962 and CLND Act 2010; Indian companies and joint ventures can build and operate; enrichment reserved to the Centre; liability Rs 100-3,000 crore by capacity (was flat Rs 1,500 crore); defective-equipment recourse removed; AERB statutory; AERAC hears appeals; 100 GW by 2047 with about 46 GW from non-NPCIL entities.

  1. 2016 | UPSC CSE Prelims | General Studies Paper 1. Question (as listed): India is an important member of the "International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor". If this experiment succeeds, what is the immediate advantage for India? (a) It can use thorium in place of uranium for power generation. (b) It can attain a global role in satellite navigation. (c) It can drastically improve the efficiency of its fission reactors in power generation. (d) It can build fusion reactors for power generation. Verify 2016 question and answer.

    Answer (secondary): (d). Link to Section 24: magnetic confinement at ITER is one route to fusion power; laser fusion (Section 23) is the other. Thorium (a) belongs to the fission three-stage programme. [Verification Required] against the official key.

  2. 2018 | UPSC CSE Mains | General Studies III. Paraphrase: Should India expand its nuclear energy programme given growing energy needs? Discuss facts and fears. Verify 2018 GS III question 16 (listed as 15 marks, 250 words; not re-opened today).

    Suggested answer approach [Additional]: Facts: 8.78 GW, the 100 GW by 2047 mission, SHANTI Act opening the sector to Indian companies, statutory AERB. Fears: liability ceiling and reduced supplier accountability, regulator independence, safety, waste, cost. Conclude with the final Rules, strong regulation and a diversified mix including fusion research.

Corrections Made

Substantive changes are marked where they occur. Typographical and spelling repairs were made silently and are not listed.

  • Laser fusion class account: no lead layer (gold hohlraum); indirect drive; 1000 C wrong (about 150 million C needed); alpha = helium-4 nucleus; lithium or FLiBe blanket not lead; gain 1.54 not 1.59; pulse in nanoseconds; NIF fires less than once a day.
  • Fusion waste: activated materials and tritium remain; only long-lived high-level spent fuel is avoided.
  • Proliferation: much lower, not "not possible"; LIFE hybrid design used fission blankets.
  • Helium-3 and Chandrayaan-4: sample-return mission planned 2027-28; He-3 not a stated objective; D-He3 needs far higher temperatures.
  • 300 s plasma: KSTAR goal versus ITER aim; no tokamak has shown Q > 1.
  • 46%: the gap to be filled by non-NPCIL entities, not the PSUs' share (NPCIL about 54 GW).
  • 49% cap: not confirmed; only foreign-incorporated companies are excluded from new licences (PRS).
  • Recourse: only the defective-equipment ground is reported as removed.
  • Tiers: SDR 300 million, fund and CSC tier unverified.
  • Bodies: AERB (not "ARV"); AERAC hears appeals; Claims Commissioner handles victims' claims.
