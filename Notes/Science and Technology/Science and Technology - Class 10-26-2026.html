1. Nuclear Energy (1a): Atomic Basics and Isotopes

1.1 Element, atom, nucleus

  • Element: a substance whose atoms all have the same atomic number (same number of protons).
  • Atom: basic unit of an element. The nucleus contains protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral); electrons (negative) surround it.
  • Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. Elements are arranged in the periodic table in increasing order of Z.
  • Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons (the nucleons). Neutron number N = A - Z.
  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element (same Z) with different mass numbers (different N).

[Correction] "Atoms of only one type" is made precise as "atoms with the same atomic number". Isotopes of one element are different nuclides yet remain the same element. [Additional] Isobars have the same A but different Z; isotones have the same N.

1.2 Isotopes of hydrogen

Hydrogen isotopes: what to remember
IsotopeCompositionKey points
Protium, H-11 p, 0 nMost abundant hydrogen. Combines with oxygen to form ordinary (light) water, H2O.
Deuterium, D or H-21 p, 1 nForms heavy water (deuterium oxide, D2O). [Additional] Used as moderator and coolant in pressurised heavy water reactors (PHWRs), which can run on natural uranium. Heavy water itself is not radioactive.
Tritium, T or H-31 p, 2 nRadioactive (beta emitter; half-life commonly cited as about 12.3 years). Fuel for nuclear fusion in the deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction. Not found in useful quantities in nature; produced in nuclear reactors and by neutron bombardment of lithium. [Additional]

[Correction] The rough notation "1H1, 1D2, 1T3" is rewritten in the standard form: mass number as superscript, atomic number as subscript (11H, 21H, 31H). In the rough notation the first number is the atomic number and the second the mass number, which is the reverse of the standard placement.

[Additional] Fission splits a heavy nucleus; fusion joins light nuclei (the D-T reaction yields helium-4 and a neutron). Tritium is therefore a fusion fuel, not a conventional fission-reactor fuel. The fusion link is relevant to the ITER question listed under PYQs. Source status: standard physics; the ITER question is verified only as listed by ForumIAS.

2. Uranium Isotopes, Fissile and Fertile Materials

2.1 Uranium isotopes

Uranium has several isotopes; three matter for nuclear technology: U-233, U-235, U-238.

Classification of key nuclides
NuclideCategoryNatural or artificialNote
U-235FissileNatural (about 0.7% of natural uranium)The only naturally occurring fissile isotope in significant amount.
U-233FissileArtificialMade from thorium-232 by neutron capture. [Correction] It is not one of the isotopes found in natural uranium.
Pu-239FissileArtificialMade from U-238 by neutron capture.
U-238FertileNatural (about 99.3%)Converts to Pu-239 when it captures a neutron.
Th-232FertileNaturalConverts to U-233. [Correction] Thorium-232 is itself not fissile.
  • Fissile: absorbs a neutron and splits (fission) into two lighter nuclei, releasing more neutrons and a large amount of energy as heat.
  • Fertile: does not sustain a chain reaction itself but, on absorbing a neutron, is converted into a fissile material.
  • The heat from a controlled chain reaction is used to generate electricity; an uncontrolled, rapid one is the basis of a weapon. Hence nuclear technology is dual-use: civil and military.

[Correction] (i) Fission releases neutrons as well as heat; the released neutrons sustain the chain reaction. (ii) The rough line "this element is fissile likewise Th-232" joins two different ideas; Pu-239 is fissile, while Th-232 is fertile, as the next line of the notes itself states. (iii) U-238 can undergo fission with very fast neutrons, but it cannot sustain a chain reaction alone, so it is classed as fertile.

[Additional] India link: Homi Bhabha's three-stage programme uses these conversions: Stage 1 PHWRs (natural uranium) produce plutonium; Stage 2 fast breeder reactors use plutonium and breed more fissile material; Stage 3 uses thorium and U-233, because India has large thorium resources and limited uranium. The current construction and commissioning status of the Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor was [Verification Required] and is not stated here.

3. Uranium Enrichment: LEU and HEU

Natural uranium is about 0.7% U-235 and 99.3% U-238 (plus a trace of U-234). Enrichment is an isotope-separation process that raises the percentage of U-235; the leftover stream with less U-235 is depleted uranium.

[Correction] The rough notes call enrichment "a qualitative process". More precisely, it changes the isotopic composition (percentage of U-235) of the uranium; it does not change its chemical identity. U-238 is not "removed" atom by atom as a separate material; the mixture is separated into an enriched stream and a depleted stream. [Additional] Industrial enrichment typically uses uranium hexafluoride (UF6) gas in cascades of gas centrifuges; older gaseous-diffusion and laser-based methods also exist. Enrichment is distinct from reprocessing, which recovers plutonium and uranium from spent fuel.

[Correction] The notes say the Jaitapur plant is "coming up" and "will use 5% enriched uranium". Jaitapur is a proposed project, not one under construction. It is planned by NPCIL near Madban village, Ratnagiri district, with six European Pressurised Reactors (EPR, Framatome/EDF technology; a pressurised-water design). The capacity was originally 6 x 1,650 MW (9,900 MW) and is now reported as 6 x 1,730 MW (10,380 MW). The environmental clearance reportedly expired in December 2022 and renewal was said in March 2025 to be under way. The specific "5%" fuel figure was not verified; LEU of this type is typically a few percent. [Verification Required] the exact fuel specification. Source: Jaitapur project summary (secondary source).

[Additional] India's PHWRs (Stage 1 of the programme) use natural uranium and heavy water; enriched LEU is used in light-water reactors such as those at Tarapur (BWR) and Kudankulam (VVER). These two plant names are standard knowledge, not re-verified in this session.

3.1 Case: Natanz and Fordow (Iran)

[Correction] The notes say Natanz and Fordow, "having thousands of centrifuges", "were bombed in July 25, 2026" to "destroy the capability" of Iran. The verified event is the US strike on three Iranian nuclear sites (Fordow, Natanz, Isfahan) on 22 June 2025 (local time; 21 June in the US), code-named Operation Midnight Hammer, during the June 2025 Israel-Iran war. The date 25 July 2026 could not be verified and appears to be a mix-up; the same source notes a separate 2026 Iran war that began in February 2026, and whether these sites were struck again on that date was not established. [Verification Required]. The damage outcome is contested: a leaked preliminary US assessment said the programme was set back by months; the Pentagon later said one to two years; the IAEA chief said the sites suffered severe but not total damage. Describing the aim as "destroying" the capability is a stated US objective, not an established result. Source: strike summary (secondary source).

[Additional] Fordow is built deep underground (reported at roughly 80-90 m); Natanz is a large enrichment complex. Before the June 2025 strikes the IAEA reported (31 May 2025) that Iran's stock of uranium enriched to 60% U-235 exceeded 400 kg. That is HEU, but below the conventional 90% weapon-grade level, which illustrates the 20-90% band in Figure 3. Attribute these figures to the IAEA as reported in the secondary source above.

4.2 Fuel enrichment claims

[Correction] The notes say naval fuel lies "beyond 20% but lower than 90%" and that the Indian boats "use 40% enriched" uranium. Naval reactor fuel varies by country: US and UK naval reactors are widely reported to use HEU at weapon-grade or near weapon-grade levels, and France is reported to use LEU. India has not officially disclosed the enrichment of its submarine fuel; figures such as 40% come from non-official reporting. [Verification Required]. The statement that submarine fuel is "above 20% and below 90%" is therefore a retained class figure, not a verified universal rule.

[Correction] The notes say that under AUKUS, Australia will receive US and UK submarines with 80% enriched fuel, and that they will "return to the respective countries" when the fuel is exhausted. The 80% value was not verified; US naval fuel is generally reported as higher. The "return" claim does not match what was reported: the AUKUS boats are described as having sealed reactors designed not to be refuelled during the vessel's life, and Australia has said it will not enrich or reprocess uranium and will manage naval spent fuel domestically. [Verification Required]. The official Australian and IAEA pages could not be opened in this session, so these points rest on standard reporting and should be checked before being used in an answer.

[Additional] Proliferation angle: a non-nuclear-weapon State that fuels a submarine with HEU removes that material from routine safeguards under the exemption for non-proscribed military use (Article 14 of the IAEA comprehensive safeguards agreement, standard knowledge). This is why naval HEU is debated in non-proliferation policy. Not re-verified in this session.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Z = protons; A = protons + neutrons; isotopes share Z, differ in A.
  • Hydrogen isotopes: protium (1p), deuterium (1p+1n), tritium (1p+2n, radioactive); D2O = heavy water (moderator, not radioactive).
  • Fissile: U-235 (natural), U-233 and Pu-239 (artificial). Fertile: U-238 → Pu-239; Th-232 → U-233.
  • Natural uranium: about 0.7% U-235, 99.3% U-238.
  • LEU: below 20% U-235; HEU: 20% or more; weapon-grade: about 90% or more.
  • Jaitapur (Ratnagiri, Maharashtra): proposed six EPR reactors by NPCIL; not under construction.
  • US strikes on Fordow, Natanz, Isfahan: 22 June 2025 (local time).
  • INS Aridhaman: third Arihant-class SSBN; commissioned 3 April 2026 (as reported).

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Dual-use dilemma: the same enrichment and reprocessing know-how serves energy and weapons. Safeguards, transparency and verification (IAEA) have to sit alongside the right to peaceful nuclear energy.
  • Enrichment is a proliferation chokepoint: the 20% line (LEU/HEU) is a policy marker, so tracking stocks and enrichment levels matters more than a single threshold. Force-based responses to enrichment sites produced contested outcomes (Section 3.1), which supports verification-led approaches.
  • Naval nuclear propulsion: HEU fuel for submarines raises safeguards gaps; AUKUS-type arrangements need transparent, verifiable fuel and waste arrangements.
  • India's fuel strategy: limited uranium but large thorium reserves explains the three-stage programme; a sea-based deterrent complements a no-first-use policy. Progress depends on the delivery of Stage 2 and 3 technology.
  • Imported reactors (Jaitapur): weigh energy security and low-carbon power against cost, liability concerns, safety and local acceptance, and consider localisation of components.
  • Way forward: expand nuclear capacity alongside strong regulation, safety culture, waste management and public engagement, and invest in indigenous reactor and fuel-cycle technology.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Thorium-232 is fertile, not fissile; U-233 is the fissile product and is not natural.
  • Fission is not fusion; tritium is a fusion fuel.
  • Heavy water is not radioactive; tritiated water is.
  • Enrichment is not reprocessing; enriched uranium is not automatically weapon-grade (that needs about 90% or more).
  • LEU for power reactors is typically a few percent, not "just under 20%". The 20% figure is only the upper limit of the LEU category.
  • Nuclear-powered is not nuclear-armed; SSN is not SSBN.
  • A planned plant is not an operating plant: Jaitapur is proposed.
  • "Indefinite" submarine endurance is limited by crew and supplies, not fuel.
  • Do not state the Natanz/Fordow strike date as July 2026; the verified US strike date is 22 June 2025.
