32. Types of Fuels: Basic Terms and Solid Propellants

The 31 Oct note (Section 30) separated rocket engines (carry fuel and oxidiser) from jet engines (carry fuel only). This section classifies what a rocket carries.

32.1 Basic terms

Terms used in this and the following sections
TermMeaning
FuelThe substance that burns.
OxidiserThe substance that supplies the oxygen for burning; a rocket carries its own, so it works in vacuum.
PropellantFuel plus oxidiser (the class uses "fuel" loosely for both).
Specific impulse (Isp)Thrust obtained per unit weight of propellant burnt each second. Isp = F / (m_dot x g0), where F is thrust in newtons, m_dot is the propellant mass flow in kg/s and g0 = 9.80665 m/s2. Its unit is the second. The class definition ("the number of seconds 1 kg of propellant can produce 1 kgf of thrust") is equivalent. The effective exhaust velocity is v_e = Isp x g0; for example 442 s x 9.80665 = about 4,335 m/s, matching the 4.33 km/s quoted for the CE-20 engine (Wikipedia). Higher Isp means less propellant for the same push.
Vacuum and sea-level IspIsp is higher in vacuum than at sea level for the same engine; for ISRO's Vikas engine 293 s (vacuum) against 262 s (sea level) (Wikipedia). Compare engines on the same basis.

32.4 Hybrid propellant

  • Definition (ISRO): "Unlike solid-solid or liquid-liquid combinations, a hybrid motor uses solid fuel and liquid oxidizer" (ISRO, 20 Sep 2022).
  • ISRO claims: liquid oxidiser flow control allows throttling and restart; "while both HTPB and LOX are green, LOX is safer to handle"; the motor is "scalable and stackable", potentially paving the way for a new propulsion system for forthcoming launch vehicles.
  • Status: a ground demonstration (30 kN, 15 s). No Indian launch vehicle flies a hybrid stage as of 2 Nov 2026 in the sources opened [Verification Required].

33. Liquid Propellants: Hypergolic, Low-Toxicity and Cryogenic

33.1 General position (class statement checked)

The class says liquid propellants generally give higher efficiency (specific impulse), allow restart and throttling, but cause corrosion and need careful handling. This is correct in outline. Contrast the two ISRO engines for which Isp is given in the sources opened: Vikas (UDMH-based fuel with nitrogen tetroxide, hypergolic) 293 s in vacuum, and CE-20 (LOX and LH2, cryogenic) 442 s in vacuum (Vikas, CE-20; see Figure 30). A figure for ISRO's solid motors was not found in the sources opened, so solids are not plotted [Verification Required]. "Corrosion" applies mainly to the storable propellants and to the extreme cold of the cryogenic pair (33.4).

33.4 Cryogenic propellants

Class statements on cryogenic engines checked
Class statementChecked position
Cryogenics is the application of temperatures below -150 deg C; the engine uses liquid hydrogen (-253 deg C) fuel and liquid oxygen (-183 deg C) oxidiser.Correct (The Hindu, 2010: oxygen remains liquid only below -183 deg C and hydrogen below -253 deg C; the -150 deg C threshold is the usual convention [Additional; not opened]). These are boiling points at normal pressure.
It has the highest specific impulse; Isp is in seconds.Correct among chemical propellants in launcher use: CE-20 442 s and the Russian KVD-1 462 s in vacuum against Vikas 293 s (Figure 30). [Additional] Electric and nuclear thermal propulsion can have higher Isp, so "highest among all" is limited to chemical rockets.
Due to higher specific impulse the payload can be increased exponentially.The relation is exponential, but in the mass ratio, not the payload. Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation: delta-v = Isp x g0 x ln(m0 / mf), so m0 / mf = exp[delta-v / (Isp x g0)] (m0 initial mass, mf final mass). [Additional] Illustration for an assumed delta-v of 4.0 km/s: with Isp 293 s, m0/mf = exp(4000 / 2873) = exp(1.392) = 4.02 (propellant 75% of lift-off mass); with Isp 442 s, m0/mf = exp(4000 / 4335) = exp(0.923) = 2.52 (propellant 60%). The same stage therefore carries a larger share of payload and structure. This is a teaching example, not a mission figure. See correction.
Suitable for upper stages and deep-space missions; clean, non-toxic products; restart and throttle possible.Sound. LVM3's C25 cryogenic stage re-ignited its thrust chamber in space after injecting CMS-03 on 2 Nov 2025 (LVM3-M5), a restart step that ISRO said is "paving the way" for multi-orbit missions (Wikipedia, quoting ISRO's chairman; secondary). CE-20 is throttleable between 180 and 220 kN (Wikipedia).
Challenges: engines are complex and costly; need ground infrastructure.Sound. India's own indigenous engine took about 16 years from the 1994 project start to the 2010 first flight attempt (The Hindu, 2010): see Section 34.
Hydrogen is light and has low density, so big tanks are needed, raising dry mass and aerodynamic drag.Correct. [Additional] Calculation using standard densities (liquid hydrogen about 71 kg/m3, liquid oxygen about 1,141 kg/m3; handbook values not opened here) and the CE-20 engine mixture ratio of 5.05 (Wikipedia): for 1 kg of hydrogen the oxygen is 5.05 kg; hydrogen volume = 1/71 = 0.0141 m3; oxygen volume = 5.05/1141 = 0.0044 m3; so the hydrogen tank is about 3.2 times the oxygen tank. For C25's 28 t load (ISRO) at that ratio: 4.6 t hydrogen (about 65 m3) and 23.4 t oxygen (about 20 m3). Illustrative only; the stage's real tank volumes were not opened.
Due to the high temperature of the combustion chamber, hydrogen starts boiling, increasing chances of failure, and it cannot be stored for long.Reworded. Hydrogen cannot be stored for long because even a small heat leak boils it off (standard physics; [Additional]). In the engine the heating is deliberate: the cold hydrogen is first run around the thrust chamber to cool it, where temperatures rise to over 3,000 deg C, and then goes to the gas generator (The Hindu, 2010, describing the staged-combustion design). A failure risk arises if hydrogen vaporises before reaching the pumps (pump cavitation; standard engineering, [Verification Required]). See correction.
The extreme low temperature makes materials brittle.Correct in general (many metals lose toughness at cryogenic temperature); India had to master new materials and processes such as vacuum brazing of the thrust chamber (The Hindu, 2010).

[Correction] Replace "payload increases exponentially" with "the propellant mass needed falls exponentially as Isp rises, so a higher-Isp upper stage carries a heavier payload to the same velocity". Payload does not grow exponentially with Isp.

[Correction] Replace "hydrogen starts boiling due to the chamber temperature" with: (i) liquid hydrogen boils off in storage from heat leak, so it cannot be stored for long; (ii) in the engine it is heated on purpose as a coolant; (iii) premature vaporisation upstream of the pump is a failure risk.

34. Indian Scenario: The Cryogenic Engine Story

34.1 From the Glavkosmos deal to indigenous engines

Class account checked against The Hindu (15 Apr 2010; updated 2016) and other sources
Class statementChecked position
1991: India and the Soviet Union signed an agreement; Glavkosmos was to transfer the cryogenic engine with technology to ISRO.January 1991: a deal with Glavkosmos to buy two cryogenic flight stages and the technology to make them in India; the engine was the 11D56 (RD-56), developed for the Soviet N1 moon rocket, giving 7.5 tonnes of thrust, in a stage carrying 12 tonnes of propellant (The Hindu; Wikipedia: KVD-1 adds a deal value of 120 million dollars, secondary). Before this, ISRO's Cryogenic Study Team (December 1982) had recommended an indigenous engine of about 10 tonnes thrust.
In 1993 Russian president Boris Yeltsin terminated the agreement on the ground that it violated the MTCR.The deal "violated the Missile Technology Control Regime" and fell foul of US law: the US imposed sanctions on ISRO and Glavkosmos in May 1992, and "a year later" Russia, which had inherited the contract after the break-up of the Soviet Union, backed out (The Hindu). Wikipedia dates the US sanctions to July 1993, which conflicts; the Hindu's account (May 1992) is used here [Verification Required]. The source opened does not name President Yeltsin; the name comes from the class.
Under MTCR, rockets and rocket subsystems cannot be transferred, whereas for cruise missiles the restriction applies above 300 km.The MTCR is a voluntary export-control regime for missiles and related technology. Its strictest category covers complete rocket systems and unmanned aerial vehicles able to carry a payload of at least 500 kg to at least 300 km, and their major subsystems such as rocket stages and engines; the 300 km figure applies to range for both rockets and cruise missiles, not only cruise missiles. The MTCR text was not opened [Verification Required]. [Additional] India became a member of the MTCR in 2016 (standard knowledge, not opened).
After persuasion, Russia agreed to share 7 cryogenic engines (KVD-1) without technology transfer.Correct in outline: Russia supplied seven ready-to-fly cryogenic stages (The Hindu). The engine flew only on GSLV Mk I (first flight 18 Apr 2001 per ISRO, though Wikipedia's KVD-1 page says 20 Apr; last flight 25 Dec 2010; Wikipedia). It was less efficient than predicted: in the first flight the rocket carried a 1.5-tonne satellite against the intended 2.5 tonnes.
ISRO started CUSP (Cryogenic Upper Stage Project), which resulted in two types of cryogenic engines.The Cryogenic Upper Stage project was launched in April 1994, to build an engine and stage closely modelled on the Russian design (The Hindu). ISRO states that CE-7.5 was developed under CUSP (ISRO GSLV page). The CE-20 was developed by LPSC for LVM3 (Wikipedia); whether it is also counted under CUSP was not opened [Verification Required].

35. Launch Vehicles of ISRO

Organisation: ISRO's Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (Thiruvananthapuram) designs launch vehicles; the Liquid Propulsion Systems Centre (Valiamala) and ISRO Propulsion Complex (Mahendragiri) develop the liquid and cryogenic stages; the Satish Dhawan Space Centre, SHAR (Sriharikota) integrates and launches them (ISRO launcher overview). ISRO lists PSLV, GSLV and LVM3 as "launchers under usage"; SSLV, HRLV, RLV-TD and the scramjet engine as "under development"; SLV-3 and ASLV as retired (page updated 17 Sep 2026).

35.1 Sounding rockets

Class statements on sounding rockets checked
Class statementChecked position
India started launching sounding rockets from Thumba (TERLS), close to the geomagnetic equator; they study the atmosphere above balloons and below satellites.ISRO: the Thumba Equatorial Rocket Launching Station (TERLS) was set up in 1963 at a place "close to the magnetic equator"; the first sounding-rocket launch from Thumba on 21 Nov 1963 "marked the beginning of the Indian Space Programme". Key Facts for Prelims

  • Isp = F / (m_dot x g0), unit seconds. Vikas 293 s (vacuum), 262 s (sea level); CE-20 442 s; Russian KVD-1 462 s. Higher Isp lowers the mass ratio needed (Tsiolkovsky).
  • Solid propellant: oxidiser + aluminium + HTPB binder; cannot be throttled or restarted; used in boosters (S200 about 205 t, S139, S12, S7, SSLV SS1-SS3). Liquid stages can be throttled and restarted.
  • Hybrid motor: solid fuel + liquid oxidiser; ISRO tested a 30 kN HTPB-LOX motor on 20 Sep 2022 (IPRC Mahendragiri, 15 s).
  • Hypergolic: self-igniting; UDMH/UH25 or MMH with N2O4 or MON; used in Vikas (PS2, GS2, L110), PS4 (MMH + MON) and SSLV VTM (MMH + MON-3).
  • Cryogenic: LH2 (-253 deg C) + LOX (-183 deg C); highest Isp of chemical propellants; upper stages; engine C25/CE-20 (LVM3) and CUS/CE-7.5 (GSLV Mk II).
  • History: Jan 1991 Glavkosmos deal (2 stages + technology); May 1992 US sanctions; 1993 Russia backed out (MTCR); Apr 1994 CUSP; seven Russian stages supplied; GSLV-D1 18 Apr 2001; GSLV-D5 5 Jan 2014 (first success with Indian cryo stage); LVM3 first flight 5 Jun 2017.
  • CE-7.5: 75 kN, staged combustion, LOX + LH2. CE-20: about 200 kN (180-220 kN), gas-generator cycle, C25 holds 28 t. C32: 32 t, 22 t engine, to raise LVM3 payload.
  • Payloads (ISRO): PSLV-XL 1,750 kg SSO (600 km); GSLV Mk II 2,250 kg GTO; LVM3 4,000 kg GTO and 8,000 kg LEO; SSLV 500 kg to 500 km.
  • Sounding rockets: TERLS 1963; first launch 21 Nov 1963; RSR 1975; RH-200 (10 kg, 80 km), RH-300 Mk-II (60 kg, 160 km), RH-560 Mk-II (100 kg, 470 km).
  • SLV-3: 4 solid stages; RS-1 on 18 Jul 1980. ASLV: 5 solid stages; D3 (20 May 1992) and D4 (4 May 1994) put SROSS satellites in orbit.
  • PSLV: 4 stages (solid-liquid-solid-liquid); first flight 20 Sep 1993; failures in May 2025 and on 12 Jan 2026. SSLV: HAL technology transfer signed 10 Sep 2025.
  • LVM3: 640 t, 43.5 m, three stages (2 x S200 + L110 + C25); all launches successful per ISRO; HRLV for Gaganyaan.

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Propellant choice is a trade-off. Solid: simple, dense, instantly ready, high thrust, but no throttle or restart; liquid: higher Isp and controllable but complex and, for hydrazines, toxic; cryogenic: highest Isp but costly, difficult to store and demands ground infrastructure; hybrid: promising for safety and restart, but only a ground demonstration so far. ISRO's vehicles mix all three because each stage has a different job (lift-off thrust, mid-flight efficiency, upper-stage performance).
  • Technology denial and self-reliance. The 1991 deal collapsed under US sanctions and MTCR pressure; India took about 16 years from the 1994 CUSP start to the first indigenous cryogenic flight attempt (2010) and about 20 years to its first success (2014), but now owns CE-7.5 and CE-20, an uprated 22 t engine and in-space restart. The lesson is that denied dual-use technology can be mastered, at a cost in time.
  • Dual-use and regimes. Rocket technology overlaps with missile technology (the class links SLV-3 with Agni and PSLV's multi-release with MIRV), so export controls such as the MTCR apply; India's later MTCR membership (2016, [Verification Required]) changed its access and its own export standing.
  • Reliability and reputation. Two PSLV failures in 2025-26 and an undisclosed cause dent the "workhorse" image just as commercialisation moves ahead (SSLV transfer to HAL; NSIL). The Hindu's editorial calls for more workforce and funds, a dedicated NavIC operator and a space law covering private participation and liability; these are the paper's recommendations, not government policy.
  • Industrialisation. HAL builds CE-20 engines (Integrated Cryogenic Engine Manufacturing Facility, Bengaluru) and has taken over SSLV; this reduces ISRO's production load and supports private launch services under the space-sector reforms.
  • Green propulsion. Replacing hydrazine propellants is a goal; credible steps are the HTPB-LOX hybrid, LOX-methane and kerosene-LOX engines, but the operational fleet is still hypergolic. Success should be judged on flown stages, not tests.
  • Way forward: (i) publish the PSLV failure cause and the corrective actions in a form that preserves customer confidence; (ii) fly the C32 stage and bring cryogenic re-ignition into operational multi-orbit missions; (iii) move the hybrid motor from the ground to a flight demonstrator; (iv) complete SSLV industrial hand-over with transparent flight milestones; (v) keep export-control compliance as technology partnerships widen.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Solid propellant needs an oxidiser: HTPB is binder-fuel, not a complete propellant.
  • "Solid always in the first stage" is false: LVM3's liquid core lights during the S200 burn; GSLV has liquid strap-ons.
  • Hybrid = solid fuel + liquid oxidiser (not the reverse); ISRO's test was in 2022, not recent.
  • Hypergolic oxidisers: N2O4 and MON (mixed oxides of nitrogen); PS4 uses MMH + MON, not UDMH + N2O4.
  • Replacement of hypergolic fuels is not complete: PSLV, GSLV Mk II and LVM3 still fly them.
  • GAP is a solid-propellant polymer binder, not a liquid fuel; "Carolox" is kerolox.
  • Isp unit is seconds; compare vacuum with vacuum. Payload does not rise "exponentially" with Isp; propellant mass fraction falls exponentially.
  • Liquid hydrogen boil-off in storage is the problem; heating in the engine is intentional regenerative cooling.
  • C25 holds 28 t (not 25 t); C32 is an uprated LVM3 stage, not an asteroid-mining engine; GSLV Mk II lifts 2,250 kg to GTO.
  • LVM3 8 t is LEO (600 km), not polar orbit; GTO capacity is 4 t. LVM3 has three stages, not four.
  • RH-200 apogee is 80 km and it is launched from Thumba and Balasore.
  • PSLV does not park satellites in geostationary orbit and its satellites do not "stay fixed in the sky" (2018 PYQ).
  • SLV-3 is not the same as the Rohini sounding rockets: RS-1 is a satellite; RH rockets are sounding rockets.
