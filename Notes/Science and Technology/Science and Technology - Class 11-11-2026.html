64.1 General relativity - gravity as curved space-time

  • According to Einstein's general theory of relativity (1915), gravity is not a force acting at a distance. It is the curvature of space-time produced by mass and energy, and objects move along the curves. Light bent by gravity was already covered in previous note, Figure 63.

64.2 What gravitational waves are

  • Gravitational waves (GWs) are ripples in space-time produced when masses accelerate. Nobel Prize material describes them as "like ripples in a four-dimensional spacetime that occur when objects with mass accelerate" (NobelPrize.org).
  • Analogy: an accelerating electric charge radiates electromagnetic waves; an accelerating mass radiates gravitational waves. The analogy has a limit: the mass must accelerate unevenly (asymmetrically). A perfectly spherical collapse or a smooth, symmetric spinning body emits none †.
  • Properties:
    • They travel at the speed of light.
    • They are transverse: they stretch and squeeze space at right angles to their direction of travel.
    • They have two polarisations, called "plus" and "cross".
    • They pass through matter almost unaffected, so they carry undistorted information about their source.
    • The effect is tiny: the strain (h = ΔL/L) at Earth is about 10-21 (APS Physics).
  • Worked numbers: ΔL = h × L = 10-21 × 4,000 m = 4 × 10-18 m. That is about one-thousandth of a proton's diameter, which is how LIGO describes the 2015 signal (LIGO).
  • Gravitational waves from many unresolved sources overlap all the time, forming a background that "washes" through the universe continuously (Section 64.12).

[Correction] "Grav waves are repulse of space time": they are ripples (oscillating distortions) of space-time. Nothing is repelled. Space is alternately stretched and squeezed.

64.4 Why gravitational waves matter

  • Study compact objects: black holes, neutron stars and pulsars. Black-hole mergers give off no light, so gravitational waves are the only way to see them.
  • Test general relativity in strong gravity. Examples:
    • GW250114 (signal of 14 Jan 2025, published 10 Sep 2025) was the clearest signal yet. It confirmed Hawking's area theorem (1971) and that the remnant behaves like a Kerr (spinning) black hole (APS).
    • Its signal-to-noise ratio was 80, compared with 26 for GW150914 (APS Physics).
  • Matter under extreme temperature, pressure and density: neutron-star mergers probe the neutron-star "equation of state". GW170817 linked such mergers to the formation of heavy elements such as gold (kilonova) †.
  • Cosmology - dark energy and dark matter [Additional]: gravitational waves give the distance to their source directly ("standard sirens"), which gives an independent Hubble-constant measurement (ESA). Mass-gap and sub-solar-mass searches test dark-matter ideas such as primordial black holes (LILA white paper). This is an indirect tool; gravitational waves do not "see" dark matter.
  • Multi-messenger astronomy [Additional]: the same event is studied through gravitational waves, light and neutrinos (Section 65.9).

64.6 LIGO and the global ground network

  • LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-wave Observatory) is funded by the US NSF and run by Caltech and MIT. It has two L-shaped detectors with 4 km arms, one at Hanford (Washington state) and one at Livingston (Louisiana), operated as one observatory (LIGO Lab).
  • Why more than one detector? Several detectors make it possible to:
    • reject local noise, because a real signal appears in every detector;
    • locate the source in the sky by comparing arrival times (triangulation). GW150914 reached Livingston about 7 ms before Hanford (LIGO).

64.7 How LIGO works

The principle is laser interferometry, as in a Michelson interferometer: two light beams that have travelled equal distances recombine, and their interference pattern reveals a tiny change in either path.

  1. Laser source: a stable, single-colour laser beam enters the instrument.
  2. Beam splitter at the corner where the two arms meet: it sends half the light down each arm.
  3. Two perpendicular arms, each 4 km long, enclosed in ultra-high-vacuum tubes so that air molecules do not disturb the light.
  4. Mirrors (test masses):
    • a mirror at the far end of each arm reflects the light back;
    • input mirrors near the corner make the light bounce back and forth many times (a Fabry-Perot cavity), which lengthens the effective path;
    • the mirrors hang as multi-stage pendulums, isolated from ground vibration †.
  5. Recombination: the returning beams meet again at the beam splitter.
  6. Photodetector at the output measures the light that reaches it.
  • No wave: the arms are tuned so that the two returning beams arrive exactly out of step. They cancel by destructive interference, and the photodetector stays (almost) dark.
  • Wave passes:
    1. One arm lengthens while the other shortens (Figure 70), by about 10-18 m.
    2. The two beams now travel unequal distances, so they no longer cancel exactly.
    3. Some light reaches the photodetector. Its rising and falling brightness traces the wave as a "chirp" pattern.
  • [Additional] Recent upgrades:
    • "Squeezed light" (a quantum technique) reduces laser noise across LIGO's band.
    • AI tools help detect signals and automate operations (Caltech, Apr 2026).

[Correction] "...the laser beams will travel unequal distance to avoid the destructive interference and some of them will pass through the beam splitter to strike photo detector": the correct sequence is as follows.

  1. Normally the beams do interfere destructively, so the output is dark.
  2. The wave makes the paths unequal, which spoils that cancellation.
  3. Light then leaks to the photodetector and produces the signal.

The "vacuum tube with a mirror" is in each arm, with the mirror at the far end.

64.8 LIGO-India

  • What it is: the third LIGO detector and the fifth gravitational-wave observatory of the global network (Caltech).
    • It is identical in design to the US detectors: an Advanced LIGO interferometer with two 4 km arms.
    • It sits on a 174-acre site chosen for low seismic noise at Aundha, Hingoli district, Maharashtra.
  • Approval and agencies:
    • The Union Cabinet approved it in April 2023 at an estimated cost of Rs 2,600 crore (PIB, 14 Apr 2023).
    • The Department of Atomic Energy is the lead agency, with the Department of Science and Technology. The US NSF and LIGO Lab supply the interferometer components and designs.
    • After completion it will be run as a national facility (PIB, 10 Aug 2023).
  • Status:
    • The foundation stones were laid in 2023.
    • Construction broke ground on 23 April 2026 (Caltech).
  • Why India matters for the network: a detector far from the US and European sites:
    • sharply improves sky localisation by triangulation;
    • resolves wave polarisation better because of its different orientation;
    • adds observing time when other detectors are down (Caltech).
  • Spin-offs [Additional]: skills in ultra-high vacuum, lasers and optics, seismic isolation, control systems and large-scale data analysis. IUCAA Pune, RRCAT Indore and IPR Gandhinagar are commonly named as lead institutions †.

64.10 LILA - Laser Interferometer Lunar Antenna

  • What it is: a proposed US-led programme, led from Vanderbilt University's Lunar Labs Initiative. Universities, NASA centres, industry and international partners take part. It aims to make the Moon a site for gravitational-wave science (Vanderbilt).
  • Link to NASA: it is designed to fit NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) and Artemis programmes (LILA white paper). It is not an approved NASA mission.
  • Why the Moon? The class reasons are correct:
    • no human (anthropogenic) noise;
    • no atmosphere, so no wind, pressure or humidity noise;
    • very low seismic noise.
    Two further advantages [Additional]:
    • A natural hard vacuum means no vacuum tubes are needed over kilometres.
    • The Moon's own vibration modes amplify low-frequency waves (arXiv 2508.18437).
  • Design in two phases (LEAG 2025):
    • LILA-Pioneer: an L-shaped set of three modules about 5 km apart, deployed by lander and lunar terrain vehicle.
    • LILA-Horizon: a triangular observatory with ~40 km arms.
  • The "bridge": it would open the 0.1-10 Hz "mid-band" between LIGO (above ~10 Hz) and LISA (millihertz). Signals in this band would give days to months of warning before neutron-star mergers (white paper).

[Correction] Two fixes to the LILA notes:

  • "Initiative of NASA": LILA is a proposal led by Vanderbilt University and its partners. It is aligned with NASA's lunar programmes but is not a NASA-approved mission.
  • "One L-shaped, other equilateral triangle": these are two successive phases, not two simultaneous observatories. The L-shaped LILA-Pioneer comes first; the triangular LILA-Horizon follows.

64.11 LISA (formerly eLISA) - the space observatory

  • Agency and status:
    • LISA is led by ESA with NASA and an international consortium.
    • It was selected as ESA's third large-class mission (L3) on 20 June 2017 and adopted on 25 January 2024.
    • Launch is planned for 2035 on an Ariane 6 rocket (ESA; ESA factsheet).
  • Design:
    • Three spacecraft form an equilateral triangle with 2.5 million km sides, more than six times the Earth-Moon distance.
    • The triangle trails Earth in its orbit around the Sun (heliocentric orbit), about 50 million km from Earth.
    • Free-floating gold-platinum test cubes sit inside each spacecraft. Lasers track changes in their separation "down to a few billionths of a millimetre" (ESA).
  • Heritage: LISA Pathfinder (launched December 2015, operated until mid-2017) proved that test masses can be kept in almost perfect free fall (ESA factsheet). The class phrase "successor of LISA Pathfinder" is correct.
  • Science:
    • mergers of massive black holes at galaxy centres;
    • tens of thousands of compact binaries in the Milky Way;
    • "extreme mass-ratio inspirals";
    • the expansion rate of the universe;
    • a possible early-universe background (ESA factsheet).

64.12 Pulsar Timing Arrays (PTA) - the galaxy as a detector

  • Principle:
    • Millisecond pulsars are rapidly spinning neutron stars. They sweep radio beams past Earth with a "ticking" that rivals atomic clocks, which is why pulsars are called nature's most accurate clocks (APS Physics).
    • A passing nanohertz wave stretches and squeezes space between a pulsar and Earth. Pulses then arrive slightly early or late.
    • Timing tens of pulsars together separates a real wave from the noise of any single pulsar.
  • Signature: the timing shifts of pulsar pairs are correlated in a way that depends on the angle between them, described by the Hellings-Downs curve (1983). Seeing this pattern is the "telltale signature" of a gravitational-wave background (APS Physics).
  • 29 June 2023 result:
    • Four collaborations reported evidence for a nanohertz gravitational-wave background:
      • NANOGrav (North America);
      • EPTA together with the Indian PTA (InPTA);
      • the Chinese PTA;
      • the Parkes PTA (Australia).
    • The statistical confidence was 3-4.6 sigma, below the usual 5-sigma "discovery" threshold (APS Physics).
    • NANOGrav alone used 67 pulsars over 15 years (arXiv 2306.16213).
    • The leading explanation is pairs of supermassive black holes. Early-universe or dark-matter origins cannot yet be excluded.
  • India's role: InPTA uses the upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (uGMRT) near Pune, run by NCRA-TIFR †. The "hum of the universe" in class refers to this background; physicists call the rising low-frequency noise a "red hum" (APS Physics).

[Correction] Four fixes to the PTA notes:

  • "Japanese PTA": the June 2023 results came from NANOGrav, EPTA/InPTA, CPTA and PPTA. No Japanese PTA was among them.
  • "Around a dozen pulsars": each array timed tens of pulsars (NANOGrav: 67).
  • "Unanimous conclusion": the results were consistent evidence at 3-4.6 sigma, not a confirmed detection.
  • "Delay in arrival of light": radio pulses arrive early or late, in a pattern correlated across pulsars.

The "Nanograv from USA" member is the NANOGrav collaboration (North America), which uses US and Canadian telescopes †.

[Correction] "The radio telescopes work on the principle of very long baseline interferometry": PTAs work by precise timing of pulses, which individual telescopes can do. They do not need VLBI.

  • The class description of VLBI is itself correct: widely separated dishes are combined into a virtual aperture as wide as the distance between the farthest dishes.
  • VLBI is the technique of the Event Horizon Telescope, which imaged black holes (Section 65.8).

65. Life Cycle of Stars and the Formation of Black Holes

65.1 A stable star - hydrostatic equilibrium

  • A star forms when a cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) collapses under gravity until its core is hot enough for hydrogen fusion. This needs at least about 0.08 solar masses (IAU OAE).
  • On the main sequence, the outward pressure from fusion heat balances the inward pull of gravity. This balance is called hydrostatic equilibrium, and it is the class idea of "expansion due to heat balanced by gravity".
  • Heavier stars burn their fuel faster and live shorter lives (IAU OAE).

65.2 Hydrogen runs out - giants and supergiants

  1. When the core's hydrogen is exhausted, fusion there stops and gravity wins: the core contracts and heats up.
  2. Hydrogen keeps fusing into helium in a shell around the core (the class "periphery").
  3. The hotter core then fuses helium into carbon (and oxygen), while the outer layers swell and cool.
  • An average (Sun-like) star becomes a red giant.
  • A massive star becomes a red supergiant. The most massive ones may become blue supergiants after losing their outer layers (IAU OAE).

65.3 End of low- and intermediate-mass stars (below about 8 solar masses)

  • When the core's helium is used up, the star is left with a carbon-oxygen core. The core is not hot enough to fuse carbon, so fusion there stops.
  • Unstable burning shells make the star pulsate. It throws off its outer layers as a glowing planetary nebula (not related to planets).
  • The core remains as a white dwarf held up by electron degeneracy pressure (IAU OAE). A white dwarf cannot exceed the Chandrasekhar limit (~1.4 solar masses) (previous note, Section 60.9; previous note, Figure 67).
  • Our Sun will follow this path.

[Correction] "In red giant, lack of enough carbon, the fusion will stop at oxygen": fusion stops because the core is not hot enough to ignite carbon, not because carbon is lacking. The core is mostly carbon and oxygen. "Loses matter layer by layer as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf" is correct.

65.4 End of massive stars (above about 8 solar masses) - core-collapse supernova

  1. Onion shells: each time a fuel runs out, the core contracts, heats and ignites the next fuel: carbon → neon → oxygen → silicon → iron. The star ends up like an onion, with an iron core surrounded by shells of silicon, oxygen, neon, carbon, helium and hydrogen (OpenStax).
  2. Why iron is the end: iron is the most tightly bound nucleus. Fusing it absorbs energy instead of releasing it, so the core loses its support (OpenStax).
  3. Collapse: electrons are squeezed into nuclei. Electrons and protons combine into neutrons and neutrinos, and the core collapses in a fraction of a second (OpenStax).
  4. Supernova: the rebound and the flood of neutrinos blast the outer layers away in a core-collapse (Type II) supernova. A different process from the Type Ia supernova of previous note, Figure 67.

[Correction] "Compression will cause fusion of carbon into iron; the helium from the periphery will come to the core to fuse and form carbon; at this point there will be a supernova" gives the wrong cause.

  • Carbon becomes iron through several stages (neon, oxygen, silicon).
  • Shell helium does not fall into the core to trigger anything.
  • The supernova happens because iron fusion yields no energy, so the iron core collapses.

65.6 Neutron stars, pulsars and magnetars

  • Neutron star: the collapsed core of a massive star, made mostly of neutrons. It is extremely dense (IAU OAE).
  • Pulsar: a rapidly spinning, magnetised neutron star that sends beams of radiation (mainly radio) from its magnetic poles. As the beams sweep past Earth, they appear as regular pulses (APS Physics). This matches PYQ 2023 (previous note).
  • Magnetar: a neutron star with the strongest magnetic field known, about 1014-1015 gauss †. It emits bursts of X-rays and gamma rays †.

[Correction] "Light-emitting neutron stars are known as pulsars" is too loose: all hot neutron stars emit some radiation. A pulsar is defined by its beamed, periodic emission produced by rotation, mostly at radio wavelengths.

65.9 Active galactic nuclei, quasars and neutrinos

  • Active galactic nucleus (AGN): the bright core of a galaxy where most radiation comes from material falling into a supermassive black hole, not from stars (IceCube).
  • Quasar: the most luminous type of AGN. The class word "egiant" refers to this.
  • Blazar: an AGN whose jet points almost straight at Earth.
  • Tidal disruption event: a star that strays too close to a black hole is torn apart. This produces a temporary flare.
  • Neutrinos from black-hole engines (IceCube, South Pole):
    • In 2018, IceCube identified the first high-energy neutrino source, the blazar TXS 0506+056.
    • In 2022, it reported evidence (4.2 sigma) of neutrinos from the active galaxy NGC 1068 (Messier 77) (IceCube).
    • IceCube was introduced in the previous note (PYQ 2015).

[Correction] "When this engulfs a star it results in an active galactic nucleus" confuses two things.

  • An AGN is powered by a supermassive black hole steadily accreting gas at a galaxy's centre over long periods.
  • A single star being swallowed causes a short-lived tidal disruption event.

65.10 India's instruments for black-hole and neutron-star science [Additional]

  • AstroSat (ISRO, 2015): India's first multi-wavelength space observatory †.
  • XPoSat (ISRO, launched 1 January 2024): India's first X-ray polarimetry mission. It studies black holes and neutron stars †.
  • uGMRT (NCRA-TIFR, near Pune): used for pulsar timing in InPTA (64.12).
  • LIGO-India: under construction (64.8).
  • Indian scientists take part in the LIGO Scientific Collaboration †.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • GR tests: gravitational redshift, gravitational lensing, Mercury's perihelion precession, gravitational waves. GWs are ripples of space-time from accelerating mass; they move at light speed and are transverse; strain ~10-21.
  • GW150914: 14 Sep 2015, announced 11 Feb 2016; black holes of 36 + 29 solar masses; 1.3 billion light-years; ~3 solar masses radiated. Nobel 2017: Weiss (half), Barish and Thorne (half).
  • Detectors:
    • LIGO: Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana; 4 km arms.
    • Virgo: Italy. GEO600: Germany. KAGRA: Japan.
    • LIGO-India: Aundha, Hingoli, Maharashtra; Rs 2,600 crore; DAE lead with DST; ground broken 23 Apr 2026.
  • Frequency bands: ground detectors ~10 Hz-kHz; LILA (Moon, proposed) 0.1-10 Hz; LISA (ESA, 3 spacecraft, 2.5 million km, launch 2035) mHz; PTA nHz (InPTA uses uGMRT; evidence 2023).
  • Stars:
    • Below ~8 solar masses: red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf (at most ~1.4 solar masses).
    • Above ~8 solar masses: red supergiant → iron core → core-collapse supernova → neutron star (~8-20/25) or black hole (above ~20-25).
    • Quark star: hypothetical. Pulsar: rotating neutron star. Magnetar: strongest magnetic field.
  • Black hole:
    • Parts: singularity; event horizon; Schwarzschild radius 2GM/c2 (~3 km per solar mass); photon sphere (1.5 Rs); ergosphere (spinning holes only); accretion disc; polar jets.
    • EHT images (VLBI): M87* (6.5 billion solar masses, 2019) and Sgr A* (4 million, 2022).
    • IceCube neutrino sources: TXS 0506+056 (2018), NGC 1068 (2022).

Mains Points

  • Gravitational-wave astronomy - a new sense:
    • It sees events that emit no light, such as black-hole mergers.
    • It tests general relativity in strong gravity (Hawking's area theorem, GW250114).
    • It gives independent cosmic distances (standard sirens).
    • Combined with light and neutrinos, it creates multi-messenger astronomy.
  • LIGO-India - significance:
    • It improves sky localisation and polarisation measurement for the whole network.
    • India becomes a host, not only a partner, of a frontier facility.
    • It builds skills in ultra-high vacuum, lasers, seismic isolation, controls and big-data analysis that carry over to industry.
    • It strengthens Indo-US science ties.
  • LIGO-India - concerns:
    • Long gestation: concept in 2011-16, approval in 2023, ground broken in 2026.
    • It depends on imported core hardware.
    • It must keep pace with LIGO upgrades so it is not obsolete when it starts.
    • It needs a long-term operating budget and a trained workforce.
  • India in multi-band GW science:
    • InPTA (uGMRT) contributed to the 2023 nanohertz evidence.
    • Indian groups can seek roles in LISA data analysis and in proposed lunar (LILA-type) and next-generation detectors.
    • Chandrayaan-era lunar capability could later host science payloads.
  • Way forward:
    • Fix and publish a LIGO-India commissioning timeline.
    • Indigenise vacuum and optics supply chains.
    • Fund national computing for GW data.
    • Integrate GW alerts with Indian telescopes (GMRT, AstroSat, XPoSat) for multi-messenger follow-up.
    • Expand university training through the IndIGO consortium.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Gravitational waves are not electromagnetic waves. LIGO uses laser light only as a ruler. The graviton has not been detected.
  • GEO600 is in Germany, not Italy. Hanford is in Washington state.
  • LIGO-India is the third LIGO detector but the fifth observatory in the network. It is under construction, not operating.
  • eLISA (old name; 1 million km; 2034) versus LISA (adopted 2024; 2.5 million km; 2035). Exam questions may use either set of figures.
  • LILA is a proposal (Vanderbilt-led), not a NASA mission. It covers the mid-band, while LISA covers mHz and PTA covers nHz.
  • PTA 2023 = evidence, not discovery. PTAs use timing, not VLBI. The EHT uses VLBI.
  • Two different supernovae: Type Ia is an exploding white dwarf; Type II is the core collapse of a massive star.
  • Quark stars are hypothetical. A pulsar is a kind of neutron star, not a separate remnant. The ergosphere exists only around spinning black holes.
  • The EHT images show the black hole's shadow and ring, not the event horizon itself. M87* (2019) was first; Sgr A* (2022) came second.
