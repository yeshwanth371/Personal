1. J&K at a Glance: Three Challenges

J&K's internal security challenge has an external dimension (cross-border terrorism), an internal dimension (militancy supported by local networks) and a criminal-economy dimension (organised crime). Each feeds the others.

The three challenges named in class (definitions added for revision)

Overground worker (OGW): a person who covertly supports militancy through finance, logistics, shelter and information without carrying arms. This is the definition from the rough notes. Treat OGW as a security-agency term; support must be proved in law, and lawful dissent or protest is not an OGW offence.

Exam point: keep terrorism (violence to create terror, often foreign-backed), insurgency / militancy (armed challenge to State authority, often with local support) and organised crime (profit-driven) separate, while showing how narco-terror and hawala link them.

2. Timeline: 1947 to 2025

3. 1947-1949: Accession and Internationalisation

3.1 Sequence

  1. Maharaja Hari Singh ruled the princely state and had three options on the lapse of British paramountcy: join India, join Pakistan or remain independent.
  2. A Standstill Agreement was offered to both dominions. Pakistan accepted it (15 Aug 1947); India did not sign and asked for further discussion.
  3. In October 1947 tribal raiders, supported by Pakistan, entered the State (Indian account: planned and supported by Pakistan; the Pakistani position differs). The Maharaja sought Indian military help.
  4. The Instrument of Accession was signed on 26 October 1947, accepted by the Governor-General on 27 October, and Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar the same day.
  5. India took the matter to the UN Security Council (1 Jan 1948, under Article 35 of the Charter). The UN formed the UNCIP; a ceasefire took effect on 1 Jan 1949, leaving the territory now called PoK and Gilgit-Baltistan under Pakistani control.
UN involvement (kept short for revision)
ItemWhat it was
UN, 1945UN Charter in force 24 Oct 1945; WW2 had just ended and the Cold War began to shape alignments. The Security Council first met in 1946 [Additional]
UNCIPUnited Nations Commission for India and Pakistan, set up in 1948; its resolutions of 13 Aug 1948 and 5 Jan 1949 sought a ceasefire, a truce and a plebiscite after Pakistani withdrawal [Additional; Verification Required: exact resolution numbers]
UNMOGIPUnited Nations Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan; monitors the Ceasefire Line after the Karachi Agreement (July 1949). India's position after the Simla Agreement (1972) is that it has no role [Additional]

[Correction] (a) "Raja Hari Singh" → Maharaja Hari Singh. (b) The rough notes say the Standstill Agreement "was signed with Pakistan which made it sceptical that Kashmir might be planning to join India". The agreement was signed by Pakistan, not by India; the sceptical party was the Pakistani leadership, which reportedly saw the State drifting toward India. (c) The UN ceasefire did not itself "give" territory to Pakistan; the ceasefire froze the positions on the ground, so the area already held by Pakistan is described by India as Pakistan-occupied. (d) "Peace plibiscite" → plebiscite proposal; it was never held because the preconditions (withdrawal and demilitarisation) were not agreed.

Exam point: the 1947 episode created the territorial dispute and the international legal frame that Pakistan later used to justify external support to militancy; India's position is that accession was complete and legal.

4. 1987-1990: Election, Armed Militancy and the Security Response

  • 1987 State Assembly election: the National Conference-Congress alliance faced the Muslim United Front (MUF). Many observers and opposition leaders claimed the election was rigged (claim is contested; no judicial finding is cited here). The perception of lost democratic space pushed some young activists towards armed groups.
  • Armed groups: JKLF (pro-independence, armed phase from 1988) and Hizb-ul-Mujahideen (pro-Pakistan, notionally formed in 1989). The All Parties Hurriyat Conference (a non-violent political alliance) was formed later, in 1993.
  • Operation Tupac (also spelt Topac): in the Indian security narrative, a Pakistani plan from about 1988 to foment an insurgency in J&K by a "bleed India" approach. Treat as an attributed claim [Verification Required].
  • 1990: targeted violence, the exodus of Kashmiri Pandits (from Jan 1990) and the collapse of civil order. The Armed Forces (Jammu and Kashmir) Special Powers Act, 1990 was enacted and areas were declared disturbed. The Army raised the Rashtriya Rifles, a dedicated counter-insurgency force under the Army (c. 1990).

[Correction] (a) "Hizbul and JKLF formed in 1984 from MUF members" → JKLF dates from 1977 and turned to arms in 1988; Hizbul dates from 1989. Some leaders had been linked to the MUF campaign of 1987, but the groups were not formed "from" the MUF. (b) "Hurriyat" was not a 1980s body; it was formed in 1993. (c) "selective violence" → targeted killings and intimidation. (d) The Pandit exodus is added because it is a core part of the 1990 security and rehabilitation story [Additional].

[Additional] AFSPA in J&K: a separate 1990 Act applies to J&K; the 21 October note covers the Sections 3-6 structure of AFSPA and the Supreme Court judgments. Its current operation in the UT after the 2019 reorganisation is [Verification Required].

5. 2016-2019: Open Support, OGWs and Operation All Out

  • 2016: the killing of Hizb commander Burhan Wani (8 July 2016) was followed by a very large funeral gathering, the first time open public support for a militant was shown on that scale. Stone pelting and mass strikes became frequent, with OGW networks supporting both street mobilisation and logistics.
  • 2017: Operation All Out - an intelligence-led, coordinated drive by the Army, J&K Police, CRPF and others to target militant commanders and their support networks. It was paired with action against terror financing (NIA) [Additional].
  • 5 Aug 2019: special provisions of Article 370 were rendered inoperative; the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 created the Union Territories of J&K (with a legislature) and Ladakh (from 31 Oct 2019). The Supreme Court upheld the abrogation in In re: Article 370 (11 Dec 2023) and directed restoration of statehood "at the earliest" [Additional; Verification Required].

[Correction] The rough notes say "militants, their commanders and over ground workers were eliminated". That overstates the outcome: Operation All Out sharply reduced the leadership of local groups and their networks, but militancy and attacks continued (see 2024-25). Also, the 2016 funeral is a claim about numbers ("lakhs") that depends on press estimates [Verification Required].

7. Reasons for the Challenges

  1. Geography: a mountain-and-valley terrain with long, difficult borders that favours infiltration and gives the region a potential to exist as a distinct unit. The rough note says this would let it "exist as an independent region"; this is an analytical argument, not a fact.
  2. Hostile neighbour: Pakistan's support to terrorist groups is the Indian position; it is relevant to the territorial claim and the use of non-state actors.
  3. Governance: corruption and weak local administration reduced trust in institutions.
  4. Youth hopelessness: uncertain jobs and a perceived lack of future increased vulnerability to recruitment and drugs. About 69% of the UT's population is below 35 (Mission Youth page; reference year not stated).
  5. Trust deficit: AFSPA and security operations have caused friction between civilians and security personnel; allegations of excesses must be addressed through accountable inquiry.

9. Initiatives for J&K: Details

The initiatives fall into three groups by implementer: (A) Army trust-building (Pehel, Sadbhavana); (B) J&K UT Government's Mission Youth (Mumkin, Tejaswini, SAHYTA, Rise Together, Parvaaz); (C) Union Government skill and empowerment schemes (Himayat, Udaan, Nai Roshni).

Mains Points

  • Layered problem: J&K combines an external territorial dispute, home-grown grievance and a criminal economy; a single instrument (force, development or politics) cannot solve it.
  • Counter-terror success versus political resolution: operations (All Out, Mahadev) reduce capability but do not remove drivers; the 2024-25 attacks on pilgrims and tourists show how terrorists target symbolic soft targets to damage normalcy and communal harmony.
  • Hearts and minds: Army-civil programmes and Mission Youth build contact and livelihoods, but depend on accountability (inquiry into excesses, calibrated use of AFSPA) and a civilian-led administration.
  • Youth focus: with about 69% of the population under 35, jobs, coaching and enterprise support (Mumkin, Parvaaz, Rise Together, Himayat, Udaan) address the recruitment pool; evaluate by outcomes (retention, wages) and not by scheme count.
  • Balanced way forward: (1) intelligence-led, rights-respecting counter-terror; (2) strict action on narco-terror and hawala; (3) credible, inclusive politics and a clear statehood roadmap; (4) quality jobs in tourism, horticulture, handicrafts and IT; (5) safe return and rehabilitation of displaced Pandits; (6) counter-radicalisation, de-addiction and online-content monitoring; (7) diplomatic pressure on state sponsorship through FATF and the UN.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Standstill Agreement: Pakistan signed; India did not.
  • Accession is not a plebiscite. The plebiscite was a UN-supported proposal, never held.
  • Hurriyat (1993) was not part of the 1987 events; Hizbul and JKLF differ (pro-Pakistan versus pro-independence).
  • Cross-border terrorism (external direction) is not the same as militancy (local armed groups); they overlap.
  • Sindoor was a cross-border strike; Keller, Nader, Mahadev were operations inside J&K.
  • Mission Pehel (Army) is not Mission Youth; Nai Roshni is national, not J&K-only.
  • Himayat (MoRD, rural livelihood) versus Udaan (MHA with NSDC and corporates).
  • OGW support must be shown in law; protest is not terrorism.
