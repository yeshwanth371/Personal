2. Internal Security: Meaning and Scope

Internal security is the security of a nation within its borders: maintaining peace, law and order and the integrity of the State against threats that arise from inside the country (insurgency, terrorism, extremism, organised crime, communal violence, cyber threats), including those supported from outside.

[Correction] (1) "Internal security means the protection of sovereignty and territorial integrity" is incomplete: defence of territory against external aggression is external defence (Ministry of Defence, armed forces), while internal security is the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) and State police domain. The two overlap in border and proxy-war situations. (2) "Insuring" is "ensuring"; "Promotio" is "promotion".

2.1 Law and Order, Public Order and Internal Security

The rough notes say wherever there are internal security challenges, law and order challenges are inevitable, and law and order problems can escalate into internal security challenges. This is correct as a working idea; it is made precise by the three concentric circles described by the Supreme Court in Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar (1965/66): law and order (widest) contains public order, which contains security of the State (narrowest). An act can disturb law and order without disturbing public order, and may disturb public order without endangering the security of the State.

Law and order
(any breach of law)→Public order
(disturbs the community at large)→Security of the State
(threatens the State itself)

[Additional] Constitutional position: "Public order" (Entry 1) and "Police" (Entry 2) are in the State List (Seventh Schedule). Article 355 makes it the Union's duty to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance. The Union supports States with Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs) and intelligence, but does not take over policing. This is why LWE is fought through State-Centre coordination (see the 2020 Mains question in the PYQ section).

Exam point: keep terrorism, insurgency, extremism, radicalisation, organised crime and protest apart. Extremism is an ideology or method that rejects constitutional and democratic means; insurgency is an organised armed rebellion against the State; terrorism is violence to create fear for a political aim; peaceful dissent is lawful and is not a security offence.

4. Causes: Development and the Spread of Extremism

LWE grew where poverty, landlessness, displacement and weak governance met a violent ideology. Development deficits do not "cause" extremism automatically; they create grievances and space that an armed group can exploit, which is why the response has to be security plus development plus rights.

4.1 Failure of Land Reforms

  • Zamindari abolition (1950s) ended large intermediaries but left unequal landholding and insecure tenants.
  • Ceiling laws limited landholding and meant to give surplus land to the landless. Problems noted in the class: the ceiling limit was too high; laws were implemented late (landlords had time to transfer land); and officials sided with landlords.
  • [Additional] Other weaknesses: exemptions (plantations, religious trusts), benami transfers, splitting of holdings among relatives, poor land records, and slow courts. Many land laws were placed in the Ninth Schedule (Article 31B) to protect them from court challenges, yet implementation was the failure. The 1972 national guidelines tried to bring more uniform, lower ceilings.
  • Result: a pool of landless labourers and marginal farmers (many Dalits and Adivasis), and the first Naxalite demand: "land to the tiller".

[Correction] "Govt officials supported" is restated as officials (revenue and local administration) often colluded with or sided with landowners; this is an analytical judgment found in land reform studies, not an allegation about every official. The rough note on timing is read as: ceiling laws were enacted late (mostly 1960s) after landlords had already transferred land, which reduced the surplus available.

4.2 Skewed Economic Development Model

  • Trickle-down approach: growth in the modern sector was expected to reach the poor over time. Where it did not (tribal and forest areas, remote districts), inequality widened.
  • Direct attack on poverty: targeted programmes for the poor, such as land redistribution, employment and anti-poverty schemes, instead of waiting for growth to filter down.
  • [Additional] Mineral-rich, forested Adivasi regions gave the State and industry large benefits, while local people bore displacement; this is why the 2015 Mains question asks about land alienation and "multiple displacements".

[Correction] "Trickle down model" and "direct attack on poverty" are two approaches to growth and poverty, not two models of the same level. The rough note is kept as a contrast: growth-led (trickle-down) versus targeted poverty-removal (direct attack).

4.3 Imbalanced Regional Development and Inadequate Rehabilitation

  • Backward regions had poor roads, schools, health care and markets, and little State presence beyond the police and forest department.
  • Displacement by mines, dams and industry with weak rehabilitation (compensation without livelihood) caused repeated impoverishment.
  • Absence of a grievance redressal mechanism: people had no trusted forum, so armed groups acted as "parallel courts" in some areas.
  • [Additional] Protective laws exist but were weakly enforced: the Fifth Schedule, PESA, 1996 (gram sabha powers in Scheduled Areas), the Forest Rights Act, 2006, and the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013. The Planning Commission's Expert Group on "Development Challenges in Extremist Affected Areas" (constituted 2006; report 2008, chaired by D. Bandyopadhyay) stressed tenure security, enforcement of PESA and FRA, and rights-respecting land acquisition. [Verification Required] confirm details in the official report.

5. Present Status of LWE (as of October 2026)

[Additional] Official reports (reported by secondary sources) say LWE violence has fallen sharply. MHA data cited in Parliament: 1,936 incidents and 1,005 deaths (civilians plus security forces) in 2010, against about 222 incidents and 95 deaths in 2025. [Verification Required] the PIB release could not be opened; figures come from a secondary summary of MHA statements.

  • MHA reportedly told States in April 2026 that no district is classified as LWE-affected; 37 "Legacy and Thrust" districts (insurgency eliminated but development and vigilance needed) and one "District of Concern" (West Singhbhum, Jharkhand) were identified. Union Home Minister Amit Shah told Parliament on 30 March 2026 that India had effectively become free of Maoism (The New Indian Express, 19 April 2026). Isolated incidents by residual elements continue in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh.

7. Gaps and Challenges

  • Unresolved grievances: land titles, forest rights, displacement and compensation are still contested in Scheduled Areas.
  • Human rights and accountability: fake-encounter and vigilantism allegations (Salwa Judum) weaken trust; security operations must stay within law.
  • Coordination: police is a State subject; inter-State cooperation and intelligence sharing are essential.
  • Rehabilitation: surrendered cadres need livelihoods and security, and the community must see lasting benefits.
  • Risk of resurgence: residual cadres, urban support networks and weak governance can revive violence.

8. Way Forward

  1. Treat LWE as a development-governance-security problem, not only a law-and-order one.
  2. Implement PESA and FRA and secure land titles; seek gram sabha consent for mining and land acquisition.
  3. Deliver last-mile services (roads, schools, health, forest produce markets) in "Legacy and Thrust" districts.
  4. Strengthen accountability mechanisms and community policing; avoid unregulated local militias.
  5. Strengthen grievance redressal and fast-track courts for tribal land disputes.
  6. Keep intelligence and inter-State coordination in place after the formal declaration.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • Naxalbari uprising: 1967, Darjeeling district, West Bengal; leaders Charu Mazumdar, Kanu Sanyal, Jangal Santhal.
  • CPI(M) formed 1964; CPI(ML) formed 22 April 1969; CPI (Maoist) formed 21 September 2004 (PWG + MCC).
  • Public order and police: State List; Article 355: Union's duty to protect States from internal disturbance.
  • PESA 1996; FRA 2006; National Policy and Action Plan on LWE 2015; SAMADHAN 2017.
  • Nandini Sundar v. State of Chhattisgarh (2011): SPOs/Salwa Judum struck down.

Mains Points

  • LWE sits at the intersection of economic grievance and ideological violence; poverty alone does not explain it.
  • Failed land reform (high ceilings, exemptions, benami transfers, collusion) is the historical root; displacement without rehabilitation is the modern one.
  • The 2008 Planning Commission expert group framed the answer as rights, development and governance, with security only creating conditions for them.
  • The Supreme Court's 2011 judgment shows the limits of a purely militarised response.
  • Balanced way forward: sustain security gains while enforcing tribal land and forest rights, accountable policing, livelihood support and inter-State coordination.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • CPI(M) (1964) is a parliamentary party; CPI(ML) (1969) was the armed Naxalite party. Not the same.
  • Naxalism, Maoism and LWE are related but not synonyms; lawful Left politics and peaceful tribal protests are not LWE.
  • Law and order is not the same as public order or security of the State (Lohia).
  • "Red corridor" is an informal term, not an official category.
  • Internal security (MHA, State police) is not external defence (MoD, armed forces).

30-Second Revision

Internal security = peace and order inside the borders; police and public order are State subjects, Article 355 is the Union's duty. LWE: Naxalbari 1967 → CPI(ML) 1969 → PWG + MCC → CPI (Maoist) 2004. Causes: failed land reforms, trickle-down growth with weak direct poverty attack, regional imbalance, displacement and no grievance redressal. Response: 2015 policy, SAMADHAN, SRE/SIS, development, PESA/FRA, rehabilitation. Status: MHA reportedly declared no LWE-affected district in April 2026; 37 Legacy and Thrust districts remain under watch. Limit: Salwa Judum (2011).
