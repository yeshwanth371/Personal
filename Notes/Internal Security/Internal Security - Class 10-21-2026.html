1. Manipur at a Glance

Manipur's conflict is ethnic and territorial in character, layered on drugs, land, migration and arms - it is not a classic secessionist insurgency alone. Since 3 May 2023 it has moved through three phases: Meitei vs Kuki-Zo (2023-25), central rule and a security-reset (Feb 2025 - Feb 2026), and a new Naga vs Kuki-Zo fault line (from Feb 2026).

2. Special Regimes: ILP, Sixth Schedule and Article 371C

  • Inner Line Permit (ILP): a travel permit regime for Indian citizens from outside the protected State, created under the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873. It was extended to Manipur by a Presidential order on 11 December 2019 (Adaptation of Laws (Amendment) Order, 2019), after a long demand to protect indigenous people from in-migration. It now covers Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram and Manipur.
  • Sixth Schedule (Articles 244(2), 275(1)): autonomous district and regional councils for tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram only.
  • Manipur's hills: governed through Article 371C (27th Amendment, 1971): a Hill Areas Committee of the legislators from the hill districts, with the Governor having a special responsibility for its working. Six hill autonomous district councils exist under the Manipur (Hill Areas) District Councils Act, 1971, which are weaker than Sixth Schedule councils; hill groups have long demanded stronger autonomy [Additional; Verification Required: number and powers].

[Correction] The rough notes say "2020 - Inner line permit" and "For Inner line permit and 6th schedule states - Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur". Corrected: the ILP order was issued in December 2019 (the regime became operational in Manipur from about 1 January 2020 [Verification Required]). The four-State list is correct for ILP only. The Sixth Schedule states are Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram; Manipur and Nagaland are not Sixth Schedule states (Manipur: Article 371C; Nagaland: Article 371A). Mizoram is the only State with both. Also: ILP is a permit, not a "permission to enter the area" for local residents, and it applies to Indian citizens (foreigners need separate permits).

4. The Ethnic Conflict Since May 2023

4.1 The Trigger

  • On 27 March 2023 the Manipur High Court directed the State to consider the Meitei demand for Scheduled Tribe status "expeditiously, preferably within four weeks", and to send a recommendation to the Union.
  • A Tribal Solidarity March in Churachandpur on 3 May 2023 opposing the order was followed by clashes, which spread across the State [Additional].
  • In May 2023 the Supreme Court termed the High Court's direction wrong because only Parliament can modify the ST list (Articles 341 and 342; State of Maharashtra v. Milind, 2000). In February 2024 the High Court deleted paragraph 17(iii) of its March 2023 order.

[Correction] The notes say the High Court "directed the state for having the provisions for identifying giving ST status". More exactly, the High Court asked the State to consider and recommend Meitei inclusion; the actual power of inclusion lies with Parliament through a law amending the Constitution (Scheduled Tribes) Order, and the High Court itself withdrew the direction in February 2024. The ST demand therefore remains a political, not a judicial, question. It was the trigger, not the only cause (Section 4.2).

4.2 The Five Causes in the Notes (with Analysis)

  1. ST status: Meiteis demand ST status to protect their identity, language and land against in-migration. Nagas and Kuki-Zo oppose it, citing the Lokur Committee (1965) and Bhuria Commission criteria. These criteria (indications of primitive traits, distinctive culture, geographical isolation, shyness of contact with the community at large, and backwardness) are meant for marginalised and isolated communities. Tribal groups also fear that a numerically and politically dominant community would compete for ST-reserved jobs, seats and land.
  2. Land: tribal land in the hills cannot be transferred to non-tribals (protection under the Manipur Land Revenue and Land Reforms Act, 1960 [Verification Required: section]), while tribals can buy land anywhere, including the valley. Meiteis, with about 10% of the area, see this as asymmetrical; tribals see it as a protective, constitutional safeguard.
  3. Language: Manipuri (Meiteilon) is the State's official language and lingua franca but tribal communities have their own languages (Tangkhul, Thadou, Paite and others) and find it hard to identify with a Meitei-language identity.
  4. Faith and identity: differences between the largely Hindu/Sanamahi Meitei and the largely Christian hill tribes add to perceptions of separateness. This is an aggravating factor, not a root cause; religion is not the same as communalism.
  5. Drugs and organised crime: the notes argue that the conflict began soon after the State's crackdown on drug production (from 2018's "War on Drugs", intensified with poppy destruction and forest evictions in early 2023), so organised criminals are fuelling it. Analytical interpretation, not established fact: Manipur lies near the Golden Triangle, poppy cultivation and drug trafficking are real problems, but blame for the violence is disputed by all sides and no court finding attributes the conflict to drug networks.

[Correction] The notes cite the "lokur committee and Bhuria commission" without detail. The Lokur Committee (1965) was the Advisory Committee on the Revision of the SC/ST Lists; its five indicators are the standard ST test in practice. The Bhuria Commission is the Commission on Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes (2002-04) [Verification Required: which Bhuria body and what it said on Meitei status]. The notes' phrase "marginalised and isolated" summarises the indicators; it is not a statutory test. Meitei groups argue that the community lost its tribal identity after the 1949 merger [Verification Required].

[Correction] "Manipuri is actually the language of the Meitei community" is accurate (Meiteilon or Meeteilon) but incomplete: it is the State's official language and lingua franca, and its Eighth Schedule inclusion in 1992 followed a long Meitei movement. Difficulty in associating with it is a perception reported by hill groups, which is why a three-language policy for schools and a protection of tribal languages are policy issues, not a legal defect.

[Correction] Note 5 in the rough notes states "this cannot be merely a coincidence" and gives organised criminals a "direct role". Retained as an argument, not as proven. Timing alone is not causation; a balanced answer should call drugs and forest-eviction policy one contributing factor, alongside the ST demand, land, identity, a weak political settlement of the hills, and arms looted from police armouries in 2023 [Additional; Verification Required: number of weapons looted].

5. Government Response and Peace Process

5.1 Constitutional and Executive Measures

  • Article 355 gives the Union the duty to protect every State against internal disturbance; public order is a State subject (Entry 1, List II), so the Centre normally acts in aid of the State.
  • President's Rule (Article 356) was imposed on 13 February 2025 after the Chief Minister's resignation, with the Assembly kept in suspended animation. It was revoked on 4 February 2026 and a new government was sworn in under Chief Minister Yumnam Khemchand Singh, with Kuki and Naga Deputy Chief Ministers (news reports) [Verification Required]. Under Article 356(4), approval by Parliament is needed every six months and the total period is capped at one year unless the conditions in Article 356(5) are met [Additional].
  • Security architecture [Additional]: a Unified Command headed by a Security Adviser (from May 2023), deployment of the Army, Assam Rifles and CAPFs, and a three-member Commission of Inquiry under Justice Ajai Lamba (June 2023) [Verification Required].
  • Supreme Court role [Additional]: relief and rehabilitation oversight (including a committee of former High Court judges headed by Justice Gita Mittal, 2023) and, in 2026, directions on the reopening of blocked highways; the Manipur government told the Court on 28 September 2026 that the NH-2 blockade had been removed [Verification Required].

5.2 Suspension of Operations (SoO)

  • Background: SoO is a ceasefire-type pact signed in 2008 among the Union Government, the Manipur Government and Kuki armed groups grouped under the Kuki National Organisation (KNO) and United People's Front (UPF) (about 24 groups). Designated camps, weapons storage and a monitoring body (the Joint Monitoring Group) are part of it.
  • Revised pact (reported 4 September 2025): (1) camps to be reduced in number and relocated away from highways, populated areas, and a reasonable distance from the Myanmar and inter-State borders; (2) foreign nationals to be removed from cadre lists; (3) weapons to be deposited in nearby CRPF/BSF camps; (4) stipends only via Aadhaar-linked bank accounts and only for verified cadres present at inspection; (5) strict verification by the JMG; (6) no violence, recruitment, extortion or ties with other armed groups; (7) acceptance of the Constitution and India's sovereignty; (8) the pact is reported to run for one year and can be reviewed or suspended for violations [Verification Required: duration, camp numbers and renewal status].

[Correction] The notes say "In 2025, the SoO was revised" and describe the signatories loosely. Corrected and clarified: the original pact dates from 2008; KNO and UPF are umbrella bodies of about 24 groups; the notes' four features match the reported revision (monitoring, relocation, financial verification, constitutional loyalty). SoO is not a peace accord: a political settlement is a separate, pending track. The Kuki-Zo side reportedly seeks a Union Territory with a legislature [Verification Required], which the Meitei and Naga sides and the State's territorial-integrity position oppose.

Common confusion: SoO (Kuki groups, Manipur; JMG monitoring) is different from the NSCN (IM) ceasefire (Nagaland; CFMG). Both are ceasefires, not settlements. SoO is also not the Free Movement Regime or the Framework Agreement.

5.3 The Naga-Kuki Tension (2026)

  • Since February 2026 (a local altercation in the Ukhrul area is reported as the starting point), tensions between Nagas and Kuki-Zo have produced killings, hostage-taking, village burning and economic blockades on national highways in hill districts such as Ukhrul, Kamjong, Kangpokpi and Senapati [Verification Required: districts and events].
  • Reasons given in the notes: competing territorial claims in mixed hill areas (Naga homeland claims versus Kuki-Zo "Kukiland"/UT demands); conflicting political aspirations (Naga final settlement with NSCN (IM), Kuki-Zo demand for a separate administration); ethnic identity questions; and the legacy of the violent Kuki-Naga clashes of the 1990s.
  • Recent pressures: displacement, resource and land competition, blockades and demands for separate administrative arrangements.

[Correction] The notes say "violent 1990 clashes". The Kuki-Naga clashes are generally dated 1992-1997 (a period of roughly a thousand deaths and hundreds of villages affected, per secondary sources); a single year "1990" is imprecise [Verification Required: dates and casualty figures]. The notes' "Feb 2026" start is retained, based on news reports.

[Additional] Why it matters for internal security: the 2026 phase breaks the earlier valley versus hill picture, so a single "Meitei vs tribal" frame no longer explains the State. It links back to Nagalim (19 October note, Section 2.3) and to the Naga final settlement: any accord that alters Manipur's territory needs the consent of the State (Article 3 requires reference to the State legislature, whose views are not binding).

6. Critical Analysis of AFSPA

The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 gives the armed forces extended powers in areas declared "disturbed". It exists because the armed forces, deployed beyond their primary role (external defence), need legal certainty against asymmetrical threats; it is criticised because the same certainty can limit accountability.

6.1 Origin and Coverage

  • The Act was first enacted in 1958 for Assam and Manipur (the Naga Hills then part of Assam) and extended to the other North-Eastern States by the 1972 amendment. A separate Armed Forces (Jammu and Kashmir) Special Powers Act, 1990 applies to J&K. A 1983 Act for Punjab and Chandigarh was withdrawn in 1997 [Additional].
  • Current coverage (reported): Nagaland, Assam, most of Manipur (valley police stations excluded) and parts of Arunachal Pradesh (Tirap, Changlang and Longding and some police-station areas). Meghalaya (2018) and Tripura (2015) are fully out. The MHA renews notifications every six months; the notification for Manipur was reported on 25 September 2026 for 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, excluding the jurisdiction of 13 police stations in five valley districts, whereas the earlier exclusion was widely reported as 19 police stations [Verification Required: current number and list].

[Correction] The notes say "1958 - Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Nagaland, Manipur; 1990 - J&K". Corrected: 1958 is the year of the Act for Assam and Manipur, extended to all North-East states in 1972; the four States listed are where it is in force today (Arunachal only partly; Manipur excluding valley police stations). The 1990 Act is a separate Act for J&K. See also C2 of the 19 October note.

Links to the Oting case study (19 October note) show the consequences of weak accountability.

Mains Points

  • Manipur is a governance and trust problem before a law-and-order problem: the High Court order was only the trigger; unresolved land, identity, hill-autonomy and representation issues, and loss of trust in the State police, sustained the violence.
  • The conflict has changed shape: valley versus hills (2023) to Naga versus Kuki-Zo (2026). Peace needs all communities at the table; talks with one group alone create new fault lines.
  • SoO shows the limits of ceasefires: a ceasefire without a political settlement and without verification invites grievance (camp location, foreign cadres, financial flows); the 2025 revisions respond to this but need independent monitoring.
  • Federal and constitutional dimensions: Article 355 versus State autonomy; President's Rule as a stop-gap; the Supreme Court as a monitor of relief and highways; Article 3 and territorial integrity in Naga and Kuki demands.
  • AFSPA: necessary only where armed groups operate and the police cannot cope, but it should be area-specific, time-bound, reviewed and accountable; Section 6 sanction should not become a shield; calibrated withdrawal (valley areas) with investigation of past cases builds trust.
  • Balanced way forward: (1) rehabilitation and return of displaced people; (2) police reform, recovery of looted arms and impartial law enforcement; (3) inclusive dialogue with Meitei, Naga, Kuki-Zo and civil society; (4) credible, independent monitoring of SoO; (5) strengthening hill autonomous councils and addressing land safeguards by consent; (6) border management and action against drug networks; (7) restoration of free highway movement with Supreme Court monitoring; (8) development projects (Kaladan, rail, air) as supplement, not a substitute.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • ILP is not the Sixth Schedule. Manipur has ILP and Article 371C, not the Sixth Schedule. Mizoram has both.
  • The ST list is changed by Parliament, not by the High Court or the State; the HC direction was withdrawn in 2024.
  • Meitei share is about 53% (Census 2011), not 65%.
  • 71st Amendment 1992 is not the Citizenship Amendment Act; it concerns the Eighth Schedule.
  • SoO (Kuki groups; JMG) is different from the NSCN (IM) ceasefire (CFMG) and from the Framework Agreement; SoO is not a political settlement.
  • AFSPA 1958 is for the North-East; the 1990 Act is a separate J&K Act; the Section 6 sanction applies to prosecution, not to registering an FIR or investigation.
  • The Court upheld AFSPA but did not grant blanket immunity; "AFSPA means the Army can kill anyone" is wrong - force needs prohibited acts and due warning.
  • Drug link to the conflict is an analytical claim, not a finding.
  • Kaladan is a Myanmar transit route to Mizoram, not a free-movement regime.
