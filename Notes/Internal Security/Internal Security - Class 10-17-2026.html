1. LWE: Meaning, Origin and Organisations (Recap with Corrections)

Left Wing Extremism (LWE) is a violent movement that draws on Marxist-Leninist-Maoist ideas. It seeks to overthrow the State through armed struggle (a "protracted people's war") and to establish a classless society. It began as a violent conflict between landless labourers and landowners at Naxalbari (Darjeeling district, West Bengal) in 1967. Peaceful left-wing politics and lawful tribal or farmer protest are not LWE.

2. Causes of the Rise of LWE (Recap with Corrections)

LWE is mainly a development and governance problem in Jal-Jangal-Zameen (water, forest, land) that an armed ideology exploited. The causes in the notes:

  1. Failed land reforms: ceilings were too high; reforms were not applied retrospectively, so landlords had already transferred land; ground-level officials sided with landowners.
  2. Skewed economic development: trickle-down growth did not reach tribal and remote areas; direct poverty-removal programmes suffered from leakages.
  3. Regional imbalance, displacement and poor rehabilitation: industry and mining in mineral-rich forest areas displaced tribals with little or no compensation; backward areas stayed backward.
  4. No grievance redressal and weak State presence: a trust deficit created a vacuum that armed groups used for their own interests.

[Correction] Land ceiling in Uttar Pradesh: the notes say the ceiling was 15 acres per individual until 1973, then 12.5 acres per family. The UP Imposition of Ceiling on Land Holdings Act, 1960 set the ceiling at about 40 acres per family (for fair-quality land); the 1972 amendment, in force from 1973, cut it to 12.5 acres per family [Verification Required: confirm from the India Code text]. The point for the exam is unchanged: the original ceilings were too high and arrived late.

[Correction] Land redistributed: the study credited to "A R Bandopadyay" (11% of land could have been redistributed, 1.1% actually was) could not be traced to a source [Verification Required]. Use it only with the words "according to one study". West Bengal: the note says communist leaders' "vested interests fuelled the violence". This is an analytical claim; a neutral statement is that land reform in West Bengal was partial in 1967 and tenancy and landlord conflicts in the Naxalbari area led to the uprising. Tribal displacement: STs are 8.6% of the population (Census 2011); the "about 40% of the displaced" figure is a widely quoted estimate, not an official count [Verification Required].

Full causes, case studies and the "trickle-down versus direct attack" correction: see the 16 October note, Section 4.

4. National Policy and Action Plan (2015)

The National Policy and Action Plan to Address LWE (approved by the Union Government in 2015) is a multi-pronged strategy with three fronts:

  • Security front: strengthen State police and Central forces, intelligence and operations (Section 5).
  • Development front: roads, mobile connectivity, schools, health, financial inclusion in affected districts.
  • Ensuring rights and entitlements: implement the Forest Rights Act, 2006, PESA and welfare entitlements for local communities.

Exam point: the policy is a Centre-State partnership. "Police" and "public order" are State subjects (State List, Seventh Schedule); the Centre supports with forces, funds, intelligence sharing and coordination (the Union's duty under Article 355).

[Correction] The class note says the "Naxal-free" transformation was "through the National Policy and Action Plan, based on three pillars: Vishwas, Nirman, Jan Kalyan". Keep two layers apart: the 2015 National Policy and Action Plan is the policy framework (security, development, rights); Vishwas, Nirman and Jan Kalyan is the three-pillar framing used in the 2026 Government of India (PIB) account of how the campaign succeeded (Section 6). 5.3 SAMADHAN Doctrine (2017)

SAMADHAN is the operational doctrine of the Ministry of Home Affairs for LWE, announced in 2017 by the Union Home Minister at a meeting with LWE-affected States.

[Correction] "N - No finances" means "No access to financing" for extremists (cutting extortion and funding, with agencies such as the NIA and Enforcement Directorate); "Action for all theatre" means a specific action plan for each theatre (area); "Dashboard for KPIs" means dashboard-based key performance indicators and key result areas. "Harnessing technology" in the notes is spelt "Harnessing".

6. Government Initiatives and the "Naxal-Free" Status

LWE is a multi-dimensional threat, so the response is multi-pronged. The Government of India's 2026 account groups the campaign into three pillars: Vishwas (trust), Nirman (development) and Jan Kalyan (welfare and dignity).

6.1 Development-Front Measures [Additional]

  • Road Connectivity Project for LWE Affected Areas (RCPLWEA): all-weather roads in remote forest areas.
  • Mobile towers and 4G coverage to break the communication blackout.
  • Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS) and ITIs for tribal youth; more bank branches and post offices.
  • Fortified police camps deep in earlier "liberated" areas, which both secure and extend the State's presence (Drishti IAS, 2026, citing Abujhmad as an example).
  • Other schemes in the earlier note: Aspirational Districts Programme, PESA and FRA.

Source for this list: Drishti IAS, "Left Wing Extremism-Free India" (secondary). [Verification Required] confirm scheme details from MHA or PIB.

[Correction] The class note says "on 31 March, India was declared Naxal-free". 31 March 2026 was the target date; the Home Minister's statement in the Lok Sabha came at about that date (reported as the day before the deadline), and the MHA communication on district classification followed in April. Use "declared Naxal-free in 2026 (target date 31 March 2026)". Interpretation: a security milestone does not by itself settle land, forest and displacement grievances. Residual risks: security vacuum when CAPFs withdraw, conversion of extortion networks into organised crime, weak service delivery ("hardware without software"), and overground or urban support networks that must be tackled without harming civil liberties (Drishti IAS, 2026).

7. India's Post-Operation Sindoor Counter-Terror Approach

7.1 Background

After the Pahalgam terror attack (22 April 2025), India launched Operation Sindoor on 7 May 2025 against terrorist infrastructure in Pakistan and Pakistan-occupied Jammu and Kashmir [Verification Required: dates from memory and secondary summaries]. In his address to the nation on 12 May 2025 the Prime Minister described a "new normal" with three principles.

[Correction] (1) "Op Sindhoor" is Operation Sindoor. (2) The notes say India "released a new security doctrine". The three principles were set out in the Prime Minister's address; they are widely described as a doctrinal shift, but no formal published doctrine document could be verified. Write "declared policy" or "stated doctrine", not "a released doctrine". (3) "Offensive defence" is an analytical label used by commentators; it was not a phrase in the address [Verification Required].

7.2 Offensive Defence and its Forms

Offensive defence (as taught in class): the defender chooses the time and theatre of action instead of waiting to absorb an attack. It can take the following forms:

[Correction] (1) Operation Sunrise was in 2019, not 2015. The June 2015 action on the Myanmar border was a separate cross-border raid and was not called Operation Sunrise. (2) Operation All Clear was an operation by Bhutan's army, so it is an example of cooperation, not of India entering Bhutan. (3) The unfinished line "Surgical strike: ... Eg: Surgical strike in" is completed with the 2016 LoC strikes (and the 2019 Balakot air strike as a later example). (4) "Hot pursuit" has a narrower meaning in international law (immediate, continuous pursuit of an offender across a boundary, for example a ship under UNCLOS Article 111); the class uses it loosely for consent-based joint action. [Verification Required] the 2016, 2019 and 2015 operational details rely on official statements widely reported; Pakistan's and Myanmar's positions differ.

7.3 Limits and Trade-offs [Additional]

  • Legal basis: self-defence claims under UN Charter Article 51 versus the ban on use of force in Article 2(4); states differ on action against non-state actors based in another country.
  • Escalation risk between nuclear-armed neighbours; hence the "no nuclear blackmail" principle must be matched with crisis communication.
  • Deterrence by punishment does not replace deterrence by denial: border fencing, intelligence, counter-terror policing, and legal tools.
  • "No distinction between terrorists and sponsors" is a policy about States and organised groups that sponsor terror. It does not mean communal or collective attribution to a community.

8. Gaps and Way Forward (Short)

  • Consolidate the gains: strengthen State police and local intelligence before CAPF drawdown; keep inter-State coordination.
  • Close the rights gap: title under FRA, PESA gram sabha consent, rights-based land acquisition and rehabilitation (the same grievances that powered LWE).
  • Deliver services, not only buildings: teachers, doctors and administrators present in remote areas.
  • Rehabilitate surrendered cadres with livelihoods and protection; avoid unregulated local militias (Salwa Judum lesson).
  • Counter-terror abroad: use graded, lawful and well-communicated responses, along with diplomacy and financial pressure (FATF).

Mains Points

  • LWE's decline shows that sustained, intelligence-led security action combined with last-mile development works; the 2015 policy's three fronts (security, development, rights) is the framework.
  • SAMADHAN converts the policy into operational management (leadership, intelligence, technology, finance control, dashboards); the Greyhounds model shows the value of State-level specialist forces.
  • Lasting peace depends on resolving the original grievances (land, forest, displacement) and on accountability in security operations.
  • The post-Sindoor approach widens deterrence by punishment but raises legal, diplomatic and escalation questions.
  • Balanced way forward: consolidate through State police and service delivery; protect tribal rights; keep intelligence and financial pressure on residual networks; use proportionate, lawful counter-terror responses with diplomatic backing.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • CPI(M) (1964) is a parliamentary party; CPI(ML) (1969) was the armed Naxalite party; CPI (Maoist) (2004) came from PWG + MCC only.
  • 31 March 2026 was a target, not the date of a formal gazette notification.
  • Vishwas-Nirman-Jan Kalyan (2026 framing) is not the same as the 2015 National Policy or SAMADHAN (2017).
  • SRE reimburses States; ACALWEMS supports central agencies.
  • DRG and Black Panther are different Chhattisgarh units; CoBRA and Bastariya Battalion are CRPF; Greyhounds are State police.
  • Operation Sunrise is 2019, not 2015; Operation All Clear was carried out by Bhutan.
  • The "new normal" is a stated policy from the PM's address, not a published doctrine document.
