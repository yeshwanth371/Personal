1. Basics of Cyber Security

Cyber security: protection of information and digital systems against unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification or destruction.

IT Act, 2000, section 2(1)(nb), simplified: protecting information, equipment, devices, computers, computer resources, communication devices and information stored in them against those six forms of harm. Sources: IT Act and accessible legal reproduction.

[Correction] The rough definition repeats "access" and omits parts of the statutory list. The corrected paraphrase includes equipment and communication devices; it is not presented as a verbatim quotation. The definition is in the amended IT Act, not necessarily its original 2000 text.

1.1 Four categories: distinguish purpose and legal threshold

Class categories: overlapping in practice, not interchangeable

Honey-trapping: manipulation through an apparent personal or romantic relationship to obtain information. It can support espionage; it becomes cyber-enabled when digital channels are used. 1.3 Cyber terrorism: law and policy

BNS, 2023, section 113: its opening limb covers acts with intent to threaten, or likely to threaten, India's unity, integrity, sovereignty, security or economic security, or intent to strike, or likely to strike, terror among people in India or a foreign country.

[Correction] The rough notes give only the opening limb, not the complete offence. Section 113 also specifies conduct and consequences, including grave harm, disruption of essential supplies/services, coercion through criminal force and hostage-taking. IT Act section 66F, rather than BNS section 113 alone, is the cyber-specific provision. A case requires its statutory elements, not merely a frightening online message. BNS section 113 commenced on 1 July 2024; the accessible secondary reproduction is dated 15 October 2026, while the official PDF could not be extracted. Sources and limitation.

[Additional] Section 66F broadly covers specified unauthorised access, access-denial or contaminant-related conduct with the requisite terror/security intent and serious consequences, and specified unauthorised access to restricted information with the requisite knowledge or reason to believe it could cause the harms listed in the section. This is a study summary, not the complete statutory test. The UAPA, 1967 remains a separate counter-terrorism statute; BNS does not simply replace it. Section 113 provides for an officer not below Superintendent of Police to decide whether registration is under that section or UAPA. See IT Act and section 113 text.

1.4 PRAHAAR: National Counter Terrorism Policy & Strategy

The MHA unveiled PRAHAAR on 23 February 2026, confirmed by a 12 August 2026 Rajya Sabha reply published by PIB. It is a policy and strategy framework, not a new penal statute. Official source.

PRAHAAR: seven pillars, following the official wording
LetterPillar

AAggregating internal capacities for synergy in a whole-of-government approach.
HHuman rights and Rule of Law-based processes for mitigation of threats.
AAttenuating conditions conducive to terrorism, including radicalisation.

[Correction] "Atinating" means attenuating (reducing); "threat post" becomes threat posed. The claim that PRAHAAR was released is verified, but "recently" is replaced by its actual release date.

Why cyberspace can enable terrorist activity: no physical border crossing may be needed; some activities have relatively low entry costs; messages can reach many people quickly; concealed identities complicate attribution.

[Correction] These are enabling factors, not proof that cyber terrorism is becoming more frequent. The notes provide no verified terrorism-specific time series. Low-cost propaganda differs from sophisticated attacks on critical systems, which can require substantial expertise and resources; anonymity is not absolute.

[Additional] Online recruitment or radicalisation may facilitate terrorism but is not interchangeable with a destructive cyber-terrorist attack. Radicalisation is a process of adopting increasingly radical ideas; it does not inevitably lead to violence. Lawful dissent, peaceful protest and unpopular opinions are not automatically security offences.

2.4 International initiatives

  • World Economic Forum: launched the Global Centre for Cybersecurity on 24 January 2018, based in Geneva; its later materials use Centre for Cybersecurity. It convenes public-private cooperation, not a global police authority. WEF's announcement.
  • UN Convention against Cybercrime: [Correction] correct name, not "UN convention for cyber security". Adopted on 24 December 2024; opened for signature in Hanoi on 25 October 2025. Entry into force requires 90 days after the fortieth instrument of ratification, acceptance, approval or accession. The UNODC page consulted still describes entry into force as pending; no claim about India's treaty status is made without checking the depositary. UNODC and treaty-status register.
  • Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001): Council of Europe treaty on criminalisation, procedural powers and international cooperation; open to accession beyond Europe. [Additional] India is not listed among its parties on the Council of Europe page consulted. Treaty and party list.
  • Paris Call for Trust and Security in Cyberspace (12 November 2018): multi-stakeholder principles for secure, stable and peaceful cyberspace. [Additional] A voluntary initiative, not a treaty creating criminal offences. WEF's participation announcement.

[Additional] Exam point: cross-border electronic evidence requires cooperation, but data access must respect privacy, safeguards and jurisdiction. Adoption, signature, ratification and entry into force are different stages.

3. Money Laundering

Concept: processing criminal proceeds to conceal or disguise their illicit origin and make them appear legitimate.

[Correction] "Black money to white money" is shorthand, not the PMLA legal test. Under section 3 read with section 2(1)(u), the offence concerns involvement in processes or activities connected with proceeds of crime linked to a scheduled offence. It includes concealment, possession, acquisition, use, projecting or claiming such property as untainted. It is not limited to the final act of "whitening" money. Tax-evaded income is not automatically PMLA proceeds of crime merely because tax was evaded. PMLA sources.

3.1 Three conceptual stages

  • Placement: proceeds may come from offences such as drug trafficking or fraud. [Correction] Cross-border smuggling of cash may occur but is not a necessary placement step; not every laundering case starts with a vast amount of physical cash.
  • Layering: complex transactions seek to distance proceeds from the underlying crime and obscure the audit trail.
  • Integration: apparent legitimate income or assets conceal continuing criminal origin; appearance does not legalise the proceeds.

Methods named in class: misuse of shell companies to conceal ownership; round-tripping, where funds return through other jurisdictions disguised as investment; and smurfing, splitting transactions into smaller amounts. [Additional] These are risk typologies, not proof of crime by themselves; a company, overseas investment or small transaction is not automatically unlawful. No operational evasion details are provided.

[Additional] Money laundering versus terror financing: laundering concerns criminal proceeds; terror financing concerns the destination or intended use of funds and can involve legally or illegally sourced money.

Maritime security protects lawful activity and interests at sea; coastal security focuses on threats arriving through or operating near the coast, including infiltration and smuggling. [Additional] This links external defence, internal policing, trade, livelihoods and non-traditional threats.

5. Communication Networks and Media

Two distinct roles: communication channels are critical services that may be attacked, and channels that offenders may misuse. Private ownership and imported equipment can create dependency and assurance challenges; they do not by themselves prove insecurity.

Five concerns from class, with proportionate responses [Additional]
ConcernHow communication networks may matterResponse
1. Misinformation / disinformationFalse claims spread quickly and may heighten fear or tension.Verify sources, timely factual communication and media literacy.
2. Cyber threatsAccount compromise, data theft and interruption of essential services.Secure systems, tested continuity plans and incident response.
3. Money launderingDigital platforms may facilitate fraud proceeds and illicit financial networks.Risk-based transaction monitoring and lawful evidence-sharing.
4. Organised crimeNetworks may coordinate fraud, trafficking and other offences.Evidence-led investigations and cross-jurisdiction cooperation.
5. Recruitment into violenceOnline propaganda can facilitate recruitment by violent militant, insurgent, LWE or terrorist groups.Target unlawful conduct; combine prevention, community support and rehabilitation.

[Correction] Media, social-media services and encrypted messaging are not inherently criminal. Misinformation is false or misleading information without necessarily an intent to deceive; disinformation is deliberately deceptive. Intent requires evidence. Terrorism, insurgency, extremism, organised crime and civil protest are not interchangeable labels.

[Additional] Balanced policy: public-private cooperation and security assurance can improve resilience, but surveillance and content controls need a clear legal basis, necessity, proportionality and accountability. Preserve lawful journalism, peaceful dissent, privacy and secure communication. Internal security and external defence can interact through cyber and maritime threats without erasing civilian policing responsibilities.

Key Facts for Prelims

  • IT Act: 2(1)(nb) cyber-security definition; 66F cyber terrorism; 70A CII protection; 70B CERT-In.
  • CERT-In / NCCC / CSK / CSIRT-Fin: technical security and response ecosystem; MHA / I4C / NCRP / 1930: cybercrime coordination and reporting.
  • PRAHAAR: unveiled 23 February 2026; seven pillars include human rights, rule of law, prevention and resilience.
  • PMLA, 2002: Act and Rules commenced 1 July 2005. ED: enforcement/investigation; FIU-IND: financial intelligence; courts decide criminal guilt.
  • FATF: standards and evaluations; APG: Asia/Pacific Group on Money Laundering. Neither is a domestic police force.
  • 12 / 24 / 200 nm: territorial sea / contiguous-zone / EEZ outer limits, all measured from baselines, not successive additions.
  • Coastal Security Scheme Phase I: 2005-2011; IMAC and IFC-IOR: distinct centres at Gurugram.
  • DPDP: 2023 Act, final 2025 Rules and phased implementation; consult the exact commencement notification for current enforceability.

Mains Points

[Additional] Analytical synthesis and suggested way forward, not official conclusions.

  • Cyber resilience: connect awareness, secure procurement, trained investigators and tested recovery. Technical incident counts cannot stand in for convictions or terrorist incidents.
  • Institutional clarity: connect CERT-In response, NCIIPC CII protection and I4C-police investigation without giving each the other's powers. Support State capacity through Union coordination.
  • Terror prevention: PRAHAAR's whole-of-government capacity must work alongside its human-rights and whole-of-society pillars; community trust and rehabilitation complement enforcement.
  • Illicit finance: trace criminal proceeds and beneficial ownership while protecting legitimate transactions and financial inclusion. Terror financing can involve lawful-source funds too.
  • Maritime security: combine lawful community reporting, shared awareness, inter-agency coordination and maintained response capacity. Zones define rights, not exclusive force jurisdictions.
  • Rights-security balance: targeted evidence access and oversight are more defensible than treating all encrypted communication or dissent as a threat. Resilient networks support both national security and daily livelihoods.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Cybercrime is not automatically cyber terrorism; cross-border origin is not proof of State responsibility or cyber warfare.
  • Hacking is not always criminal: authorisation and conduct matter. Espionage need not include a public leak.
  • NCCC is not I4C; CERT-In is not a police station; FIU-IND is not ED.
  • CSIRT-Fin is the verified current finance-response name in the source opened; do not substitute an old proposal.
  • Undisclosed income is not automatically PMLA proceeds; laundering does not legally require all three textbook stages.
  • Territorial sovereignty differs from EEZ sovereign rights; 24 nm and 200 nm are not measured from the 12-nm line.
  • IFC-IOR differs from IMAC; NC3I, not N3CI. Information-sharing is not a fixed command hierarchy.
  • Policy is not statute; enactment is not commencement; treaty signature is not ratification or entry into force.
  • Lawful dissent is not extremism or terrorism; public order is not identical to every law-and-order issue.

30-Second Revision

Protect: confidentiality, integrity, availability. Classify: crime / terrorism / espionage / warfare. Respond: CERT-In technically, I4C with police for crime, NCIIPC for CII. PRAHAAR: prevention through rights-based resilience. Follow proceeds: placement, layering, integration; PMLA-ED-FIU; FATF-APG standards. At sea: 12-24-200 from baselines; overlapping roles, shared awareness. Across all: verify attribution, commencement and evidence; preserve constitutional safeguards.
