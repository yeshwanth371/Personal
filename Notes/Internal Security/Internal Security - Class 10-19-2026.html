1. North-East at a Glance

Nagaland and Assam produced the largest and longest insurgencies of the region; Mizoram (1986), Tripura (2015 onward) and Meghalaya (2018 onward) are the examples most often quoted as peace consolidated. Roughly: demand for sovereignty or homeland → violence → ceasefire or accord → splinter group → new talks. This repeating pattern is the hydra-head tendency (Section 2.4).

2. Nagaland

Nagaland is the oldest and most complex insurgency of the North-East: it began as a demand for an independent Naga State in 1947, shifted to a demand for Nagalim (a Naga homeland across State and international borders), and now continues as a negotiation over political status, a separate flag and constitution, and territory.

2.1 Naga Identity and Early Organisation

  • The Nagas are not one tribe but a group of ethnicities and tribes, for example Ao, Angami, Sumi (Sema), Lotha, Rengma, Konyak, with distinct dialects and village-based governance.
  • Naga Club (1918): formed by educated Nagas and returnees from the First World War Labour Corps to protect Naga identity and culture. In 1929 it submitted a memorandum to the Simon Commission asking that Nagas be left alone [Additional].
  • Naga National Council (NNC), 1946: the Naga Club and the Naga Hills District Tribal Council were merged into a political body. It was first led by T. Sakhrie and Imti Aliba (Ao) and later by Angami Zapu Phizo, who radicalised it toward independence [Additional; Verification Required: early leadership].
  • Phizo became NNC president in 1950, went underground in 1956 and formed a parallel Naga Federal Government, later living in exile in London [Additional; Verification Required].

[Correction] (1) The agreement is the Hydari Agreement (after Sir Akbar Hydari, Governor of Assam) of 29 June 1947, also called the Nine-Point Agreement; the rough notes spell it "Hyadri". The talks were between the NNC and the Government (Governor of Assam on behalf of the Union); the notes call them "tripartite", which is not how the sources describe the 1947 talks [Verification Required]. (2) "NNC broke the agreement" is one side's reading. The ninth clause made the Governor the Nagas' guardian for ten years, after which the NNC could decide the future; the NNC understood this as a right to choose independence, while the Indian side did not accept that reading. Treat the dispute as one of interpretation. (3) Phizo is better described as the father of Naga nationalism and the independence movement; Nagalim was articulated mainly by the later NSCN. (4) Independence was declared on 14 August 1947, a day before India's independence.

2.2 Timeline of the Naga Question

  • Naga Hills-Tuensang Area (1957) and the 16-Point Agreement (1960): the Naga Hills (Assam) and Tuensang (NEFA) were first administered together as a Centre-run area. In July 1960 the Naga People's Convention and the Union Government agreed on a State; the NNC rejected it [Additional].
  • 1963: Nagaland became the 16th State through the State of Nagaland Act, 1962 and the 13th Constitution Amendment, which inserted Article 371A (special protection to Naga customary law, religious and social practices, ownership of land and resources) [Additional].
  • Shillong Accord, 11 November 1975: signed by the Governor of Nagaland (for the Union) and representatives of the underground NNC and Naga Federal Government. They agreed to accept the Indian Constitution and surrender arms. Many underground leaders rejected it as a betrayal; this rejection led directly to the NSCN.

[Correction] The rough notes say "1958 - AFSPA was imposed in Assam". More exactly, the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 was enacted by Parliament for Assam and Manipur (the Naga Hills were then part of Assam) and later extended to other North-Eastern States by amendment (1972). It does not apply automatically: it applies only in an area declared "disturbed" by notification. An Ordinance preceded the Act in 1958 [Verification Required].

2.3 The Demand: Nagalim (Greater Nagaland)

  • Nagalim means the integration of all contiguous Naga-inhabited areas under one administrative arrangement: parts of Assam, Manipur and Arunachal Pradesh and parts of Myanmar, in addition to present Nagaland.
  • The notes list "Nagaland" among the areas; the claim covers present Nagaland plus additional Naga areas in the neighbouring States and Myanmar.
  • Why it is hard to settle: Assam, Manipur and Arunachal Pradesh have repeatedly opposed any change to their territory (Manipur's territorial integrity is a politically sensitive issue); part of the claimed area lies in Myanmar and cannot be settled by India alone.

2.4 NSCN and the Hydra-Head Tendency

  • NSCN (1980): the National Socialist Council of Nagaland was formed on 31 January 1980 by Isak Chishi Swu, Thuingaleng Muivah and S.S. Khaplang (the rough notes write "T. Muviah"). It rejected the Shillong Accord and revived the demand for a sovereign Nagalim, mixing Naga nationalism with a socialist and (as the notes and sources say) a Christian identity [Verification Required: ideology].
  • 1988: the NSCN split into NSCN (IM) (Isak-Muivah, mainly Tangkhul and Sumi support) and NSCN (K) (Khaplang, a Hemi Naga from Myanmar) after leadership and ethnic disputes, followed by violent rivalry between the factions [Additional].
  • Ceasefires: NSCN (IM) in 1997 (1 August); NSCN (K) in 2001. The NSCN (K) ended its ceasefire in March 2015 and was later declared an unlawful association under the UAPA [Additional; Verification Required: dates].
Hydra-head tendency (definition): when a group signs a ceasefire or accord, dissatisfied members break away, form a new armed group and continue fighting, so that a settlement with one group does not end the conflict. Why it happens: leadership and ethnic rivalries, unequal benefits from accords, extortion income and an unresolved core demand. Policy lesson: settle the core political demand and implement the package with rehabilitation; do not rely only on talks with a single leader.

[Correction] (1) The 1997 and 2001 agreements with the NSCN (IM) and NSCN (K) are ceasefire agreements, not final "peace agreements". (2) "NSC" in the rough notes is read as NSCN. (3) The Council's name is National Socialist Council of Nagaland; the IM faction itself uses "Nagalim" in its name. (4) Faction names and years of the splinters are from secondary reports and not from MHA documents [Verification Required].

2.5 The Peace Process: Framework Agreement (2015) and After

  • Framework Agreement, 3 August 2015: signed by the Government of India's interlocutor and NSCN (IM) in the presence of the Prime Minister. It is a framework for a final settlement, not the settlement.
  • Agreed Position, 17 November 2017: reached with the NNPG working committee [Verification Required].
  • Sticking points: NSCN (IM) demands a separate Naga flag and constitution and the integration of Naga areas; the Government has described the Framework Agreement as being within the Constitution and has not accepted a separate flag and constitution (as reported).

[Verification Required: reported only by news and commentary sources; treat the NSCN (IM) split as unconfirmed until an official statement is available].

[Correction] The rough notes say "in 2015, to address insurgency Naga peace accord was formed". The 2015 document is the Framework Agreement (3 August 2015) between the Government and NSCN (IM). It is not a final peace accord and the Naga political issue remains unresolved as of October 2026.

2.6 AFSPA in Nagaland

  • Meaning: the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 gives armed forces in a notified "disturbed area" special powers: to fire after due warning, to arrest without warrant and to search without warrant. Section 6 bars prosecution of personnel without the prior sanction of the Central Government.
  • Who declares: the Governor or Administrator of the State or Union Territory, or the Central Government, under Section 3. In Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh and Manipur the notification is renewed by the MHA every six months; in Assam the State Government notifies it (reported) [Verification Required].
  • Current position: reported news says the MHA extended AFSPA in parts of Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh and Manipur from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, covering nine districts and 21 police-station areas in Nagaland. In Assam, disturbed-area status was reported to be in force in three districts (Tinsukia, Charaideo, Sivasagar) [Verification Required: check the latest MHA notification and the Assam notification].
  • Judicial position: in Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India (27 November 1997) the Supreme Court upheld the Act's constitutional validity but required safeguards, such as using the minimum force necessary and handing over arrested persons promptly to the police [Additional]. In Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association v. Union of India (2016) the Court held that even in disturbed areas excessive or retaliatory force is not protected and that allegations are open to investigation [Additional; Verification Required: date and wording].
  • Reform view: the Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy Committee (2005) recommended repeal and the incorporation of necessary provisions in ordinary law; the Second ARC made a similar suggestion [Additional; Verification Required].

Case study: Oting (Mon district), 4 December 2021

  • Context: a security operation based on intelligence about militants.
  • Event: soldiers of an Army special-forces unit fired on a vehicle carrying coal-mine workers, killing six civilians; violence the same day left more civilians and one soldier dead. Reports say 13 or 14 civilians in all [Verification Required: exact total].
  • Response: the Nagaland Government's Special Investigation Team recommended prosecution of 30 Army personnel; the Centre refused sanction under AFSPA Section 6; in 2024 the Supreme Court closed the criminal proceedings for want of sanction, while noting that sanction could still be granted and that the Army could take disciplinary action (as reported) [Verification Required].
  • Lessons: the Section 6 sanction requirement and intelligence-led operations need better accountability; the incident intensified Naga demands for AFSPA repeal. Source: news reports (not official documents).

2.7 Ceasefire Mechanism and the NSCN (K)

  • [Additional] Ceasefire with NSCN (IM): in force since 1997 and extended periodically under agreed ground rules (for example, no new recruitment, no extortion or attacks, designated camps). It is overseen by a Cease-Fire Monitoring Group (CFMG) [Verification Required: composition and current extension].
  • Common confusion: the Joint Monitoring Group (JMG) belongs to the Suspension of Operations (SoO) agreement (2008) with Kuki groups in Manipur, not to the NSCN (IM) ceasefire. "Ceasefire" (with NSCN) and "Suspension of Operations" (with Kuki groups, ULFA pro-talks, etc.) are different instruments [Verification Required].
  • NSCN (K) and the 2015 break: NSCN (K) ended its ceasefire in March 2015. On 4 June 2015 an ambush on an Army convoy in Chandel, Manipur killed 18 soldiers; NSCN (K) and allied Manipur groups claimed it. India announced a cross-border operation in Myanmar on about 9 June 2015 (casualty claims were disputed and Myanmar's position was contested). On 16 September 2015 NSCN (K) was declared an unlawful association for five years under the UAPA, 1967, and in November 2015 it was reported to have been designated a terrorist organisation (Schedule to the UAPA) [Verification Required: gazette dates; later renewals].
  • Lesson: a ceasefire without a political settlement is fragile; groups sheltering across the Myanmar border can end a truce and strike. This links to the Myanmar border management in Section 7.

[Correction] The rough notes treat the 2001 NSCN (K) agreement as a settled peace; it ended in March 2015 and the group was banned the same year (see above). Only the NSCN (IM) ceasefire from 1997 has continued.

2.8 Eastern Nagaland: ENPO and the Frontier Nagaland Territorial Authority

  • [Additional] The Eastern Nagaland People's Organisation (ENPO) speaks for about eight tribes of six eastern districts (Mon, Tuensang, Longleng, Kiphire, Noklak, Shamator). It demanded a separate Frontier Nagaland State or territory because of perceived neglect, then accepted an autonomy arrangement within Nagaland.
  • Agreement: a tripartite agreement (Government of India, Government of Nagaland, ENPO) to create the Frontier Nagaland Territorial Authority (FNTA) was reported as signed in February 2026, with executive, legislative and financial powers over about 46 subjects and Central funding for set-up [Verification Required: date, legal basis (whether under the Sixth Schedule or a State law), and number of subjects; reported by news sources only].
  • Why it matters: a new sub-State demand inside Nagaland shows that settlement with one group does not end identity claims; it also complicates a Nagalim settlement.

2.9 Civil Society and Mediation

  • [Additional] The Naga Hoho (apex tribal body), Naga Students' Federation (NSF), Naga Mothers' Association (NMA) and the Baptist churches have repeatedly pressed for talks, ceasefire and reconciliation among factions. The 1964 Peace Mission (Jayaprakash Narayan, Rev. Michael Scott and B.P. Chaliha) produced a ceasefire that lasted until 1967 [Verification Required].
  • Lesson: in Nagaland, civil society, churches and village bodies can influence armed groups more than the State can; any settlement needs their consent.

3. Assam

Assam combines three overlapping problems: a migration-identity issue (Assam Movement and Accord), sovereignty-oriented insurgency (ULFA) and sub-State ethnic homeland demands (Bodo, Karbi, Dimasa, Kamtapuri and others).

3.1 Illegal Migration and the Assam Movement

  • Background: migration from East Bengal and later East Pakistan and Bangladesh, increased by the 1971 war and its aftermath.
  • Perception (1970s): that the demography of Assam was shifting in favour of illegal migrants and that they were being added to the electoral rolls (the voter-list concern, strongly raised after the 1978 Mangaldoi by-election) [Additional; Verification Required]. Note that this was a perception and a political claim; the status of individual persons is decided by legal process.
  • AASU (All Assam Students' Union): led a mass movement from 1979 demanding detection, deletion and deportation of illegal foreigners. It began as peaceful protest but was handled firmly and became violent in places; one result was the diversion of some youth toward armed groups such as ULFA.

3.2 Assam Accord, 15 August 1985

A Memorandum of Settlement signed by the Government of India, the Government of Assam, AASU and the All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad (AAGSP) (the rough notes list only AASU, the Centre and the State) in the presence of Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi.

  • Section 6A, Citizenship Act, 1955: inserted in 1985 to give effect to the Accord. On 17 October 2024 a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court upheld its validity by a 4:1 majority (as reported), holding that the 25 March 1971 cut-off was not arbitrary [Verification Required: bench split and wording].
  • Other commitments: constitutional, legislative and administrative safeguards to protect the cultural, social and linguistic identity of the Assamese people; economic development, including a Numaligarh Refinery (commissioned 1999) and IIT Guwahati (established 1994) [Additional; Verification Required: dates].
  • NRC (National Register of Citizens) update: a register first prepared after the 1951 Census; updated in Assam under the Supreme Court's supervision using the 24 March 1971 cut-off. The final list of 31 August 2019 left out about 19 lakh applicants out of about 3.3 crore [Additional; Verification Required: exact figures].

[Correction] (1) The rough notes list "NRC, IIT Guwahati, Numaligarh Refinery" as what the Accord agreed to. The Accord provided for detection, deletion and deportation by cut-off dates and for development projects; the NRC update itself was done much later, under Supreme Court orders. (2) The group between 1966 and 1971 is not "citizens without the right to vote" permanently; their names are removed from the electoral roll for ten years, after which they get full rights. (3) The cut-off for foreigners is "on or after 25 March 1971" (that is, entry after 24 March 1971). (4) The Accord was signed by four parties, not three.

3.3 ULFA (United Liberation Front of Asom)

  • Formation: 7 April 1979 at Rang Ghar, Sivasagar, by Paresh Baruah, Arabinda Rajkhowa, Anup Chetia and others [Additional; Verification Required]. The rough notes say it was formed "by Arbinda Raj Khowa" alone.
  • Aim: a sovereign Assam by armed struggle, invoking the glory of the Ahom kingdom before the Treaty of Yandabo (1826), which ended the first Anglo-Burmese War and brought Assam under British control.
  • Response: the group was banned in 1990; large army operations followed (Operation Bajrang, 1990; Operation Rhino, 1991) [Additional; Verification Required].
  • Split: the leadership under Rajkhowa moved toward talks (pro-talks faction) and Paresh Baruah, who did not accept the talks, led ULFA (Independent), or ULFA (I). A group of cadres who surrendered earlier are called S-ULFA (Surrendered ULFA); the claim in the rough notes that they returned to violence after their surrender package ended is not sourced [Verification Required].
  • 2023 settlement: a tripartite agreement on 29 December 2023 between the pro-talks ULFA faction, the Government of India and the Government of Assam. The faction agreed to give up violence, surrender arms, disband and join the mainstream. Reports say the Centre announced a development package of about Rs 1.5 lakh crore for Assam over several years (investment, not a payment to the group) and that political and land-rights safeguards for indigenous people were included.

[Correction] (1) Rajkhowa was arrested in December 2009 (handed over to Indian authorities after his arrest in Bangladesh) and was released on bail in about 2011; talks and a Suspension of Operations arrangement followed. The rough notes say "2011 arrested". [Verification Required: dates] (2) The rough notes say "97 out of 126 seats in the Assam Legislative Assembly are reserved for indigenous communities". The Constitution provides reservation only for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Articles 330 and 332). The reports describe a "protection of political rights of indigenous people" measure, connected with delimitation, not a constitutional reservation; the exact mechanism is [Verification Required]. (3) The "Rs 1.5 lakh crore" is a Government announcement, not an amount given to the group. (4) ULFA (I) did not sign and remains banned under the UAPA [Verification Required: latest notification]. (5) The spelling is "Arabinda Rajkhowa" and "Paresh Baruah".

3.4 Bodoland

  • Who: the Bodos are the largest plains tribal community of Assam. They demanded a separate State, then a territorial autonomy arrangement within Assam, citing migration, loss of land, and neglect.
  • Movement: started as a student movement under the All Bodo Students' Union (ABSU) (1987 "divide Assam fifty-fifty" campaign) and developed into armed groups: Bodo Liberation Tigers (BLT) and the National Democratic Front of Bodoland (NDFB) [Additional; Verification Required: 1987 campaign].
  • Settlements: the BLT signed the 2003 Memorandum that created the Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC) under the Sixth Schedule; the NDFB (progressive) entered ceasefire in 2005; and all factions joined the Bodo Accord of 27 January 2020.
  • Bodo Accord, 2020 (reported provisions): BTAD renamed Bodoland Territorial Region (BTR); BTC seats raised from 40 to 60; more powers for the BTC; a commission to examine adding contiguous Bodo villages and excluding non-Bodo villages; a package of Rs 1,500 crore; rehabilitation of about 1,500 NDFB cadres; Bodo in Devanagari as an associate official language of Assam; a Bodo-Kachari Welfare Council for Bodo villages outside BTR [Verification Required: details].
  • Districts of the BTR: Kokrajhar, Baksa, Chirang, Udalguri and Tamulpur (the last created in 2022 out of Baksa) [Verification Required: year]. These areas fall under the Sixth Schedule.
  • Language: Bodo was added to the Eighth Schedule by the 92nd Constitution Amendment Act, 2003 (along with Dogri, Maithili and Santhali).

[Correction] (1) "BLTF" is the Bodo Liberation Tigers (BLT); "NDLB" is the National Democratic Front of Bodoland (NDFB). (2) "Chirong" is Chirang. (3) The notes say the ceasefire years "2003 and 2005"; 2003 was a settlement with the BLT and 2005 a ceasefire with an NDFB faction, while other NDFB factions continued until 2020. (4) The Accord made Bodo (Devanagari) an associate official language, not the sole official language; Bodo's Eighth Schedule status is from 2003, not from the 2020 Accord. (5) The BTR is a territorial council under the Sixth Schedule, not a separate State.

3.5 Dima Hasao (Dimasa) Peace Pact, April 2023

  • Parties: Government of India, Government of Assam and the Dimasa National Liberation Army (DNLA), a group formed in about 2019 that demanded a separate Dimasa homeland [Additional; Verification Required]. Signed on 27 April 2023 (news reports).
  • Provisions: a development package of Rs 500 crore each from the Centre and Assam; a Dimasa Welfare Council for Dimasas living outside the North Cachar Hills Autonomous Council (NCHAC); a review by a commission (Sixth Schedule, paragraph 14) of the inclusion of contiguous villages in NCHAC; rehabilitation of cadres.
  • DNLA's commitments: abjure violence, surrender arms, disband its organisation and join the mainstream.
  • Link: Dima Hasao is under the Sixth Schedule (NCHAC), so the pact works through that Council and a separate welfare body.

3.6 Other Demands in Assam

  • Karbi Anglong: Karbi groups sought autonomy (up to a separate State) for the Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council area. A tripartite Karbi Anglong Agreement was signed on 4 September 2021 (reported package: about Rs 1,000 crore; about 1,000 cadres) [Verification Required].
  • Kamtapur: a demand for a Kamtapur State for the Koch-Rajbongshi people of North Bengal, lower Assam and neighbouring areas, pursued by the Kamtapur Liberation Organisation (KLO) and others [Verification Required].
  • Adivasi groups (tea-garden communities): an agreement with several groups was reported in September 2022 [Additional; Verification Required].

[Correction] "Karbi Anglo autonomy" is Karbi Anglong; "Kantapur" is Kamtapur.

3.8 Clause 6 of the Accord and the Biplab Sarma Committee

  • Clause 6 promises constitutional, legislative and administrative safeguards to protect the cultural, social and linguistic identity and heritage of the Assamese people.
  • [Additional] A high-level committee headed by Justice (retd.) Biplab Kumar Sarma was set up in 2019 and reportedly submitted its report in 2020. It was reported to propose a definition of "Assamese people" and 80-100% reservation of Assembly and local-body seats for them, plus job and land safeguards. The report was not fully made public and its implementation status is unclear [Verification Required].
  • Link to the 2023 ULFA pact: the reported "97 of 126 seats" is best read as part of the political-safeguard debate (see correction C6), not a constitutional reservation.

4. Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura

4.1 Arunachal Pradesh

  • The rough notes say "No issues". This is only partly true: the three districts of Tirap, Changlang and Longding (TCL), bordering Nagaland and Myanmar, have seen activity by NSCN factions and ULFA (I), extortion and abductions, and AFSPA continues in them and in a few police-station areas of Namsai (reported; Verification Required).

[Correction] Arunachal Pradesh is among the most peaceful North-Eastern States but is not free of insurgency-linked activity; it should be described as limited and localised to the TCL belt. Border and Chinese-claim issues are a separate, external-security matter.

4.2 Meghalaya

  • "Peace since 2018": AFSPA was fully withdrawn in April 2018 after improvement in the situation. Earlier armed groups were the Garo-based GNLA and ANVC and the Khasi-based HNLC; the ANVC and its faction settled in 2014 and the GNLA weakened after 2012 [Additional; Verification Required]. Some residual groups remain.

4.3 Mizoram

  • Cause: the 1959 famine (bamboo flowering and the "Mautam" famine) and perceived neglect led to the Mizo National Front (MNF) under Laldenga; an armed uprising began in 1966 [Additional].
  • Mizo Accord, 30 June 1986: MNF gave up arms; Mizoram became a full State on 20 February 1987; Article 371G protects Mizo customary law, religious practices and land. This is the model of a political settlement that held: a recognised leader, a clear demand and rapid statehood.
  • Bru-Reang displacement: an issue after 1997 ethnic violence; a quadripartite agreement was signed on 16 January 2020 to settle displaced Brus in Tripura [Additional; Verification Required].

4.4 Tripura

  • "Peace since 2015": AFSPA was withdrawn from Tripura in May 2015 after violence fell sharply. Militancy arose from tribal fears over migration (tribal groups became a minority after 1947) and land.
  • Tripura peace pact, 4 September 2024: a tripartite agreement between the Government of India, Government of Tripura, the National Liberation Front of Tripura (NLFT) and the All Tripura Tiger Force (ATTF) in New Delhi. Reported terms: surrender and disbanding by about 328 cadres (a later total of about 584 is reported), a special package of about Rs 250 crore for tribal development and rehabilitation [Verification Required: PIB release 2051900 not opened].
  • Implementation: news in September 2026 reported a shutdown by returned cadres over delays in rehabilitation and withdrawal of cases [Verification Required] - a reminder that accords fail slowly if implementation lags.

[Correction] "Peace since" years for Meghalaya (2018) and Tripura (2015) correspond to the withdrawal of AFSPA, a government indicator, not to the end of every armed group. Mizoram's date (1986) is the Mizo Accord.

4.5 Additional State-Level Issues

  • Tripura - Tipra Motha accord (2 March 2024): a tripartite agreement between the Government of India, Government of Tripura and TIPRA (Tipra Motha) on the history, land, political rights, identity and language of indigenous people, to be implemented by a joint working group and giving the Tripura Tribal Areas Autonomous District Council (TTAADC) more administrative and financial powers (reported). It followed a "Greater Tipraland" demand for a separate State or arrangement. Phased implementation was reported to have begun by September 2026 [Verification Required]. Do not confuse with the 2024 NLFT-ATTF pact: that pact was with armed groups; this one is with a political party.
  • Meghalaya: a demand for the Inner Line Permit (after CAA protests and border incidents); the State Assembly has passed resolutions but the Centre has not extended the ILP [Verification Required]. Border dispute with Assam: see Section 3.9.
  • Mizoram: border dispute with Assam (2021 clash) and the influx of refugees from Myanmar and Bangladesh's Chittagong Hill Tracts after 2021 raise humanitarian and security questions [Verification Required].
  • Arunachal Pradesh: boundary with Assam (2023 agreement reported); the Chinese claim over parts of the State is an external-defence issue and is separate from insurgency.

5. Peace Accords of the North-East: Timeline

Common pattern: accords succeed when they (1) address a core political demand (autonomy, language, land), (2) bring all major factions, (3) include a development package, rehabilitation and withdrawal of non-heinous cases, and (4) are implemented on time. They fail or lead to splinter groups when any of these is missing.

7.2 The India-Myanmar Border and the Free Movement Regime (FMR)

  • The border is about 1,643 km and crosses Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Manipur and Mizoram. The FMR allowed people living within about 16 km on each side (border-tribes) to cross without a visa for short stays; it reflects kinship ties split by a colonial boundary.
  • 2024 decision: in February 2024 the Union Home Minister announced that the FMR would be ended and the border fenced (reported cost about Rs 31,000 crore, plus a patrol track and surveillance); a revised, stricter border-pass arrangement was reported later [Verification Required: current rules and distance].
  • Why: arms and drug flows, insurgent sanctuaries, illegal migration and refugees after the 2021 coup in Myanmar, and the ethnic violence in Manipur.
  • Objections: Nagaland, Mizoram and tribal bodies argue that it divides kin, damages border trade and local livelihoods, and ignores colonial boundary-drawing; the fence cannot replace cooperation with Myanmar's authorities and local communities.
  • [Additional] The India-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement (2015) resolved the enclaves issue and demarcation along parts of the Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram borders [Verification Required].

8. Trends in North-East Violence

The MHA and Parliament replies report a sharp decline in insurgency incidents, civilian deaths and security-force deaths since 2014, and a shrinking area under AFSPA. Reported ranges: incidents down roughly 70-80%, civilian deaths down roughly 80-90% and security-force deaths down roughly 60-90% compared with 2014, depending on the year and source compared.

9. Analysis: Gaps and Way Forward

  • Nagaland: move from a framework to a final settlement that accommodates the NNPGs and NSCN (IM) and respects the territorial concerns of Assam, Manipur and Arunachal Pradesh; settle the flag and constitution issue within the constitutional framework (Article 371A and other special provisions are one route); keep talks inclusive of civil society, churches and tribal bodies; manage sub-State demands such as Eastern Nagaland through autonomy arrangements without weakening the main talks.
  • Federal cooperation: settle inter-State boundaries through negotiated agreements and, where needed, Parliament (Article 3) or the Supreme Court (Article 131); build joint police mechanisms along disputed lines.
  • Border management: combine fencing and surveillance with community consultation, trade and cross-border cooperation with Myanmar; resolve the Assam Rifles' dual control so that command is clear.
  • Assam: implement Clause 6 of the Accord (safeguards for the Assamese people) fairly; finalise the citizenship process under law with due process and appeals; avoid treating whole communities as suspect.
  • Accords: create a monitoring mechanism with time-bound reports; deliver rehabilitation and withdraw cases promptly; avoid a "reward for violence" perception by rewarding development for all.
  • AFSPA: use a graded withdrawal tied to objective indicators (as done in Tripura, Meghalaya and most of Assam), strengthen the accountability of the forces and investigate allegations promptly.
  • External dimension: sanctuaries and arms and drug flows across the Myanmar border make cooperation with Myanmar, Bangladesh and Bhutan essential (see the 18 October note, Section 6).

Mains Points

  • North-East insurgencies are political before they are military: they began as identity, autonomy, migration and land disputes; security operations contained violence but only political accords (Mizoram, Bodo, Karbi, Tripura) reduced it.
  • Hydra-head tendency shows the limits of talks with single groups: splinters appear when benefits are unequal or the core demand remains open; inclusive negotiations and credible implementation reduce this risk.
  • Nagaland is the test case: the 2015 Framework Agreement shows progress but a final settlement needs to reconcile Nagalim with other States' territorial integrity and the flag and constitution demands with the Constitution.
  • Assam shows that migration-identity questions must be handled through law and fairness: Section 6A and the NRC process show both the strength of a legal route and the human cost of exclusion and statelessness; community-wide stereotyping is both wrong and counter-productive.
  • AFSPA: a time-bound, area-specific and accountable use is consistent with the Supreme Court's position, but the Oting case shows that Section 6 sanction and a lack of accountability damage trust.
  • Federalism and borders: boundary disputes (Assam-Mizoram 2021, Assam-Meghalaya 2022) and Myanmar border fencing show that internal security in the North-East is also a Centre-State and community-consent issue; Articles 3 and 131, Sixth Schedule councils and Article 371 provisions are the tools.
  • Balanced way forward: complete and monitor accords; use Sixth Schedule councils and Article 371 provisions with financial and administrative capacity; invest in connectivity and Act East links; strengthen border and financial controls against drug and arms flows; maintain human rights safeguards.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Hydari Agreement (1947) is not the Shillong Accord (1975) or the Framework Agreement (2015).
  • NNC (Phizo) is not the NSCN; NSCN (IM) and NSCN (K) both have ceasefire histories but only the IM has the 2015 Framework Agreement.
  • The 1997 and 2001 documents are ceasefires, not peace accords; 2015 is a framework, not a settlement.
  • AFSPA 1958 was not "imposed in Assam only"; it applies only where an area is notified as disturbed.
  • Assam Accord cut-offs: 1 Jan 1966 and 25 March 1971; Section 6A is in the Citizenship Act, not in the Constitution; NRC is not the Accord itself.
  • BLT (2003) versus NDFB (2005 and 2020); BTAD renamed BTR; Tamulpur is the fifth district.
  • Constitution reserves seats only for SC and ST; "97 of 126 seats for indigenous people" is not a reservation.
  • "Peace since 2015 / 2018" means AFSPA withdrawn (Tripura, Meghalaya), while Mizoram's peace dates from the 1986 Accord.
  • CFMG (NSCN-IM ceasefire) is not the JMG (Kuki SoO). NSCN (K)'s 2001 ceasefire ended in 2015 and the group is banned under the UAPA.
  • Sixth Schedule (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram) versus Article 371A (Nagaland) versus 371C (Manipur hills).
  • The Tipra Motha accord (2024) is with a political party; the NLFT-ATTF pact (2024) is with armed groups.
  • Assam Rifles: administrative control MHA, operational control Army; it guards the Myanmar border while the BSF guards the Bangladesh border.
