1. Salwa Judum: Formation, Requirement, Issues and End

Salwa Judum ("peace march" in Gondi) was a State-sponsored, anti-Maoist mobilisation of local tribal villagers in southern Chhattisgarh (Bijapur and Dantewada, in the Bastar region) from 2005. Villagers were organised into armed groups of Special Police Officers (SPOs), also called Koya Commandos. The Supreme Court declared this arrangement unconstitutional on 5 July 2011 (Nandini Sundar v. State of Chhattisgarh).

1.1 Formation

  • Background: Bastar and Dantewada were sparsely populated, mineral-rich and among the poorest tribal regions. The Maoists had gained influence by mobilising around poor governance, land and forest rights and livelihoods, and by force.
  • Precursor: the Jan Jagran Abhiyan (about 1990-91), an earlier anti-Maoist movement associated with Mahendra Karma, collapsed and its leaders needed police protection [Verification Required: year, from a secondary source].
  • 2005: the movement was revived as Salwa Judum with State support. Mahendra Karma, then a Congress MLA and Leader of the Opposition, was its public face; the State government gave it police backing, and it later drew support across party lines (a secondary-source summary) [Verification Required].
  • Legal route for arming villagers: SPOs were appointed under the Chhattisgarh Police Act, 2007, which gave the Superintendent of Police wide discretion. Under the Police Act, 1861, a magistrate's approval is needed to appoint special police officers. SPOs got firearms for self-defence and an honorarium of about Rs 3,000 per month.
  • Union role: the Centre said its role was limited to approving the maximum number of SPOs per State for reimbursement of the honorarium under the SRE scheme; appointment, training and deployment were State matters.

1.2 Requirement (Why the State Said It Was Needed)

  • Intelligence and local knowledge gap: outsiders in the forces did not know the terrain, dialects or the Maoists' local supporters; SPOs worked as guides, translators, informants and spotters.
  • Manpower shortage: regular forces found it hard to operate in remote areas.
  • Protection of relief camps and villagers who had resisted the Maoists; preference was given to victims of Maoist violence.
  • Livelihood argument: the State said it gave jobs where employment was scarce. The Court rejected this (Section 1.4).
  • Context of violence: official figures cited by the Indian Express put deaths in Bastar between 2005 and 2011 at about 1,019 villagers, 726 security personnel and 422 Maoists.

1.4 Conclusion: How It Came to an End

  • Petition (2007): sociologist Nandini Sundar, historian Ramachandra Guha and former IAS officer E.A.S. Sarma challenged the practice; both the Union (then Congress-led) and the BJP-led State government defended it.
  • Judgment (5 July 2011): a Bench headed by Justice B. Sudershan Reddy held that arming and deploying SPOs against Maoists violated Article 14 (equality: poorly trained, poorly paid SPOs were made to do the work of regular police) and Article 21 (life and dignity: exposing them to lethal risk without training or protection). The Court said the State could not treat citizens as "expendable instruments" and that placing vulnerable youth in danger was not "livelihood".
  • Directions (as widely reported): stop recruiting SPOs for counter-insurgency, recall their firearms, stop Union and State funding for such use, and investigate alleged crimes; counter-Maoist operations must be by trained police or paramilitary forces.

1.5 Transformation After 2011

  • Chhattisgarh enacted the Chhattisgarh Auxiliary Armed Police Force Act, 2011 to create a formal, regulated auxiliary force that took in former SPOs. Petitioners said it only rebranded the banned scheme. In May 2025 the Supreme Court is reported to have dismissed a contempt petition, holding that enacting a law is not contempt unless the law itself is struck down; the Act's validity needs a separate challenge [Verification Required: secondary reports only].
  • Local recruits were later organised into regular police units such as the District Reserve Guard (DRG) and the CRPF's Bastariya Battalion (see the 17 October note, Section 5.2); critics say some former SPOs and surrendered cadres were absorbed, and supporters say these units are properly trained and legally constituted [Verification Required].

[Correction] The 16 October note says the Court "ordered ... an end to State funding for such groups" and struck down "Salwa Judum". More exactly, the Court held the use of SPOs for counter-insurgency unconstitutional and ordered it stopped; Salwa Judum is the name of the movement that relied on it. The "State" and the "Union" were both directed.

Exam points: (1) Policing is a State subject, but the State cannot outsource core security tasks to untrained civilians. (2) Counter-insurgency must stay within Articles 14 and 21. (3) Local knowledge is valuable, so use it through regular, trained, accountable forces (DRG, Greyhounds-type units), not militias. (4) Linked cause: weak trust and development deficit, which is why the development front below matters.

Why development and security must move together (cause-impact-response): poor access and services → State absent → Maoists fill the vacuum (taxation, "people's courts") → operations without services deepen distrust (the Salwa Judum case) → response: roads, towers and schools after forces secure an area, and local, accountable administration, so that gains last.

[Correction] (1) Terrorism has a goal; what marks it out is the method (violence against civilians to spread terror) and, in law, the definition in the UAPA, 1967 (Section 15) and BNS, 2023 (Section 113) [Verification Required: BNS section number]. (2) "Militancy" has no fixed legal meaning; it is a descriptive term. The rough notes give it the same demand as insurgency, and that is only a usage convention. (3) "Insurgency generally does not attack civilians" and "LWE attacks all" are both too broad; insurgents have killed civilians and LWE has largely targeted forces, officials and alleged informers. (4) "Parallel government" is not limited to LWE; the NSCN (IM) runs a parallel taxation and administration set-up in parts of Nagaland [Verification Required]. (5) "LWE: demand to establish their own government" means they seek to replace the constitutional order, not to secede.

Common confusion: insurgency is about a political objective and control of territory; terrorism is about a method. A single group can do both, which is why law treats acts and organisations separately.

5.2 Ethnic Conflicts

  • Conflicts between Nagas and non-Nagas (for example Naga-Kuki clashes in Manipur in the 1990s), Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities (large-scale violence in Manipur since May 2023), and Bodos and non-Bodos (clashes in Assam, for example in 2012) [Verification Required: dates and current status].
  • Drivers: competing land and homeland claims, identity fears, migration, reservation and autonomy demands, and overlapping claims on the same territory (for example Nagalim versus the territorial integrity of Manipur).
  • Note: describe conflicts by their causes and political demands; do not attribute violence to whole communities.

5.3 Organised Crime

BNS, 2023 (in force 1 July 2024), Section 111 defines organised crime as any continuing unlawful activity such as kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offences, cyber-crimes, trafficking of persons, drugs, weapons or illicit goods or services, or human trafficking, done by a person or group acting in concert, singly or jointly, as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on its behalf, using violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion or other unlawful means to get a direct or indirect benefit, including financial benefit [Verification Required: verify exact wording on India Code].

  • "Continuing unlawful activity" (as quoted by secondary sources): a cognizable offence punishable with three years or more, where more than one charge-sheet has been filed in the preceding ten years and the court has taken cognizance. This is a safeguard against misuse (a Rajasthan High Court bail ruling is reported to have stressed it) [Verification Required].
  • Organised crime syndicate: a group of two or more persons acting singly or jointly who engage in continuing unlawful activity.
Trafficking, smuggling, syndicate and cartel (class definitions, corrected)
TermClass definitionCorrected understanding
TraffickingIllegal trade in prohibited goods and services, for example drugs, humansIllegal trade in prohibited items or in people (human trafficking involves exploitation; it is not the same as migrant smuggling)

[Correction] The rule "syndicate = identity unknown; cartel = identity known" is not a recognised legal or academic distinction and could not be traced to a source; learn the BNS definition of "organised crime syndicate" and the economic meaning of "cartel". "Act in consult" is "in concert". Section 111 BNS is the first general central-law definition of organised crime; older definitions are in State laws such as the Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, 1999 [Verification Required].

Terror-crime nexus

  • Organised criminals can support terrorists with logistics, finance, arms, forged documents and safe routes; terrorists can fund themselves from crime (narco-terrorism, extortion, fake currency). Crime networks may also divert the attention of authorities.
  • In the North-East, the proximity to Myanmar and the Golden Triangle opium and synthetic-drug region, arms flows, extortion ("taxation") by armed groups, human trafficking and smuggling of wildlife and other goods create the nexus [Verification Required: current seizure data not added].
  • Response: border management, inter-agency intelligence, financial investigation (money-laundering and terror-financing controls), the NIA and State agencies, and cooperation with Myanmar, Bangladesh and Bhutan.

6. Reasons for Isolation and Alienation in the North-East

  1. Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation (BEFR), 1873: a colonial law that created the Inner Line, restricting entry into hill areas; the region became isolated from the plains economy and politics.
  2. Inner Line Permit (ILP): permit regime that continues the isolation. It applies in Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland and Mizoram, and Manipur since December 2019 [Verification Required]; it protects local identity but also restricts the movement and investment that could integrate the region.
  3. Geographical connectivity: the Siliguri Corridor ("Chicken's Neck") joins the North-East to the rest of India and is about 17-22 km wide at its narrowest (sources give 20-22 km most often; the rough notes say 22 km). The region has international borders on most sides, so it is poorly connected with the mainland. Isolation turned into alienation.
  4. Cultural differences: many ethnic groups that fear loss of identity, language and land.
  5. Perceived political apathy: the feeling of being neglected in national attention, representation and investment.
  6. External actors: safe havens, arms and training reported in neighbouring countries; the rough notes list the point without detail [Verification Required].

[Additional] Safeguards in the Constitution include the Sixth Schedule (autonomous councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram), Article 371A (Nagaland) and the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act as a contested tool. The 17 October note covers cross-border cooperation examples (Operation All Clear, 2003; Operation Sunrise, 2019).

7. Gaps and Way Forward (Short)

  • LWE: keep State police and intelligence strong before CAPF drawdown; deliver teachers, doctors and administrators, not only roads and towers; resolve FRA and PESA gaps; avoid any return to militia models.
  • North-East: complete and implement peace accords with development and inclusion; improve connectivity beyond the Siliguri Corridor; manage borders and the drug and arms trade together with neighbours; protect rights while using security laws.
  • Organised crime: use BNS Section 111 with its safeguards, financial investigation and inter-State and international cooperation.

Mains Points

  • Salwa Judum shows the limits of outsourcing security: local knowledge is valuable, but arming untrained civilians breached Articles 14 and 21, displaced communities and deepened the conflict. Better route: regular, trained, accountable local units plus services and rights.
  • Development is a security tool only if it is local and accountable: the IAP idea (Collector, SP and DFO deciding district projects) puts the district at the centre; the weakness is staffing, delivery and rights implementation.
  • "Naxal-free" is a milestone, not an end state: two districts still classified, 35 under watch, and risks of extortion networks, geographical shift and unresolved land grievances. "Urban Naxalism" must be tackled by evidence-based law, not by treating dissent as a threat.
  • North-East: insurgency, ethnic conflict and organised crime feed each other; isolation (BEFR, ILP, the Siliguri Corridor) and perceived neglect created alienation; accords work when followed by development and genuine autonomy, and fail when factions split or ethnic claims overlap.
  • Balanced way forward: peace accords with implementation tracking; trade, transport and Act East connectivity; border and financial controls against drug and arms networks; accountability for security forces and respect for fundamental rights.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Salwa Judum is the movement; SPO use was held unconstitutional. DRG and Bastariya Battalion are regular, legally constituted units, not Salwa Judum (although critics question the continuity).
  • IAP (2010) is not the same as SCA (2017); the IAP committee is Collector, SP, DFO.
  • Roshni (rural development) versus EMRS (tribal affairs) versus skill centres (skills ministry).
  • 31 March 2026 was the target date; the Lok Sabha statement came about 30 March; two districts were still "LWE affected".
  • Terrorism has a goal; militancy is not a legal term; not every North-East movement seeks sovereignty.
  • "Syndicate = unknown, cartel = known" is not a legal rule; BNS Section 111 needs more than one charge-sheet in ten years.
  • BEFR 1873 is the law; ILP is the permit it created; Manipur joined the ILP list only in 2019.
