2. What is a society?

2.1 Meaning and definitions

  • Word origin: "society" comes from the Latin socius - companion, associate. The word itself points to association among people.
  • Study of society = sociology. [Additional] Auguste Comte coined the word "sociology" (1838) from Latin socius and Greek logia (LibreTexts; Britannica).
  • Society as interaction: the sum total of interactions among individuals - verbal (speech, writing) and non-verbal (gestures, dress, distance, rituals).
  • Society as a web of social relationships - economic, cooperative, emotional, political and so on. This is the classic definition of R. M. MacIver and C. H. Page, Society: An Introductory Analysis (1949). [Additional] They also called society "cooperation crossed by conflict" (summary of MacIver and Page).

[Correction] The Latin root is socius (companion, associate), not "scious". Source: Lawrist - Society: meaning and definitions; LibreTexts.

[Correction] The notes say "study - socialist". The study of society is sociology, and the person who studies it is a sociologist. A socialist is a supporter of socialism, a political-economic ideology. Do not mix the two in answers.

2.2 Five elements of a society (class mnemonic "STUPA")

A society is a group of people who live in a territory, share a culture, are aware of shared norms, feel a sense of oneness, and continue over generations.

ElementMeaningIndian example / note
S - Shared cultureA common way of life: food, dress, language, festivals, values (see section 4).Festivals, cuisines and dress that vary by region but are shared within a community.
T - TerritoryA relatively permanent area the group occupies.Tribal communities have defined home areas. The Sentinelese live only on North Sentinel Island (Andaman Islands) - see Map 1.
U - Unity (psychic)A psychological sense of oneness ("we-feeling") arising from shared culture and awareness.See the term note in section 4.4.
P - PopulationThe number of people living in a given area who reproduce and replace themselves.The class used the biological wording ("individuals of a species in a region"). In sociology and demography, population means the people of an area, counted by the Census.
A - Awareness (mutual)Members know what behaviour is acceptable and what is not (norms).Learnt through socialisation.
[Additional] How sociologists put it: definitions stress relationships rather than people. MacIver and Page: "a web of social relationships"; F. H. Giddings: "consciousness of kind" (the sense of likeness that creates group identity); Talcott Parsons: the total complex of human relationships arising from action. The STUPA list is a useful checklist, but answers should open with a thinker's definition (summary).

2.3 Territory example: the Sentinelese of North Sentinel Island

  • The Sentinelese live in isolation, practising a hunting-and-gathering way of life. They are one of the Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
  • Legal protection: the whole island plus 5 km of coastal sea from the high-water mark is notified as a tribal reserve. The laws include the A&N Islands (Protection of Aboriginal Tribes) Regulation, 1956, the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, restrictions under the Foreigners (Restricted Area) Orders, 1963, the Indian Forest Act, 1927 and the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972.
  • Policy: the government follows an "eyes-on and hands-off" approach: surveillance by Coast Guard and Marine Police, but no attempt at contact.
  • Population: 50, as estimated in the 2011 Census (MHA reply in the Lok Sabha, 5 February 2019). The same reply put the Jarawa at 514. These are estimates; no enumeration is possible on the island.

Source: PIB, MHA - Sentinelese Tribe (5 February 2019). The five A&N PVTGs (Great Andamanese, Onge, Jarawa, Sentinelese, Shompen) are listed in this summary of A&N Administration material.

Exam-ready definition (from the class, polished): Society comes from the Latin socius, meaning companion. It is the sum total of interactions among individuals and, in MacIver and Page's words, "a web of social relationships". Put simply, a society is a group of people living in a particular territory who share a culture, are mutually aware of its norms, feel a psychic unity, and continue as a population over generations.

3. Socialisation

Socialisation is the process by which a person learns what behaviour is acceptable and unacceptable in a society, and so becomes a member of it. NCERT describes it as the process by which a helpless infant gradually becomes a self-aware, knowledgeable person, skilled in the ways of their culture.

BasisPrimary socialisationSecondary socialisation
WhenInfancy and early childhoodFrom later childhood through the whole of life
Main agentsFamily (parents, siblings, kin)School, peer group, mass and social media, religious bodies, workplace, training institutions, the state
What is learntLanguage, basic norms, emotional bonds, gender rolesSpecialised roles, impersonal rules, professional ethics, citizenship
NatureIntimate, emotional, informalMore formal and impersonal

[Correction] The notes place primary socialisation "after birth till maturity" and list schools and peer groups under it. In the standard (NCERT) usage, primary socialisation is in the family during infancy and early childhood. School and peer groups are agents of secondary socialisation, which is lifelong. Training institutions, rightly placed under secondary in the notes, are one example. Sources: NCERT Class XI, ch. 4 summary (NCERT Help); ClearStudy - NCERT ch. 4.

[Additional] Mains use: socialisation explains how gender roles, caste norms and values are passed on (and how they can change). Use it in questions on the family, gender, digital childhood and values. See the 2023 PYQ on mobile phones replacing "child cuddling" (PYQ 6).

4. Culture and values

4.1 What is culture?

  • Culture = the way of life (lifestyle) of a people. It is learnt (not inherited biologically), shared, and dynamic.
  • [Additional] Classic definitions:
    • E. B. Tylor, Primitive Culture (1871): culture is "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society".
    • Clyde Kluckhohn, Mirror for Man (1949): culture is "the total life way of a people, the social legacy the individual acquires from his group".
  • Elements of culture (class list): food habits and behaviour, dress, language, festivals (the class described them as a way to break the monotony of life), music, dance, and the value system.
  • Culture evolves with the environment. Climate shapes food, dress and housing. [Additional] NCERT: different natural and social settings produce different cultures. No culture is superior; each is judged by how well it copes with its setting.
  • [Additional] NCERT's three dimensions of culture: cognitive (ideas, symbols, knowledge), normative (folkways, mores, customs, laws) and material (tools, technology, objects). Related terms: ethnocentrism (judging other cultures by one's own), cosmopolitanism (valuing difference), and culture lag (norms lagging behind technology).

[Correction] The notes attribute "way of life" to "Al. Koreber". The anthropologist is A. L. Kroeber, who with Clyde Kluckhohn compiled a well-known review of definitions of culture (1952) †. The wording "total (life) way of a people" is Kluckhohn's, in Mirror for Man (1949). Cite Kluckhohn (or Tylor) for the definition. Sources: extract from Mirror for Man; Britannica - Mirror for Man; Tylor's definition, 1871.

4.2 Values

Values are shared ideas about what is good, right and desirable. Norms are the specific rules of behaviour built on values, backed by sanctions (praise, ridicule, fines).

4.3 Collectivism (India) vs individualism (USA)

  • On the basis of values and culture, the class contrasted India - collectivism (group, family and community first) with the USA - individualism (self-interest, personal autonomy).
  • [Additional] Evidence: Geert Hofstede's cultural-dimensions model scores the USA at 91 on individualism (the highest of its countries) and India at 48, close to the middle of the scale (Figure 4).

[Correction] "India = collectivism, USA = individualism" is a useful contrast but too sharp. On Hofstede's scale India scores 48, a mix of collectivist and individualist traits; China (20) and Pakistan (14) score far more collectivist. National averages also hide large regional, urban-rural, class and generational differences. Write "India is relatively collectivist" and cite the source.

4.4 Psychic unity and the individual-society chain

  • Psychic unity (as used in class) = a psychological sense of oneness among members, derived from shared culture and mutual awareness.
  • The individual and the family are the basic units of society. The chain runs:
Individual → Family / group → Group with STUPA traits → Society

[Correction] (terminology) In sociology, a group's sense of oneness is usually called "we-feeling" or, after F. H. Giddings, "consciousness of kind". The phrase "psychic unity" normally means something else: the anthropological idea of the "psychic unity of mankind" (Adolf Bastian †) - that all humans share the same basic mental capacities. Use "we-feeling" in answers to avoid confusion. Source for Giddings: BNS Institute summary.

6. Hierarchy

Hierarchy is the placement of things or people one above the other in a rank order. It has two broad types.

BasisTraditional hierarchyModern hierarchy
Basis of positionBirth (ascribed status)Merit or talent (achieved status)
NatureFixed and permanentFlexible; positions change
MobilityLittle or none (closed system)High (open system)
ExamplesMonarchy, patriarchy, casteBureaucracy, corporate and military ranks
LegitimacyTradition, religion, customRules, qualifications, law
[Additional] Concepts to use:
  • Ascribed vs achieved status - Ralph Linton, The Study of Man (1936). Ascribed status is assigned "without reference to ... innate differences or abilities"; achieved status is open to individual effort. Indian example: caste (ascribed) vs IAS rank (achieved). Mains use: to show how modernisation shifts the basis of status (Foladare on Linton's definitions).
  • Bureaucracy as the model of modern, rule-based hierarchy - Max Weber (legal-rational authority) †.
  • Purity and pollution - Louis Dumont, Homo Hierarchicus (1966): hierarchy in caste rests on the opposition of the pure and the impure, not on wealth or power (summary).

7. The varna order: from the Purusha Sukta to birth-based hierarchy

7.1 Traditional hierarchical social order in the Rig Veda

The four-fold order appears in the Purusha Sukta, hymn 90 of the tenth book (Mandala 10) of the Rig Veda (Rig Veda 10.90.12). It describes society as born from the body of the cosmic being, Purusha. Collectively the four groups are called the varna system.

VarnaBody part in the hymnWork assigned (later texts and class notes)
BrahmanaMouth (mukha)Livelihood through learning: priests, teachers, authors, advisers, performers of rituals
Rajanya / KshatriyaArms (bahu)Kings, soldiers, military commanders, wrestlers; protection and justice
VaishyaThighs (uru)Farmers, cattle-rearers, traders, merchants
ShudraFeet (pad), "supporting the body"Service to the other three; in practice agricultural labour and crafts

[Correction] Two fixes. (1) The varna is Brahmana (Brahmin), not "Brahma" (Brahma is a deity). (2) In the hymn the Brahmana is the Purusha's mouth (mukham), not the head. The second group is named Rajanya, not "Kshatriya", which is the term later texts use. Griffith: "The Brahman was his mouth, of both his arms was the Rajanya made. His thighs became the Vaisya, from his feet the Sudra was produced." Sources: Rig Veda 10.90.12; Wilson/Sayana.

[Correction] (date) "Rig Veda ~1500 BCE" is fine for the text as a whole (Britannica: c. 1500-1200 BCE). However, the Purusha Sukta is in Mandala 10, which scholars treat as the youngest layer of the Rig Veda (late Rigvedic). The fourfold order is therefore not part of the oldest hymns. Sources: Britannica - varna; NCERT-based note on varna.

[Correction] (who was "outside" the varnas) The notes list barbers, washermen, shoemakers, sanitation workers and cremation workers under Shudra. These are jati-level occupations of later periods, not part of the hymn. Communities doing sanitation, cremation or leather work were often treated as "untouchable" and placed outside the four-fold order (avarna) by later Brahmanical texts and practice. Putting them inside the Shudra varna mixes up two layers of hierarchy. Article 17 abolished untouchability in 1950. Use the constitutional term Scheduled Castes when describing these communities today.

7.2 Based on work or based on birth?

  • Until the (early) Vedic period: closer to choice. Membership seems to have rested mainly on function and skill. A Rig Veda verse (9.112.3) says: "I am a poet; my father is a physician, my mother a grinder of corn" - three occupations in one family.
  • Class examples of mobility: Valmiki, Veda Vyasa, Karna, Parashurama.
  • Post-Vedic: compulsion. By about 600 BCE varna had become fixed by birth. Rules then spread to language, food, dress and gender roles (Figure 6).

[Correction] (nature of the evidence) Valmiki, Vyasa, Karna and Parashurama are figures of epic and Puranic tradition. Their stories illustrate an idea; they are not historical evidence of mobility. Two need care: Parashurama was a Brahmana who took up arms, so he changed his role, not his varna; Karna, raised by a charioteer, was repeatedly denied status because of his birth, which makes his story evidence of birth-based rank. The standard textual example of changing varna is Vishvamitra (Kshatriya king to Brahmarshi) †. The epic story of Ekalavya shows the opposite lesson: skill did not overcome birth (NCERT-based note).

[Correction] (chronology) Hardening was gradual, not a single date. Varna began turning hereditary in the Later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE). The Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras (from about the 6th-5th century BCE onwards; the Manusmriti is the best known) then codified it. "~600 BCE" is best read as "by the end of the Later Vedic period". Britannica notes that older texts stress the function of each class more than hereditary membership. Sources: Britannica - varna; NCERT-based note on Dharmashastras.

[Additional] Competing readings of the Purusha Sukta (attribute; do not endorse):
  • Functional / organic reading: the hymn describes interdependent parts of one body; it neither ranks the groups nor uses the word varna.
  • Hierarchical reading: later law-books (Dharmashastras, Manusmriti) and centuries of practice read it as a divinely sanctioned ladder from head to foot.
  • Interpolation argument: B. R. Ambedkar (Who Were the Shudras?, 1946 †) and historians such as Suvira Jaiswal argued the social verses were a later insertion to justify a hardening order.
  • Philologists Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton (2014) see the varna system in the Rig Veda only "in embryo", more a social ideal than a social reality.

Source for the readings: Sanatans.in - two readings of Rigveda 10.90 (cites Jamison and Brereton, Jaiswal, Ambedkar). This page argues one side; the attributions it reports are what is used here.

7.4 Timeline: from the Purusha Sukta to Census 2027

  • c. 1500-1000 BCE Early Vedic (Rigvedic) society: groups broadly functional; family members in different occupations.
  • Late Rigvedic Purusha Sukta (RV 10.90) names four groups.
  • c. 1000-600 BCE Later Vedic period: varna becomes hereditary; Shudras excluded from Vedic rites.
  • 6th-5th c. BCE on Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras codify varna duties; Buddhism and Jainism question birth-based status ("a Brahmana is such by his deeds" - Sutta Nipata).
  • 1950 Article 17 abolishes untouchability; Arts. 14-16 guarantee equality.
  • 1955 Untouchability (Offences) Act, renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act (1976 †).
  • 1989 SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act.

8.2 Age-based hierarchy

  • Elders get a higher position than the young.
  • Gerontocracy = rule by elders. In the traditional joint family the eldest male (the karta in the Hindu joint family †) heads the household, so age and gender hierarchies combine.
  • [Additional] Change: education, migration and earning power of the young are weakening age-based authority. This links to the GS I topic of ageing (to be covered under population).

9. Model paragraph: hierarchy as a salient feature (from the class, polished)

India is one of the oldest continuous civilisations. Over millennia it built up customs, practices and traditions that became the salient features of its society. Hierarchy based on birth is among the most distinctive. It took shape in the varna system, which grades society into four orders - Brahmana, Kshatriya, Vaishya and Shudra. The scheme is first stated in the Purusha Sukta of the Rig Veda (10.90) and was later codified as hereditary in the Dharmashastras. Groups treated as "untouchable" were pushed outside it altogether. Beyond varna, hierarchy runs through everyday life: rules on food (commensality), language (forms of address), age (gerontocracy), gender (patriarchy), dress and the overall social order. Louis Dumont called this the principle of purity and pollution. The Constitution reverses birth-based rank through Articles 14-17. Yet caste endogamy and identity persist, so hierarchy today is changing in form rather than disappearing.

Study guidance, not an official answer. For data, add Census 2011 SC/ST shares (Figure 5) and NCRB figures on crimes against SCs/STs (to be added when the caste class is taken up).

  • Kinship Organisation in India (1953) - Irawati Karve (four kinship zones).
  • Purusha Sukta = Rig Veda 10.90 (Mandala 10, the youngest layer). Mouth - Brahmana; arms - Rajanya; thighs - Vaishya; feet - Shudra.
  • "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" - Simone de Beauvoir, The Second Sex (1949).
  • Gender socialisation processes: manipulation, canalisation, verbal appellation, activity exposure (Ann Oakley, after Ruth Hartley).
  • "Little republics" - Charles Metcalfe (1830). "Sink of localism, a den of ignorance ..." - Ambedkar, CAD, 4 November 1948.
  • Article 17 abolishes untouchability; PCR Act 1955; SC/ST (PoA) Act 1989.
  • AnSI People of India project: 4,635 communities, 43 state volumes. 75 PVTGs; 705 ST groups (Census 2011).
  • Sentinelese: North Sentinel Island + 5 km sea = tribal reserve; A&N (Protection of Aboriginal Tribes) Regulation, 1956; "eyes-on, hands-off"; 50 (2011 estimate).
  • Census 2027: reference date 1 March 2027; caste enumeration in Phase II (February 2027).

Mains Points

DimensionPoints with examples and data
SocialHierarchy by birth persists through endogamy, kinship and family; socialisation reproduces caste and gender norms. Joint family and gerontocracy are weakening with migration and education.
EconomicTraditional link of caste and occupation is loosening (modern jobs are achieved statuses), but SC/ST disadvantage remains visible in Census 2011 housing and amenity data.
PoliticalAmbedkar chose the individual, not the village, as the unit of the Constitution. Caste re-enters politics as identity; Census 2027 caste enumeration revives the debate.
CulturalDiversity (4,635 communities) with shared civilisational threads; syncretism; relatively collectivist values (Hofstede 48) changing with urbanisation.
LegalArts. 14-17, 25-30, 40, 342; Fifth and Sixth Schedules; PCR Act 1955; PoA Act 1989; A&N PAT Regulation 1956 (Sentinelese).
Way forwardEducation and gender-sensitive socialisation; strict enforcement of anti-untouchability laws; respect for tribal autonomy (the Sentinelese "eyes-on, hands-off" model); evidence-based policy using Census 2027 data.

Common Traps / Confusions

  • Sociologist vs socialist - student of society vs supporter of socialism (c02).
  • Primary vs secondary socialisation - school and peers are secondary (c03).
  • Kroeber vs Kluckhohn - "total life way of a people" is Kluckhohn (1949) (c04).
  • "Psychic unity" - group oneness is "we-feeling"; "psychic unity of mankind" is a different idea (c06).
  • Brahma vs Brahmana; head vs mouth; Rajanya vs Kshatriya in RV 10.90.12 (c07).
  • Purusha Sukta is in Mandala 10, not the oldest books (c08).
  • Shudra vs avarna - groups treated as untouchable were placed outside the four varnas (c09).
  • Varna vs jati - four textual orders vs thousands of real endogamous groups (7.3).
  • Sex vs gender; Beauvoir's exact words (c12).
  • Collectivist "India" vs data - Hofstede puts India mid-scale (48) (c05).
  • Census 2011 ST figure - 104.3 million (PIB) vs 10.42 crore (ORGI report): cite the source you use.

30-Second Revision

Socius (companion) → Society = web of relationships (MacIver and Page) → STUPA → Socialisation (family / school-peers-media) → Culture (Tylor; Kluckhohn) and values → Salient features (diversity ... hierarchy) → Hierarchy: ascribed vs achieved (Linton); purity-pollution (Dumont) → Varna: RV 10.90 (mouth-arms-thighs-feet) → hereditary by c. 600 BCE → Article 17 (1950) → Gender: Beauvoir; Oakley's four processes

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Questions are copied from compilations of official UPSC papers (links given). Suggested approaches are [Additional] study guidance, not an official marking scheme.

1. UPSC CSE Prelims 2019 (GS Paper I) - Consider the following statements about Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) in India:

  1. PVTGs reside in 18 States and one Union Territory.
  2. A stagnant or declining population is one of the criteria for determining PVTG status.
  3. There are 95 PVTGs officially notified in the country so far.
  4. Irular and Konda Reddi tribes are included in the list of PVTGs.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  1. 1, 2 and 3
  2. 2, 3 and 4
  3. 1, 2 and 4
  4. 1, 3 and 4

Answer: (c) 1, 2 and 4. There are 75 PVTGs, not 95. The Sentinelese of section 2.3 are one of the five in the A&N Islands, the only UT with PVTGs. Source: UnlockIAS - Prelims 2019 Q51; PMF IAS.

2. UPSC CSE Mains 2019, GS Paper I (10 marks) - What makes the Indian society unique in sustaining its culture? Discuss.

Suggested approach [Additional]: Intro: define culture (Tylor or Kluckhohn) and note India's civilisational continuity. Body: the family and primary socialisation; kinship and marriage norms (Karve); syncretism and tolerance; festivals, oral traditions and the village; diversity held together by a "we-feeling"; the constitutional protection of culture (Arts. 29-30). Add adaptability: tradition coexisting with modernity. Conclusion: continuity through change. Links: section 4, section 5. Source: First Principles IAS - GS I PYQs on salient features.

3. UPSC CSE Mains 2021, GS Paper I (15 marks) - How does Indian society maintain continuity in traditional social values? Enumerate the changes taking place in it.

Suggested approach [Additional]: Continuity: family and socialisation, rituals and festivals, religion, joint family obligations, caste endogamy, respect for elders (gerontocracy). Changes: nuclear families, individualism with urbanisation (Hofstede: India is mid-scale), gender roles, inter-caste marriage, the decline of age-based authority, and digital media as a new agent of socialisation. Conclude with "selective adaptation". Links: 4.2, 4.3, 8.2. Source: First Principles IAS.

4. UPSC CSE Mains 2020, GS Paper I (10 marks) - Has caste lost its relevance in understanding the multi-cultural Indian Society? Elaborate your answer with illustrations.

Suggested approach [Additional]: Intro: varna vs jati; hierarchy as a salient feature. Weakening: Article 17, urban anonymity, decline of commensality rules, occupational mobility (achieved status). Persisting: endogamy, politics, discrimination, Census 2027 caste enumeration. Conclude that caste is changing form, not disappearing. Links: section 7, section 9. Source: SuperKalam - Mains 2020 GS I; EaseMyPrep.

5. UPSC CSE Mains 2018, GS Paper I (10 marks) - "Caste system is assuming new identities and associational forms. Hence, caste system cannot be eradicated in India." Comment.

Suggested approach [Additional]: Show how caste has moved from ritual hierarchy (Dumont) to identity groups, associations and political interest groups. Weigh this against legal change (Arts. 15-17, PCR 1955, PoA 1989), education and urbanisation. Conclude whether "cannot be eradicated" is overstated. Links: section 6, 7.4. Source: First Principles IAS.

6. UPSC CSE Mains 2023, GS Paper I (15 marks) - Why is caste identity in India both fluid and static?

Suggested approach [Additional]: Static: ascription by birth, endogamy, purity-pollution, Census categories. Fluid: early Vedic functional groups, Sanskritisation and other mobility (to come), changing jati ranks, occupational change, new associations. Use the work-vs-birth debate (7.2) and Linton's terms. Source: First Principles IAS.

7. UPSC CSE Mains 2023, GS Paper I (10 marks) - Child cuddling is now being replaced by mobile phones. Discuss its impact on the socialization of children.

Suggested approach [Additional]: Define primary socialisation (family, early years). Effects of screens replacing parental contact: weaker emotional bonding, language delays, reduced social skills, exposure to unfiltered content (media as an early secondary agent). Note positives: learning apps. Way forward: parental time, screen-time norms, digital literacy. Link: section 3. Source: First Principles IAS.

Corrections Made

  1. c01 - Latin root is socius, not "scious".
  2. c02 - Study of society is sociology; its practitioner a sociologist, not a "socialist".
  3. c03 - School and peer groups are agents of secondary socialisation; primary socialisation is family-based and early.
  4. c04 - "Way of life" definition: Kluckhohn (1949); the name is A. L. Kroeber.
  5. c05 - India is mid-scale (Hofstede 48), not purely collectivist.
  6. c06 - Group oneness = "we-feeling" / consciousness of kind; "psychic unity" means something else.
  7. c07 - Brahmana (not Brahma) from the mouth (not head); second group named Rajanya.
  8. c08 - Purusha Sukta is in Mandala 10, the latest layer of the Rig Veda.
  9. c09 - Groups treated as untouchable were placed outside the varnas (avarna), not within Shudra.
  10. c10 - Valmiki, Vyasa, Karna, Parashurama are epic/Puranic figures; Parashurama and Karna do not show varna change.
  11. c11 - Varna hardened gradually (Later Vedic c. 1000-600 BCE), then was codified in the Dharmashastras.
  12. c12 - Beauvoir's quotation: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" (1949).
  13. c13 - Standard names of the four gender-socialisation processes; Oakley credits Ruth Hartley.
